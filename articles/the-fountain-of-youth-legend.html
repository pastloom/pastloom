The Fountain of Youth Legend

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Fountain of Youth is a magical spring from old stories: drink or bathe in its water, and you become young again. Spanish explorers were said to have searched for it in Florida 500 years ago — above all, Juan Ponce de León.

The honest history is more interesting than the tale: there is no evidence that Ponce de León ever hunted for such a fountain. The link between him and the legend was written after his death. The fountain itself is a story that humans have told, in one form or another, for thousands of years.

Old tales of young-again waters

Long before Florida, storytellers dreamed of healing waters. Herodotus wrote of a spring among the long-lived Ethiopians; tales of Alexander the Great described a Water of Life; and a famous medieval letter from the priest-king Prester John boasted of a fountain in his kingdom that kept bathers young.

When Europeans reached the Americas, they carried these stories with them — and Native peoples' tales of healing springs in the Bahamas, which the Spanish called Bimini, seemed to fit the old dream perfectly.

Ponce de León and the story that stuck

Ponce de León, the conqueror of Puerto Rico, sailed in 1513 and landed on a flower-covered coast he named Florida. His royal contract speaks of discovering and settling new lands — it says nothing about a fountain. On a second voyage in 1521 he was wounded by a Calusa arrow and died in Cuba.

The fountain entered his story only afterwards. In 1535 the chronicler Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo — who disliked Ponce — wrote that the ageing governor had hunted a youth-restoring spring in Bimini. Later historians repeated it as fact, and one of exploration's most durable myths was born, possibly from a joke at a dead man's expense.

The fountain today

St. Augustine in Florida still welcomes visitors to its Fountain of Youth archaeological park, where guests drink from a spring on the site of an old Native settlement — the water is ordinary, the pleasure is in the story.

Meanwhile the dream itself has moved into laboratories and advertisements: creams, diets and longevity science all promise what the fountain promised. The legend of Ponce's search endures because the wish behind it is universal.

Did You Know?

The writer who first linked Ponce de León to the fountain seems to have meant it as mockery of an old man chasing youth — historians suspect the world took a sarcastic jibe and turned it into a five-hundred-year legend.
