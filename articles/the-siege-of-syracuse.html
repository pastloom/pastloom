The Siege of Syracuse

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Syracuse, the great Greek city of Sicily, was besieged by Rome from 214 to 212 BCE, during the Second Punic War. Defending it was the most brilliant engineer of the ancient world: Archimedes.

His war machines held the Roman army and fleet at bay for two years. When the city finally fell, Archimedes was killed by a Roman soldier — one of history's most famous and saddest losses.

Why Rome attacked Syracuse

Syracuse had been Rome's ally for decades, but after its old king Hiero II died, his young grandson took the city over to Carthage's side in the great war between Rome and Carthage.

Rome could not allow Sicily's strongest city to become a Carthaginian base. The general Marcellus attacked by land and sea — and ran into defences prepared by the elderly Archimedes, the city's famous mathematician.

The machines of Archimedes

Archimedes filled the walls with clever engines: catapults of every size, which struck Roman ships at every distance, and cranes that dropped huge stones — or swung down an iron claw that grabbed a ship's bow, lifted it, and smashed it back into the sea.

Later writers even claimed he burned Roman ships with mirrors focusing the sun. That story is probably a legend, but the real machines were frightening enough: Marcellus joked that Archimedes was using his ships as cups to ladle water from the sea.

The fall of the city

For two years the siege failed. At last, during a festival when the defenders relaxed, Roman soldiers scaled an unwatched tower and opened the gates. Syracuse was captured and looted in 212 BCE.

In the chaos, a Roman soldier found Archimedes drawing diagrams in the dust. The old man told him not to disturb his circles — and the soldier, not knowing or not caring who he was, killed him. Marcellus, who had ordered Archimedes spared, was deeply sorry. Rome had won Syracuse, but lost the greatest mind of the age.

Did You Know?

Syracuse had faced a famous siege before: in 415–413 BCE Athens tried to conquer it during the Peloponnesian War, and lost an entire army and fleet in the attempt — one of the turning points of Greek history.
