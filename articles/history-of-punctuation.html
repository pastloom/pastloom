History of Punctuation

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Punctuation — the dots, commas and question marks in writing — feels as if it has always existed. In fact, the earliest books had none of it at all: just long streams of letters with no spaces and no stops.

Readers and printers slowly invented the marks we know over 2,000 years. Each mark began as a practical tool to help a reader breathe, pause and understand.

Writing without stops

A page of ancient Greek or Latin had no spaces, no capital letters and no punctuation. Reading was slow work done aloud, separating words and sense as you went.

Around 200 BCE, the librarian Aristophanes of Byzantium is said to have introduced dots placed high, middle or low to show short, medium and long pauses — the seed of the comma, colon and full stop.

Monks and marks

Medieval scribes copying books by hand added their own helps for readers: slashes, dots and early question marks, often decorated in red ink.

Word spacing, capital letters and paragraph marks arrived in the same centuries. Together they turned reading from a performance into a silent, private act.

Print fixes the rules

Printing froze the marks into standard shapes. In 1494 an Italian book printed the first semicolon, a mark meant to pause longer than a comma but less than a full stop.

Since then, new marks keep being proposed — an irony mark, an interrobang mixing question and exclamation — but the classic set from the printing age still does nearly all the work.

Did You Know?

The exclamation mark is said to have begun as the Latin word io — meaning 'joy!' — written with the i above the o by medieval scribes, until the two letters melted into one mark.
