History of Carousels

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A carousel — also called a merry-go-round — is a rotating platform ride with carved horses and other animals that move up and down as music plays. It is one of the oldest fairground rides, and its name comes from a training game of knights and horsemen.

Steam power in the 1800s turned the carousel into the shining centrepiece of fairs, and hand-carved horses from its golden age are now museum treasures.

A game for knights

The carousel began as serious training. Centuries ago, Arab and Byzantine horsemen played a game tossing clay balls to each other at full gallop — missing meant being splashed with scented water. Crusaders carried the game to Europe, where it became the 'little battle' — carosello.

By the 1600s, French knights practised on a rotating device with wooden horses, spearing rings while the platform turned. Spectators loved watching — and smaller versions were soon built for children to ride for fun.

Steam, music and galloping horses

In 1861, Thomas Bradshaw presented a steam-driven roundabout at an English fair — the carousel had an engine. Frederick Savage of King's Lynn improved the machinery, adding the crank mechanism that makes horses gallop up and down.

Carousels spread to fairs everywhere, growing organs, mirrors, painted scenery and electric lights. In America, immigrant carvers created magnificent horses with jewels, flowing manes and fierce expressions.

The golden age and its treasures

Between the 1880s and 1920s, thousands of carousels turned in American parks and fairs. Companies like Dentzel and the Philadelphia Toboggan Company built rides that were moving works of folk art.

The Great Depression ended the golden age, and many carousels were broken up. Today the survivors are protected and restored, and new carousels still turn in parks worldwide — carrying riders in the same circle as the knights' game of 900 years ago.

Did You Know?

On a traditional carousel, only the horses on the outside row are fully carved in detail on both sides on some rides — the inner 'romance side' of American horses, facing the crowd, was carved most beautifully, while the side facing the centre was often left simpler.
