The Partition of India, 1947: The Line That Divided a Land

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In August 1947, British India became free — and was divided in two. A new border split the provinces of Punjab and Bengal, creating India and Pakistan in one of the largest and saddest migrations in human history.

Perhaps 15 million people left their homes to cross the new line, and up to a million or more died in violence along the way. This is the human story of how a line on a map divided one land into two nations.

Five weeks to draw a border

Britain, exhausted by war, decided to leave India quickly. The last viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, brought the date forward to August 1947, and the new nations agreed to divide Punjab and Bengal by religion — Muslim-majority areas to Pakistan, the rest to India.

The man chosen to draw the line, Cyril Radcliffe, had never been to India. Given about five weeks, old maps and census figures, he drew borders through villages, farms, rivers and even houses. The award was published only after independence day — many people did not know which country they were in.

The great migration

As the line became known, fear and violence exploded, above all in Punjab. Hindus and Sikhs fled west-to-east; Muslims fled east-to-west. Whole trains arrived carrying only the dead, and refugee columns on foot stretched for miles.

Cities like Lahore, Amritsar, Delhi and Karachi changed almost overnight. People left behind homes, land, photographs and graves — often with nothing but the clothes they wore. It was the largest mass migration of the 20th century.

A wound and a new beginning

Mahatma Gandhi, heartbroken by the division he had opposed, spent independence day fasting in Calcutta, working to stop the killing — and for a time, his presence calmed the city.

Two new nations were born from the pain: India and Pakistan (which then included today's Bangladesh). Families divided in 1947 still tell the stories, and the unsettled border in Kashmir has troubled both countries ever since.

Did You Know?

Some villages learned which country they belonged to only when officials arrived to raise a flag — Radcliffe's border award was kept secret until two days after independence.
