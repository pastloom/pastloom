Fireworks and Celebrations Through History

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Wherever people celebrate, fireworks are never far away. For more than a thousand years, bursts of light and noise have marked new years, weddings, victories and the biggest nights of the calendar.

This is the story of fireworks not as an invention, but as a celebration — the festivals, crowds and traditions that made the night sky bloom with colour.

Noise and light to drive away evil

In China, burning bamboo crackled and popped in the fire — and once gunpowder was packed inside, firecrackers became part of New Year. People believed the bangs frightened away evil spirits, including the legendary beast Nian.

Fireworks became the sound and colour of good luck: red paper, gold sparks and deafening volleys to greet weddings, births and festivals. The custom travelled with Chinese communities around the world.

Festivals of fire around the world

In India, Diwali fills the night with lamps, candles and fireworks, celebrating light winning over darkness. In Britain, bonfires and fireworks on 5 November remember the night in 1605 when Guy Fawkes was caught guarding barrels of gunpowder beneath Parliament.

America adopted fireworks for Independence Day from its very first celebration in 1777 — John Adams had hoped the day would be marked with 'illuminations' forever. France lights the sky on Bastille Day, and cities race to stage the grandest New Year display on Earth.

Celebrations today

Modern displays are planned by computer, with shells timed to music in 'pyromusicals' watched by millions. A single great show can use tens of thousands of fireworks.

Yet the heart of it has not changed since ancient China: a crowd in the dark, faces turned up, sharing a moment of wonder. Fireworks are the world's oldest way of saying — tonight is special.

Did You Know?

American president John Adams wrote in 1776 that Independence Day should be celebrated with 'bonfires and illuminations' — and fireworks have lit the Fourth of July ever since.
