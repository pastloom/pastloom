History of Factories

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A factory is a building where many workers and machines make the same thing, again and again, all day long. Before factories, most goods were made slowly by hand, at home or in small workshops.

The factory changed everything: how goods were made, where people lived, when they worked — even how time itself was measured, by the factory clock and whistle.

From workshop to mill

Venice's Arsenal showed the future early: thousands of workers, each doing one task, could fit out a warship in a day. But most goods were still made by families at home, spinning and weaving between farm jobs.

In 1771 Richard Arkwright opened his water-powered cotton mill at Cromford in England. Workers now came to the machines, worked fixed hours to a bell, and were paid wages — the factory system had begun.

Steam, smoke and factory towns

The steam engine freed factories from riversides. Mills and works clustered in coal-rich towns like Manchester, which boomed into smoky 'factory towns' where the mill whistle ruled the day.

Conditions were harsh: long hours, deafening machines, child workers and dangerous dust. Yet factories made cloth, tools and goods cheaper than ever before — and their workers began organising for better lives.

The moving line and the robot

In 1913 Henry Ford set his car factory's work on a moving line: the car came to the worker, not the worker to the car. Production soared, prices fell, and the Model T put the world on wheels.

After the Second World War, factories spread worldwide, then machines began replacing hands: today robot arms weld and paint in near-silent plants. The factory Arkwright built with water and bells now runs on computers.

Did You Know?

Richard Arkwright's Cromford Mill of 1771 is often called the first true factory. Its water-powered machines spun cotton day and night — and its workers were among the first in history to live by the clock.
