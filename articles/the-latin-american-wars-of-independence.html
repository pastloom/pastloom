The Latin American Wars of Independence

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Between about 1810 and 1825, the Spanish colonies of Latin America fought their way to freedom, creating a string of new nations from Mexico to Argentina. It was one of the largest independence movements in world history.

Two generals became its legends: Simón Bolívar, who freed the north of South America, and José de San Martín, who crossed the Andes mountains to free the south.

Why the colonies rose

Creoles — people of Spanish descent born in the Americas — resented being ruled from Spain and shut out of top jobs. They read Enlightenment ideas and watched the United States and Haiti win independence, and France's revolution spread bold thoughts about rights.

When Napoleon invaded Spain in 1808 and seized its king, the colonies suddenly had no clear ruler. Local juntas took power 'for the king' — and soon began ruling for themselves.

Bolívar and San Martín

Bolívar, a wealthy Venezuelan, lost battles, fled into exile and returned again and again. His crossing of the frozen Andes in 1819 to surprise the Spanish in Colombia is ranked among the great feats of military history. He went on to free Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia — the country named after him.

In the south, Argentina's San Martín built an army in the Andes foothills and in 1817 led 5,000 men over some of the world's highest passes into Chile, freeing Chile and then sailing to free Peru.

New nations, hard beginnings

By 1825, Spain kept only Cuba and Puerto Rico in the Americas. Brazil had also separated peacefully from Portugal in 1822 under a Portuguese prince.

Independence did not bring easy unity. Bolívar's dream of one great republic broke into separate states, and the new nations faced civil wars and dictators. But the map of Latin America drawn in these years is, in large part, the map we still see today.

Did You Know?

In 1822 Bolívar and San Martín met in the city of Guayaquil — the two liberators of a continent talking face to face. Nobody knows exactly what they said: the meeting is still called 'the Mystery of Guayaquil'.
