History of Papua New Guinea

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Papua New Guinea, the eastern half of the great island of New Guinea, is one of the most diverse countries on Earth — with more than 800 living languages, the most of any nation.

People have lived there for at least 40,000 years, and its highland farmers were among the first in the world. Germany and Britain divided the island in 1884, Australia ruled for decades, and independence finally came in 1975.

One island, hundreds of nations

Humans reached New Guinea when it was still joined to Australia in the ancient land of Sahul. In the highland valleys, people drained swamps at Kuk and grew taro and bananas about 10,000 years ago — inventing farming on their own, as early as anywhere on Earth.

Mountains and jungle kept communities apart, so hundreds of languages and cultures grew side by side. Coastal peoples traded shells, pots and obsidian by canoe across wide seas.

Germany, Britain and Australia

In 1884 Germany claimed the north-east and Britain the south-east; the Dutch held the west. After the First World War, Australia took over the German part, and ruled both territories — Papua and New Guinea.

The Second World War brought fierce fighting to the island: Japanese forces tried to cross the Owen Stanley mountains along the Kokoda Track in 1942, and were stopped by Australian and Papuan soldiers — remembered in Australia as one of the war's great stands.

Independence and today

Papua New Guinea became independent on 16 September 1975, with Michael Somare as its first prime minister. Uniting hundreds of peoples into one democracy was, and remains, a huge task.

A civil war on Bougainville island (1988–1998) over a copper mine ended with a peace agreement and, in 2019, a vote for independence whose final result is still being negotiated. Through it all, village life, custom (kastom) and clan remain the strong heart of the nation.

Did You Know?

With over 800 languages, Papua New Guinea has more languages than any other country — about 12 percent of all the languages spoken on Earth.
