History of Zero and Numbers

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Zero seems simple — it is the number for nothing. Yet for most of history, nobody had it. The Romans built an empire without a zero, and Greek mathematicians did geometry without one. Numbers with a true zero were developed in India about 1,500 years ago.

This article follows counting itself: from tally marks on bones, through Babylonian and Maya number systems, to the Indian invention of zero and the digits — 0 to 9 — that the whole world uses today.

Counting before zero

The oldest number records are tally marks cut into bones — one notch per animal or day. Farming peoples needed more: the Egyptians wrote numbers with picture signs, and the Romans used letters — I, V, X, L, C — a system fine for carving dates but painful for multiplying.

Babylon counted in sixties, which is why our hours still have 60 minutes. Babylonian scribes eventually used a placeholder mark for an empty position — a step towards zero, but not yet a number in its own right. Across the ocean, Maya astronomers in Central America independently developed their own zero sign for their calendar.

India invents zero

In India, mathematicians went the final step. By the 600s CE, zero — called shunya, 'empty' — was treated as a real number. In 628 CE, Brahmagupta wrote rules for adding, subtracting and multiplying with zero and with negative numbers — the first clear rules of their kind anywhere.

Indian numerals, with place value — where a digit's position gives its size — made calculation fast and easy. Traders and scholars carried the system west; in Baghdad, al-Khwarizmi wrote a book about Indian reckoning, and the numerals became known in Europe as 'Arabic numerals'.

The numbers conquer the world

Europe resisted at first — the new figures were nicknamed 'cipher' and some cities banned them, preferring Roman numerals and the counting board. But merchants found the new arithmetic unbeatable, and Fibonacci's book of 1202 helped spread it.

Printing fixed the shapes of 0–9, and the system became the world's common language of number — the basis of science, money, computers and space flight. Every time we write a date, a price or a phone number, we use an invention that travelled from ancient India to everywhere on Earth.

Did You Know?

The word 'zero' comes from the Arabic sifr, which itself came from the Sanskrit word shunya — meaning 'empty'. Our word 'cipher' comes from the same root.
