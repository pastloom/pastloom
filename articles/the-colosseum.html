The Colosseum

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Colosseum is a giant oval arena in Rome, Italy — the largest amphitheatre ever built in the ancient world. The Roman emperors built it about 1,950 years ago for public shows.

Inside, crowds of about 50,000 people watched gladiator fights, wild animal hunts and dramas. Its broken but mighty walls are still the symbol of Rome today.

A gift to the people of Rome

After the hated emperor Nero died, the new emperor Vespasian drained Nero's private lake and built a public arena on the site — a gift of entertainment to the Roman people. His son Titus opened it in 80 CE with 100 days of games.

The Colosseum was a marvel of design. Eighty numbered entrances let huge crowds pour in and out in minutes, and a giant cloth roof, the velarium, could be pulled across by sailors to shade the seats.

The shows inside

Gladiators fought in the arena, wild beasts such as lions, elephants and hippos were hunted, and criminals faced terrible deaths. In the earliest days, the arena could even be flooded to stage mock sea battles with real ships.

Beneath the wooden floor lay the hypogeum — two floors of tunnels, cages and lifts that raised animals and fighters into the arena as if by magic.

From arena to ruin to symbol

Earthquakes in the Middle Ages shook down part of the outer wall, and much of its stone was carried away to build palaces and churches. For centuries the Colosseum was used as a quarry, homes and workshops.

In the 1700s the popes protected it as a holy place, and today it is cared for as a treasure of world history. About seven million visitors a year walk through the arena where Rome once roared.

Did You Know?

When the Colosseum opened in 80 CE, the emperor Titus celebrated with 100 days of games — and the arena could be flooded with water to stage battles between real warships.
