History of Roads and Travel

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Roads are among the oldest human inventions. Long before wheels, people followed paths between hunting grounds, rivers and villages — some footpaths are thousands of years old.

Empires built great roads to move armies, taxes and news: Persia's Royal Road, Rome's stone highways and the routes that joined the Silk Road. Travel was slow and dangerous for centuries — then railways and cars changed it forever.

Paths, and the first great roads

Early travellers walked or rode animals along paths worn by use. The Persian Empire built the Royal Road across its lands, with rest stations and fresh horses, so the king's messages could travel in days instead of months.

China's rulers built roads and canals, and merchants linked them into the Silk Road — not one road but a web of routes carrying silk, spices and ideas between East and West.

Rome paves an empire

Roman engineers built straight, paved roads with layers of stone and drainage ditches — so well made that some are still used today. Milestones told travellers the distance to Rome, the heart of the network.

Roads let Roman armies march fast, traders carry goods, and letters move across Europe. When the empire fell, many roads decayed, and travel in Europe became slow and unsafe for a thousand years.

Coaches, trains and the open road

From the 1600s, stagecoaches ran on timetables, and toll roads paid for better surfaces. Then steam trains in the 1800s shrank journeys from days to hours.

Cars and motorways in the 1900s gave ordinary families the freedom of the road. A journey that took a Roman messenger a month can now be driven in a day — or flown in hours.

Did You Know?

The saying that all roads lead to Rome was once nearly true — Rome's milestones measured distances from a golden marker in the Roman Forum.
