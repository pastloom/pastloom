Ancient Civilisations

The Aztec Empire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Aztec Empire was the last great civilisation of ancient Mexico. In less than a century after 1428, the Mexica people built an empire of millions from their island capital Tenochtitlan — a city of canals, temples and markets on the lake where Mexico City stands today.

Their world of astronomy, poetry and human sacrifice ended with shocking speed in 1519–21, when Spanish invaders, Indigenous allies and European diseases combined to destroy it.

The Mexica find an eagle

The Mexica — the people we call Aztecs — were latecomers to the rich Valley of Mexico, arriving in the 1200s as poor migrants hired as soldiers by greater powers. Their priests said their god Huitzilopochtli had promised them a sign: an eagle perched on a cactus. In 1325, tradition says, they saw it on a swampy island in Lake Texcoco — and there they built Tenochtitlan.

Building a city on a lake took genius: causeways linked it to the shore, aqueducts brought fresh water, dikes held back floods, and chinampas — 'floating gardens' of mud and reeds — grew maize, beans and tomatoes in the shallows.

The Triple Alliance empire

In 1428 Tenochtitlan joined Texcoco and Tlacopan in the Triple Alliance and began conquering. Within ninety years the alliance ruled some 5–6 million people, from the Pacific to the Gulf of Mexico. It was not a tightly governed empire but a tribute machine: conquered cities kept their rulers and paid — in maize, cacao, cotton cloth, feathers, jade and warriors.

Tenochtitlan swelled to perhaps 200,000 people — larger than London or Seville at the time — with markets that drew tens of thousands daily, a great temple precinct, schools, zoos and botanical gardens that amazed the arriving Spanish.

Cosmos, calendars and sacrifice

Aztec religion held that the world had already ended four times, and that the fifth sun would die unless fed. Human sacrifice — usually of war captives — was offered to keep the sun rising; the scale of it is still debated by historians, but it was central to Aztec state religion and terrified their enemies.

The Aztecs were also fine astronomers and mathematicians. Priests tracked a 260-day ritual calendar alongside a 365-day solar year, meshing them in 52-year cycles. The great Sun Stone, carved about 1500, maps their cosmos in stone. Their physicians, herbalists and surgeons were skilled, and Nahuatl poetry — 'flower and song' — remains a treasure of world literature.

Cortés, disease and the fall

In 1519 Hernán Cortés landed with a few hundred Spaniards. The conquest succeeded not by Spanish strength alone: Cortés gained tens of thousands of Indigenous allies, above all Tlaxcala, which hated Aztec tribute demands. After a first welcome, violence exploded; the emperor Moctezuma II died in 1520, and the Mexica drove the Spanish out in a night of slaughter.

But smallpox — a disease new to the Americas — was already raging, killing perhaps a quarter of Tenochtitlan's people, including the new emperor. In 1521 Cortés returned with ships built on the lake, cut the aqueducts, and after a brutal 93-day siege the starving city fell on 13 August 1521. Mexico City rose on its ruins — yet Nahuatl is still spoken by over a million people, and Aztec foods, from chocolate to tomatoes, conquered the world's kitchens in return.

Did You Know?

The Aztec capital Tenochtitlan was built on a lake with canals instead of streets — visitors from Spain compared it to Venice, and its floating gardens still grow flowers in Mexico City today.
