History of Cutlery

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Cutlery — knives, forks and spoons — seems so natural that we forget each piece was invented separately, thousands of years apart. The knife is ancient, the spoon is very old, but the fork reached most tables only a few hundred years ago.

In much of Asia, chopsticks have done the same work for more than 3,000 years. How we eat is a history of tools, manners and trade.

Knives and spoons first

Stone Age people cut food with flint knives, and the knife stayed the main eating tool for millennia — carried at the belt and used for everything. Spoons of wood, shell, bone and bronze scooped soups and porridge in Egypt, Greece and China alike.

At medieval tables, food was shared from common dishes. Guests brought their own knives, ate with spoons and fingers, and wiped their hands on the tablecloth — or the bread.

The fork's slow journey

Table forks appeared in the Byzantine Empire and reached Italy by the 1000s — legend says a Byzantine princess shocked Venice by eating with a golden fork. From Italy, forks spread to France, helped by Catherine de' Medici's court in the 1500s.

England resisted: the writer Thomas Coryat brought a fork home from Italy in 1611 and was teased for it. But forks made eating cleaner and politer, and by the 1700s the knife-fork-spoon set ruled Western tables.

Cutlery for the world

Factories in Sheffield and Solingen made steel cutlery for millions in the 1800s, and Harry Brearley's stainless steel of 1913 ended the age of polishing and rust.

Meanwhile, chopsticks — perfected in China and spread to Japan, Korea and Vietnam — serve a third of humanity. Knife, fork, spoon or chopsticks: every culture solved the same puzzle, how to carry food neatly from plate to mouth.

Did You Know?

When forks first reached England, some priests called them an insult to God — since God gave people fingers to eat with.
