History of Ocean Exploration

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The oceans cover more than two thirds of the Earth, and people have explored them for thousands of years — first in small boats along the coasts, then across the open sea, and finally down into the deep itself.

Ocean exploration gave us world maps, global trade and whole sciences. It also carried disease, conquest and slavery — the sea connected the world for good and for ill.

The first ocean peoples

Long before Europeans dared the open sea, Polynesian navigators crossed the vast Pacific in double-hulled canoes. They read the stars, the winds, the waves and the flight of birds, and settled islands scattered over an area larger than any empire on land.

Phoenician sailors traded around the Mediterranean and beyond, Arab dhows crossed the Indian Ocean with the monsoon winds, and Chinese treasure fleets under Zheng He reached Africa in the 1400s.

Sailing around the world

In 1492 Columbus crossed the Atlantic, and in 1519 Ferdinand Magellan's expedition set out to circle the Earth. Magellan died on the way, but his ship Victoria completed the voyage in 1522 — proof that the oceans join into one world sea.

For 300 years, explorers mapped coastlines and hunted for sea routes to Asia's riches. Captain James Cook's careful charts of the Pacific in the 1700s filled in the last great blanks of the world map.

Science takes to the sea

In 1872 the British ship Challenger began a four-year voyage to study the oceans themselves: their depths, temperatures, currents and creatures. Its 50 volumes of reports founded the science of oceanography.

In the 1900s, echo sounders revealed mountains and trenches beneath the waves, and divers and submersibles went down to see them. Today satellites watch the whole ocean from space — yet most of the deep sea floor is still less well mapped than the surface of the Moon.

Did You Know?

Polynesian navigators crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean without compass, clock or chart — some carried 'stick charts' of shells and sticks that mapped the pattern of the waves.
