The Indian Ocean Tsunami of 2004

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

On the morning of 26 December 2004, one of the most powerful earthquakes ever recorded struck beneath the Indian Ocean near Sumatra, Indonesia. The quake lifted the sea floor and sent giant waves — a tsunami — racing across the ocean in every direction.

The waves struck the coasts of fourteen countries, from Indonesia and Thailand to India, Sri Lanka and even East Africa. About 230,000 people died, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history.

The sea floor tears

At 7:59 local time, a stretch of sea floor about 1,300 km long ruptured as one tectonic plate, forced under another for centuries, finally snapped upward. The whole ocean above it was lifted and dropped, and waves sped outward as fast as a jet airliner in deep water.

In Aceh, closest to the quake, the sea first pulled back, exposing the sea bed, then returned as walls of water up to 30 metres high in places. Whole towns were smashed and swept away within minutes.

Waves across an ocean

Because there was no tsunami warning system in the Indian Ocean, most coasts had no official warning at all. The waves reached Sri Lanka and India about two hours after the quake, and Thailand's holiday beaches about the same time, catching thousands of local people and tourists completely by surprise.

Some lives were saved by knowledge and luck: a ten-year-old British girl, Tilly Smith, recognised the strange behaviour of the sea from a school geography lesson and warned her beach in Thailand, and on one Indonesian island, an old oral tradition about earthquakes and the sea led villagers to flee to high ground — almost all survived.

The world responds

The disaster brought one of the largest aid efforts in history: governments, charities and ordinary people around the world gave billions of dollars, and rescue teams flew in from dozens of countries.

Its greatest legacy is protection. The Indian Ocean now has a tsunami warning system with sea-floor sensors and warning centres, modelled on the one the Pacific had enjoyed for decades, so that the warning gap of 2004 should never happen again.

Did You Know?

The earthquake was so powerful that it made the whole Earth wobble slightly on its axis and shortened the length of the day by a tiny fraction of a second.
