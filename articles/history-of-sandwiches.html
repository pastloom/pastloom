History of Sandwiches

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A sandwich is a meal held in the hand: a filling carried between slices of bread. The idea is far older than its famous name — people have wrapped food in bread for thousands of years.

The name comes from an English lord of the 1700s who, legend says, did not want to leave his card game for dinner. His snack conquered the world.

Bread as a plate and a wrapper

Long before the name existed, workers carried cheese or meat in bread. In ancient Jewish tradition, Hillel the Elder wrapped lamb and herbs in soft bread at Passover, about 2,000 years ago.

Medieval eaters used thick slices of bread called trenchers as plates that soaked up juices — an edible plate is only one step from a sandwich.

The Earl and his cards

John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich, was a busy politician — and, the story goes, a keen card player. Around 1762 he asked for meat between slices of bread so he could eat without leaving the table or dirtying the cards.

Other gentlemen began ordering the same as Sandwich, and the name stuck. Historians note he may simply have wanted a quick working lunch at his desk — either way, the sandwich was born.

The world's packed lunch

Factory workers, schoolchildren and travellers adopted the sandwich because it needs no plate, knife or reheating. Tea sandwiches became dainty in Victorian England, while giant filled rolls fed labourers elsewhere.

Every country has made it local: Vietnam's banh mi, the Middle East's wraps, America's towering club sandwich. One English earl's snack became a world language of lunch.

Did You Know?

The town of Sandwich in England really exists — the Earl took his title from it, and in 2012 the town celebrated 250 years of the sandwich with a festival.
