Nebuchadnezzar II

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Nebuchadnezzar II was the greatest king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire. He ruled from 605 to 562 BCE — more than forty years.

He is remembered for two things: rebuilding Babylon into the wonder-city of its age, and conquering Jerusalem, an event that changed the history of the Jewish people forever.

The warrior king

Nebuchadnezzar first won fame as crown prince, crushing Egypt at the Battle of Carchemish in 605 BCE. When his father died that same year, he raced home to claim the throne.

As king he campaigned almost every year, keeping Egypt at bay and bringing the small kingdoms of Syria and the Levant — including Judah — under Babylonian control.

The builder of Babylon

At home, Nebuchadnezzar poured his empire's wealth into Babylon: double walls, the blue Ishtar Gate, the Processional Way, temples, palaces and canals.

Ancient writers credited him with the Hanging Gardens, a terraced mountain of greenery said to have been built for his wife Amytis, who missed the hills of her Median homeland — though archaeologists still debate whether the gardens stood in Babylon at all.

Jerusalem, the exile, and after

When Judah rebelled, Nebuchadnezzar struck twice. In 597 BCE he took Jerusalem and carried its king and craftsmen to Babylon. When rebellion came again, his army destroyed the city and Solomon's Temple in 586 BCE.

Thousands of Judeans were exiled to Babylon. This Babylonian Captivity lasted until Persia's Cyrus let the exiles return — and it deeply shaped Jewish faith, writing and memory.

No later Babylonian king matched him. Weak rulers and quarrels followed, and just 23 years after his death Cyrus of Persia took Babylon.

Yet Nebuchadnezzar's name never faded: he appears in the Bible's Book of Daniel, and Verdi's opera Nabucco — named after him — gave Italy one of its most beloved choruses.

Did You Know?

Verdi's famous opera about this Babylonian king is called Nabucco — a shortened Italian form of Nebuchadnezzar's name.
