Inventions in History

Paper

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Paper is so ordinary that we forget someone had to invent it. It was created in China — perfected by the official Cai Lun in 105 CE — and it slowly replaced bamboo, silk, papyrus and animal skin as the world's writing surface.

Paper travelled the Silk Road to the Islamic world and then to Europe, where it met the printing press and made books, newspapers and ideas cheap for the first time. Almost everything in modern life — money, packaging, schoolbooks — still depends on it.

Writing before paper

Every civilisation needed something to write on, and everything was flawed. Mesopotamia used heavy clay; Egypt used papyrus, which grew only on the Nile and rotted in damp climates; China wrote on bamboo strips and expensive silk; Europe and the Islamic world used parchment — animal skin, costly and slow to make. Books were treasures because their pages were.

In China, experimenters were already making sheets from plant fibres by the 2nd century BCE. The breakthrough is credited to Cai Lun, a court official who in 105 CE presented the emperor with a standard process: bark, hemp waste, rags and fishing nets, beaten to pulp, mixed with water, and caught on a screen to dry as a sheet. It was light, smooth, cheap — paper.

Paper travels west

For centuries China guarded the craft while paper spread through its world — to Korea, Japan and Vietnam. Then, tradition says, after the Battle of Talas in 751 CE, captured Chinese papermakers set up mills in Samarkand. The Islamic world embraced paper eagerly: Baghdad had mills by the 790s, and paper helped fuel the great age of Arabic scholarship, administration and bookselling.

From Muslim Spain and Sicily the craft entered Christian Europe — mills in Italy by the 1200s, Germany by 1390. European paper, made from linen rags, was cheaper than parchment but still dear; a book remained a serious purchase. Paper was waiting for a partner.

Paper meets print

The partner arrived about 1440: Gutenberg's printing press with movable type. Printing on parchment was unthinkable at scale — an edition of 180 Bibles would have needed a herd — but paper could feed the presses. Mills multiplied along Europe's rivers, rags became a traded commodity, and the price of books collapsed.

Cheap paper then carried everything: newspapers, pamphlets, novels, schoolbooks, banknotes, maps and government files. The scientific revolution, the Reformation and the spread of literacy all ran on rag paper. In the 1800s, machines learned to make paper from wood pulp in endless rolls, and paper became so cheap the modern world wrapped itself in it.

The paperless age that wasn't

In the late 1900s, computers promised a 'paperless office'. Instead, for decades, offices printed more than ever — paper is a superb display: cheap, foldable, readable in sunlight, and it never runs out of battery. Only with phones and tablets did newsprint and office paper finally decline, even as packaging paper boomed with online shopping.

Two thousand years after Cai Lun, the world still makes over 400 million tonnes of paper a year. His invention remains what it was in Han-dynasty China: the cheapest way ever found to carry words and wrap the world.

Did You Know?

Before paper, Europe wrote on parchment made from animal skins — a single large Bible could require the skins of more than 200 sheep. Paper changed the price of a book forever.
