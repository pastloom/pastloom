Ancient Civilisations

The Sumerians

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Sumerians built the world's first civilisation. In southern Mesopotamia — modern Iraq — between about 4500 and 1900 BCE, they raised the first cities, invented writing, the wheel, the plough and the 60-minute hour.

Almost everything later civilisations did, the Sumerians did first: schools, laws, taxes, epic poetry and beer recipes. When we check the time or divide a circle into 360 degrees, we are using Sumerian ideas.

The land between two rivers

Sumer lay at the southern end of Mesopotamia, 'the land between the rivers' — the Tigris and Euphrates. The rivers flooded unpredictably and rain was scarce, so Sumerian farmers dug canals and dikes to carry water to their barley fields. Organising that work took leaders, records and cooperation — and produced history's first cities.

By 3500 BCE, Uruk had grown into perhaps the largest city on Earth, with tens of thousands of people, monumental temples on stepped towers (ziggurats), and walls that legend credited to King Gilgamesh. Rival city-states — Ur, Lagash, Kish, Eridu — competed for land and water for a thousand years.

The invention of writing

To track grain, taxes and trade, temple accountants pressed marks into clay tablets. By about 3400–3300 BCE these marks became cuneiform — 'wedge-shaped' writing made with a cut reed. It is the earliest writing system we know, older even than Egyptian hieroglyphs in their developed form.

Writing changed everything it touched. Sumerians wrote laws, letters, school exercises, hymns, jokes — and the Epic of Gilgamesh, the oldest great work of literature, telling of a king's friendship and his failed quest to escape death. Thousands of their tablets survive in museums today.

First in almost everything

The Sumerians' list of firsts is astonishing: the potter's wheel and then the wheeled cart; the plough and the sail; bronze-making; a number system based on 60 — which is why our hour has 60 minutes, our minute 60 seconds, and our circle 360 degrees. They brewed beer, kept calendars of moon months, and divided the day into hours.

They also built the first schools — 'tablet houses' where boys learned cuneiform through long days of copying — and some of the first law codes, such as the Code of Ur-Nammu, older than Hammurabi's by three centuries.

Absorbed, forgotten, rediscovered

Sumer's city-states weakened through war and the slow salting of their irrigated fields. Around 2000 BCE the Elamites sacked Ur; Amorite kingdoms, then Babylon and Assyria, inherited Sumer's culture. The Sumerian language died as a mother tongue but lived on for two thousand years as the Latin of its world — studied in schools, used in ritual.

Then it was forgotten entirely, until archaeologists dug up the tablets in the 1800s and scholars deciphered cuneiform. The first civilisation had to be discovered twice: once by the people who built it, and once by us.

Did You Know?

We have Sumer to thank for the shape of our clocks: their number system was based on 60, so we still divide hours into 60 minutes and circles into 360 degrees.
