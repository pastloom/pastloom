Leonardo da Vinci

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519) was the defining genius of the Renaissance — painter of the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, and at the same time an engineer, anatomist and inventor who filled thousands of notebook pages with designs centuries ahead of his time.

He had almost no formal education. What he had was bottomless curiosity: he dissected bodies to learn how they moved, studied rivers to paint water, and asked questions about everything — why the sky is blue, how birds fly, how the heart works.

The apprentice from Vinci

Leonardo was born in 1452 in the hill town of Vinci, the illegitimate son of a notary and a peasant girl — a start that barred him from university and the professions. Apprenticed at about fourteen to the Florentine artist Verrocchio, he learned painting, sculpture, metalwork and engineering in one of Europe's finest workshops. Legend says Verrocchio, seeing an angel his young apprentice had painted, put down his own brush forever.

By his twenties Leonardo was a master in his own right — but an unusual one: as interested in how water flows and muscles pull as in how a Madonna should smile. That double curiosity made him the supreme symbol of the Renaissance.

Painter of two immortal pictures

Leonardo painted slowly, experimentally, and finished little — yet two of his works became the most famous paintings on Earth. The Last Supper (1495–98), painted on a refectory wall in Milan, catches the instant Christ announces his betrayal: each apostle a study in shock. Its experimental technique began decaying within decades, and restorers fight for it still.

The Mona Lisa (begun about 1503) — a Florentine merchant's wife with a half-smile and a gaze that follows the room — became, after its theft from the Louvre in 1911 made world headlines, the most visited, parodied and valuable portrait in existence. Leonardo carried it with him for years, perfecting its smoky softness — sfumato — layer by layer.

The notebooks: a mind on paper

Leonardo's truest work may be his notebooks — thousands of pages, written in mirror script, crammed with drawings: flying machines with flapping wings, a parachute, a helicopter-like screw, diving suits, an armoured car, studies of rivers, plants, storms and the human body, which he explored by dissecting corpses by night.

Almost none of it was published in his lifetime, so his science influenced little directly — he was a prophet without an audience. When the notebooks finally emerged, the world marvelled at how far one mind, armed only with eyes and questions, had seen into the future.

The old master in France

Leonardo's last years were spent in France, invited by King Francis I, who gave him a manor at Clos Lucé near Amboise and, Vasari says, simply wanted him near — 'the king was in the habit of visiting him often'. There the old man, his right hand partly paralysed, organised his notes and received visitors like a living legend.

He died on 2 May 1519, aged 67. Five centuries later he remains the archetype of genius — the painter-scientist in whom art and knowledge were one thing: a man who believed that to truly see anything, from a smile to a river, was to begin to understand everything.

Did You Know?

Leonardo wrote his notebooks in mirror writing, readable only in a mirror — probably because he was left-handed and it kept the ink from smudging, not to keep secrets.
