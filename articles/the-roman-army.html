The Roman Army

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Roman army was the most successful fighting force of the ancient world. Its legions conquered an empire of 70 million people — and then guarded it for about 500 years.

Its secret was not just courage. Roman soldiers were trained like athletes, drilled like machines, and worked like builders: they marched about 30 kilometres a day, then built a fortified camp before dinner.

The legions

A legion was divided into ten cohorts and smaller centuries of about 80 men, led by centurions — tough veteran officers who kept strict discipline. Legionaries were Roman citizens; other troops, called auxiliaries, came from the provinces and earned citizenship after 25 years of service.

Every soldier carried his weapons, tools, food and cooking pot. The men joked that they were 'Marius's mules', after the general who made them carry so much.

Training and discipline

Recruits trained with wooden swords twice as heavy as real ones, marched in full kit, and practised building camps, bridges and siege works. Discipline was famously harsh — but the rewards were real: regular pay, a share of plunder, and land or money at retirement.

This training made Roman armies hard to beat. Even after a disaster, Rome could raise a new army, train it the same way, and fight on — as it did against Hannibal.

An army that built an empire

The army was also Rome's great building service. Soldiers built the roads armies marched on, the walls that guarded the frontiers — like Hadrian's Wall in Britain — and forts that grew into cities.

Where the legions camped, Roman law, language and goods followed. Many European cities, from London to Vienna, began as Roman army camps.

Did You Know?

A Roman legionary's retirement gift after 25 years could be a plot of land — many veteran colonies became towns whose names still end in '-chester' or '-caster' in England, from the Latin word castra, meaning a camp.
