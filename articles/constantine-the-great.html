Constantine the Great

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Constantine the Great was a Roman emperor who changed the course of history twice. He made Christianity legal in the Roman Empire after centuries when Christians could be punished for their faith.

He also built a new capital city, Constantinople — modern Istanbul — which remained one of the world's great cities for more than a thousand years.

A soldier emperor

Constantine was the son of a Roman army commander. When his father died in Britain in 306 CE, the soldiers there proclaimed young Constantine emperor.

But there were other claimants, and Constantine had to fight his way to power. His great test came in 312 CE, marching on Rome against his rival Maxentius.

The sign in the sky

Before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, Christian writers say, Constantine saw a sign of the cross in the sky with the words 'In this sign, conquer.' His soldiers painted the symbol on their shields — and won the battle.

The next year, the Edict of Milan made Christianity legal across the empire. Constantine gave money to churches, built great basilicas, and was baptised shortly before his death.

A new capital: Constantinople

Constantine decided Rome was too far from the empire's rich eastern lands and dangerous frontiers. On the site of the Greek city of Byzantium he built a 'New Rome', named after himself: Constantinople.

Guarded by the sea and strong walls, Constantinople became the capital of the eastern Roman — Byzantine — world, and it did not fall to an enemy for more than 1,100 years.

Did You Know?

Constantinople, the city Constantine founded in 330 CE, stayed a great imperial capital until 1453 — more than eleven centuries later.
