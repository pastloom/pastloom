History of Anaesthesia

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Anaesthesia is the medical art of making patients sleep deeply and feel no pain during operations. Before it existed, surgery was a race against agony — patients were held down, and speed was the only mercy.

In October 1846, a Boston dentist showed the world that ether could put a patient peacefully to sleep. Within months, surgery everywhere was transformed forever.

Surgery before sleep

For thousands of years, healers tried to dull pain with opium, strong drink, mandrake root, cold, pressure on nerves — even hypnotism. Nothing worked reliably, and strong doses could kill.

So operations stayed short and brutal. The best surgeons were the fastest, patients dreaded the knife more than the disease, and many chose to suffer illness rather than face surgery awake.

Ether Day, 1846

In the 1840s, dentists noticed that ether and laughing gas — used at travelling shows for fun — left people insensible to pain. Crawford Long quietly used ether for surgery in 1842, but did not publish his work.

On 16 October 1846, William Morton gave ether to a patient at Massachusetts General Hospital while surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumour. The patient felt nothing. 'Gentlemen, this is no humbug,' Warren declared — and the news raced around the world.

Safer sleep for surgery

Chloroform followed within a year, and anaesthesia spread to childbirth, dentistry and battlefields. But early gases were dangerous in careless hands, and a new specialist was born: the anaesthetist, whose whole job is safe sleep.

Modern anaesthesia blends measured gases and drugs, with machines watching every breath and heartbeat. The screaming operating theatre of 1840 has become one of the calmest, most carefully watched rooms in the hospital.

Did You Know?

News of ether crossed the Atlantic by ship — yet within just two months of the Boston demonstration, surgeons in London were already operating on sleeping patients.
