Cleopatra

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Cleopatra VII was the last ruler of ancient Egypt, and one of the most famous women of the ancient world. She was not only a queen but a skilled politician who spoke nine languages, and she used her intelligence to protect her kingdom at a time when Rome was swallowing every land around the Mediterranean Sea.

For twenty-one years she held Egypt together through alliances with two of Rome's most powerful men, Julius Caesar and Mark Antony. When she died in 30 BCE, Egypt became a Roman province, and the three-thousand-year age of the pharaohs came to an end.

The last pharaoh

Cleopatra belonged to the Ptolemies, a Greek family that had ruled Egypt for almost 300 years after the death of Alexander the Great. She became queen at eighteen, sharing the throne first with her father and then with her younger brother, Ptolemy XIII, as Egyptian custom demanded.

She was different from every ruler of her family before her. The Ptolemies had always spoken Greek and kept Egyptian ways at a distance, but Cleopatra learned the Egyptian language, dressed as an Egyptian queen, and presented herself as the living form of the goddess Isis. It made her genuinely popular with her people.

Caesar and the rolled carpet

In 48 BCE her brother drove her from power, and at the same moment Julius Caesar arrived in Alexandria chasing his rival Pompey. Cleopatra knew her throne depended on Caesar. According to the famous story, she had herself smuggled into his rooms rolled inside a carpet or a linen sack, and stepped out in front of him.

Caesar backed her claim, her brother died in the fighting that followed, and Cleopatra was queen again. She later visited Rome as Caesar's honoured guest, and their son, Caesarion, was born in 47 BCE. When Caesar was murdered in 44 BCE, she hurried home to Egypt and waited to see who would win Rome.

Antony and the dream of an eastern empire

The winner in the east was Mark Antony, one of the three men who now ruled Rome. Cleopatra met him in 41 BCE, sailing up the river to his camp on a golden barge, dressed as the goddess of love. Antony needed Egypt's wealth and grain for his wars; Cleopatra needed a Roman protector. They became allies, partners, and eventually husband and wife, with twins and a third child.

Together they dreamed of an eastern empire ruled from Alexandria. Antony gave Cleopatra and her children lands that had once belonged to Egypt. In Rome, Antony's rival Octavian used all of this as propaganda, painting Cleopatra as a dangerous foreign queen who had bewitched a Roman hero. In 32 BCE Rome declared war — officially on Cleopatra, not on Antony.

Actium and the end of an age

The war was decided at sea. On 2 September 31 BCE, at Actium in Greece, Octavian's fleet defeated the combined ships of Antony and Cleopatra. The couple escaped back to Egypt, but their power was broken. The next summer Octavian's army reached Alexandria itself.

Antony, falsely told that Cleopatra was already dead, took his own life. Days later, in August 30 BCE, Cleopatra also died — by her own hand, the ancient writers say, though the famous story of the snake bite may be legend. Octavian annexed Egypt as a Roman province, and with Cleopatra died the last pharaoh of Egypt.

Did You Know?

Cleopatra lived closer in time to the iPhone than to the building of the Great Pyramid of Egypt.
