Roman Aqueducts

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Roman aqueducts were channels built to carry fresh water from hills and springs into cities, sometimes over distances of 80 kilometres or more. They worked with no pumps at all — only gravity.

Rome itself had eleven aqueducts, filling its baths, fountains and homes with more water than many modern cities enjoy. Their arches still stride across the countryside of Europe.

Why Rome needed water

As Rome grew towards a million people, its river and wells were not enough — and not clean. Baths, public fountains, toilets, gardens and fullers' workshops all demanded constant running water.

Other Roman cities copied the capital. Wherever Rome ruled, an aqueduct on the horizon announced that Roman comfort had arrived.

Building with gravity alone

Surveyors plotted a route that fell very gently — sometimes only a few centimetres per kilometre — so the water flowed steadily without bursting the channel.

Most of the line ran underground or in covered channels at ground level to keep the water clean and cool. Only where valleys had to be crossed did the famous arches march across the land.

Famous aqueducts, and what happened to them

The Pont du Gard in France carried water about 50 kilometres to the city of Nîmes; its three tiers of arches still stand nearly 49 metres high.

Spain keeps the towering aqueduct of Segovia, and Rome's own Aqua Claudia entered the city on arches that still cross the Roman countryside.

Invaders cut aqueducts during sieges, and in the Middle Ages most fell out of repair. Popes later restored some lines to feed Rome's fountains.

One ancient line, the Aqua Virgo, still supplies Rome today — its water splashes into the famous Trevi Fountain, as it has, in changing forms, for 2,000 years.

Did You Know?

The Pont du Gard drops only about 25 centimetres per kilometre along its route — Roman surveyors achieved that precision with simple water levels and plumb lines.
