Inventions in History

The Wheel

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The wheel is one of humanity's most important inventions — yet it arrived surprisingly late, only about 5,500 years ago, and only in one part of the world. First came the potter's wheel in Mesopotamia; then carts, chariots and wagons changed farming, trade and war forever.

Without the wheel there would be no cars, trains, bicycles, clocks or turbines. With it, loads that once needed a hundred backs could be moved by one animal — and the world began to roll.

An idea whose time came slowly

The wheel feels obvious — but humans lived without it for hundreds of thousands of years. Rolling logs probably helped move heavy stones first. The true wheel needed two ideas together: a round disc, and an axle it turns around. That wheel-and-axle pair is one of the six simple machines of physics.

The oldest evidence points to about 3500 BCE, and the first wheels may not have been for transport at all: the potter's wheel appeared in Mesopotamia in this period, letting craftspeople spin clay into perfectly round pots. Within a few centuries, wheeled carts show up almost simultaneously in Mesopotamia, the Caucasus and Europe — the idea spreading, or sparking, along trade routes.

Wheels at war and work

Early wheels were solid wooden discs — heavy but strong, pulled by oxen. Around 2000 BCE came the breakthrough of the spoked wheel: light, fast and springy. Mounted on chariots, it transformed warfare — Egyptian, Hittite and Chinese chariot armies dominated battlefields for a thousand years. Later came the war-wagons, coaches and artillery of gunpowder ages.

In peace, wheels ground grain in watermills, lifted water, drove looms and clocks, and carried the world's goods. The Industrial Revolution ran on wheels: flywheels, gear wheels, locomotive wheels, and finally the bicycle — whose 'safety' design of the 1880s put personal transport within everyone's reach.

The world without wheels

The wheel was not universal. The civilisations of the Americas knew the principle — wheeled toys survive from ancient Mexico — but built no wheeled transport: they had no large domestic animals to pull carts, and in the Andes the llama was a pack animal suited to mountain paths, not roads. Great cities like Tenochtitlan and Cuzco ran on feet, boats and llamas instead.

Australia's Aboriginal peoples and many others likewise never adopted the wheel. The lesson is subtle: inventions spread only where geography and economy make them useful. The wheel needed roads, draught animals and loads worth hauling.

The wheel today

Today the wheel is invisible because it is everywhere: more than a billion cars, countless bicycles, trains, turbines, hard drives and the wheels inside every machine that turns. The tyre — pneumatic since 1888 — is its refined descendant.

From a potter's turntable in Mesopotamia to the rovers rolling on Mars, the wheel remains humanity's most successful machine part. Five and a half thousand years old, and still turning.

Did You Know?

Nature never evolved a wheel — no animal rolls. The wheel is one of the very few great inventions with no model in the natural world.
