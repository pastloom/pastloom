History of Reading

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Reading feels natural, but the way we read has a history. For most of the past, reading meant reading aloud — even when alone — and books were rare treasures read by a few.

Silent reading, word spaces, cheap books and finally screens each changed who could read and how. The quiet reader sitting alone with a book is a surprisingly modern invention.

Reading aloud

In ancient Greece and Rome, a text was something you performed. Letters ran together without spaces or punctuation, so reading meant working the words out with your voice.

Trained readers often read aloud to their masters and guests. A silent reader was so unusual that Augustine wrote about Ambrose's habit as a marvel.

Spaces, silence and spectacles

Between the 700s and 800s, Irish monks — for whom Latin was a foreign language — began separating words with spaces. Reading became faster, quieter and more private.

As silent reading spread, so did private prayer, private study and eventually the novel. Spectacles, invented in the 1200s, let ageing scholars keep reading.

Reading for everyone

Printing, schools and cheap editions turned reading from a priestly skill into a mass habit in the 1800s. Railway bookstalls, lending libraries and penny papers created the modern reading public.

In our own time, reading has moved to screens and competes with video and games. Yet more people read more words each day — messages, posts, pages — than at any time in history.

Did You Know?

The Latin phrase for old-style writing, scriptio continua, means 'continuous writing' — a page with no spaces at all, which the reader had to separate into words while reading.
