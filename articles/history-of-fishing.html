History of Fishing

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Fishing is catching fish for food or for sport, with hooks, nets, traps and spears. It is one of the oldest human skills: people were fishing tens of thousands of years before farming began.

For most of history, fishing meant survival and trade. Only in the last few centuries did 'angling' — fishing with a rod for pleasure — become one of the world's favourite pastimes.

Hooks of bone and shell

Long before farms, rivers and coasts fed humanity. Early fishers used their hands, spears, woven traps and lines with hooks carved from bone, shell and thorn. A shell fish hook found in a cave in East Timor is about 23,000 years old, and some finds may be older still.

Ancient Egyptians painted fishers at work on tomb walls, and the Romans fished with rods, lines and even early reels. Fishing villages grew wherever water was rich, and dried and salted fish became a great trade food.

The gentle art of angling

Fishing for pleasure has its own classic book. In 1653 Izaak Walton published The Compleat Angler, a calm celebration of rivers, patience and the fisherman's life. It became one of the most reprinted books in English.

Through the 1700s and 1800s, rods grew lighter, reels smoother, and artificial flies — tiny handmade insects of feather and silk — turned trout and salmon fishing into a skilled art. Angling clubs, competitions and fishing magazines followed.

An industry and a warning

The 1900s transformed fishing at sea: steam trawlers, refrigerated ships, nylon nets and sonar let fleets find and catch fish on an enormous scale. Fishing became one of the world's great food industries.

It also brought a warning. Whole fish populations collapsed from overfishing, and nations now set limits and protected areas. The oldest of food skills faces its newest test: catching fish without emptying the water.

Did You Know?

Izaak Walton's 'The Compleat Angler' has been reprinted hundreds of times since 1653 — some say it is among the most reprinted books in the English language after the Bible and Shakespeare.
