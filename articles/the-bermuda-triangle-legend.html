The Bermuda Triangle Legend

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Bermuda Triangle is a stretch of Atlantic Ocean between Florida, Bermuda and Puerto Rico where, legend says, ships and planes vanish without a trace. Books and films call it one of the world's great mysteries.

Here is the honest part: the Bermuda Triangle is a legend, not a proven danger. When researchers checked the famous cases one by one, almost all had ordinary explanations — storms, accidents and reporting mistakes. The real history here is the history of a story, and how it grew.

How the legend began

The waters between Florida, Bermuda and Puerto Rico are among the busiest on Earth — so, like any busy road, they see many accidents. Columbus himself noted strange compass behaviour in the region, caused by a natural quirk of Earth's magnetism.

In 1950 and 1952, magazine writers linked several losses in the area into one mystery. The pieces were read widely, and the idea of a cursed patch of ocean took hold.

Flight 19 and the bestsellers

The legend's centrepiece is Flight 19: five torpedo bombers that vanished on a training flight in December 1945. The evidence shows their leader became disoriented, their compasses troubled them, and they ran out of fuel over a stormy sea at night. A rescue flying boat sent after them exploded in mid-air — a known danger of that aircraft, witnessed by a ship.

In the 1960s and 1970s, popular books gathered old cases, dropped the ordinary details and added new claims — sunken Atlantis, UFOs, time warps. The Bermuda Triangle became an entertainment industry.

What careful checking found

Librarian and pilot Larry Kusche re-checked the famous cases against original records. Ships said to have vanished in calm weather had sunk in documented storms; some 'mysteries' were ships that arrived safely; others had been lost in the Pacific, not the Atlantic at all.

Insurers charge no extra for crossing the Triangle, and scientists point to real but ordinary hazards: hurricanes, the powerful Gulf Stream current, and sudden violent weather. The legend survives not on evidence, but because a mystery is more fun than a weather report.

Did You Know?

Some Bermuda Triangle books listed ships as mysteriously 'vanished' that later checking found safe in port — or wrecked in completely different oceans on the other side of the world.
