The French Revolution

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The French Revolution (1789–1799) was the uprising that destroyed France's thousand-year-old monarchy and proclaimed that all men are born free and equal. It began with bread prices and taxes, stormed the Bastille, executed a king, descended into the Terror — and ended with Napoleon.

No event did more to create the modern political world. Its slogan — liberty, equality, fraternity — and its Declaration of the Rights of Man still echo in every constitution on Earth.

A kingdom in crisis

By 1789 France was Europe's richest and most populous kingdom — and bankrupt. Years of war, including help to the American Revolution, had emptied the treasury; taxes fell hardest on the poorest; bread prices soared after bad harvests. Society was divided into three Estates: clergy, nobility and everyone else — the Third Estate, 97% of the people, paying for all.

King Louis XVI, well-meaning but weak, summoned the Estates-General for the first time in 175 years to raise taxes. Instead, the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly and swore the Tennis Court Oath: they would not disperse until France had a constitution.

1789: the year everything changed

On 14 July 1789, a Paris crowd stormed the Bastille, the royal fortress-prison — a symbol of tyranny, and France's national day ever since. In August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges in a single night and issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: men are born free and equal; sovereignty belongs to the nation.

In October, thousands of market women marched to Versailles and brought the king back to Paris as a virtual prisoner. The Church's lands were seized, a constitution made France a constitutional monarchy — and the old order, a thousand years deep, had fallen in months.

Republic and Terror

War changed everything. In 1792 revolutionary France went to war with Austria and Prussia; defeats and rumours of treason radicalised Paris. The monarchy was abolished, a republic proclaimed, and in January 1793 Louis XVI was guillotined — followed by Queen Marie Antoinette. Europe's kings watched in horror and armed.

To save the revolution from its enemies, the Committee of Public Safety under Robespierre unleashed the Reign of Terror (1793–94): some 17,000 people were officially executed and many more died in prison or civil war, especially in the Vendée. The Terror devoured its own — in July 1794 Robespierre himself was arrested and guillotined, and the fever broke.

Napoleon and the legacy

Five unstable years followed under the Directory, until in November 1799 a brilliant young general, Napoleon Bonaparte, seized power — ending the Revolution and, in time, crowning himself Emperor. Yet Napoleon spread the Revolution's core achievements across Europe by law and by conquest: legal equality, the Napoleonic Code, careers open to talent, the end of feudalism.

The French Revolution's ideas — liberty, equality, citizenship, human rights, the nation as the source of power — became the founding language of modern politics. Every revolution since has argued with 1789, and every democracy lives in its shadow.

Did You Know?

The Revolution gave France — and the world — the metric system, the tricolour flag, and the words 'left' and 'right' in politics, from where deputies happened to sit in the assembly.
