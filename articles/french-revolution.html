The French Revolution

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The French Revolution began in 1789, when the people of France rose up against their king and the old order. France was deep in debt, bread was expensive, and ordinary people had almost no say in how they were ruled.

The revolutionaries demanded liberty, equality and fraternity — freedom, fairness and brotherhood. Their ideas changed France, and inspired revolutions around the world.

Why people rose up

France's king and nobles lived richly while the country was in debt from wars. Ordinary people paid most of the taxes and had no vote.

When bread prices rose after bad harvests, hunger pushed people to anger. In 1789 the king called a meeting — and the common people demanded a new kind of government.

The Bastille and the new ideas

On 14 July 1789, a crowd in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal prison. The date is still France's national day.

The revolutionaries wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Man, saying all men are born free and equal in rights. Church lands were taken, and old privileges of nobles were swept away.

Terror, Napoleon and the legacy

The revolution turned violent. During the Reign of Terror in 1793–94, thousands — including the king and queen — were executed by guillotine.

A young general, Napoleon Bonaparte, took power in 1799 and spread French laws across Europe. The revolution's ideas of rights and citizenship still shape the modern world.

Did You Know?

The French Revolution's new calendar even tried to change the week — it had ten days instead of seven.
