Life of a Roman Soldier

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A Roman soldier signed up for 25 years. In that time he might march the length of the empire, build roads and forts, fight in battles — and, if he survived, retire with land, money and honour.

This article follows his life: the training that made boys into legionaries, the food, pay and punishments of the camp, and the retirement every soldier dreamed of.

Joining and training

A recruit needed to be a Roman citizen (for the legions), healthy, and tall enough — about 1.7 metres was preferred, though not always found. He took an oath to the emperor, received his kit, and began brutal training.

New soldiers marched about 30 kilometres in five hours carrying full packs, learned to build a camp from nothing, and fought with heavy wooden practice swords. Those who failed drills trained again — the army had no room for the unready.

Daily life in camp

A soldier's day mixed guard duty, patrols, training and endless work: digging ditches, cutting timber, building roads and cleaning. Food was plain but regular — wheat ground and baked by the soldiers themselves, bacon, cheese, lentils and vegetables, washed down with sour wine mixed with water.

Soldiers shared a tent — and their lives — in groups of eight, the contubernium, cooking and eating together for 25 years. Officially, legionaries could not marry while serving, though many had families waiting outside the fort walls.

Danger, reward and retirement

In battle the legionary fought behind his big curved shield, throwing his javelin, then closing with the short sword. Wounds were treated in the fort hospital; cowardice could be punished terribly — a unit that fled might face decimation, one man in ten executed.

But a soldier who served his 25 years received a handsome bonus — under Augustus, 3,000 denarii, many years' pay — or a plot of farmland, often in a colony of veterans. Auxiliary soldiers received Roman citizenship, recorded on a bronze diploma they treasured for life.

Did You Know?

We know one soldier's life in unusual detail: letters home on papyrus from Claudius Terentianus, a recruit in Egypt about 100 CE, beg his father for boots, socks and money — and complain that army life is not what he expected.
