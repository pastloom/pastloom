History of Canals

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A canal is a waterway dug by people so that boats can travel where no river goes. Long before trains and lorries, canals were the motorways of the world — slow, quiet roads of water that carried coal, grain, stone and cloth.

The idea is very old. Ancient Egyptians and Chinese dug canals thousands of years ago, and in the 1700s canals helped power Britain's Industrial Revolution.

Ancient water roads

China's Grand Canal linked the Yellow River and the Yangtze so that grain could feed the capital. Begun in the 5th century BCE and joined up under the Sui dynasty, it is still the longest canal ever built.

In Egypt and Mesopotamia, canals carried water to dry fields and boats to cities. Wherever people dug them, farms and trade grew richer around the water.

The canal age in Britain

In 1761 the Bridgewater Canal opened in England, carrying coal into Manchester. Coal prices fell at once, and factory owners rushed to dig more canals.

Soon about 6,400 km of canals crossed Britain. Horses walked the towpaths pulling loaded narrowboats, and whole families lived aboard. Then fast railways arrived in the 1830s and 1840s, and most trade left the canals within a generation.

Giant ship canals

Two canals changed world trade forever. The Suez Canal (1869) joined the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, so ships no longer sailed around Africa. The Panama Canal (1914) cut through Central America, joining the Atlantic and Pacific oceans.

Today canals live a double life: giant container ships still pass through Suez and Panama, while old narrow canals in Europe carry holiday boats, walkers and wildlife.

Did You Know?

A single flight of locks at Caen Hill in England lifts boats up 72 metres through 29 locks — a staircase of water that takes about six hours to climb.
