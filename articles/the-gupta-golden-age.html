The Gupta Golden Age: Science, Art and Learning

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Gupta Empire ruled much of northern India from about 320 to 550 CE. Historians often call its best years a Golden Age, because learning, art, poetry and science all flowered at the same time.

Under Gupta kings, scholars worked out ideas the whole world still uses — including the number zero and our decimal counting system. Poets wrote great plays, artists carved beautiful temples, and a famous university drew students from faraway lands.

An empire of strong kings

Chandragupta I founded the empire around 320 CE. His son Samudragupta conquered far and wide and was also a poet and musician — his coins show him playing a stringed instrument called the veena.

Chandragupta II, called Vikramaditya, brought the empire to its peak. The Chinese pilgrim Faxian visited during his reign and wrote of a peaceful, well-run land where people were free to travel and trade.

The age of zero and the stars

Gupta-age scholars developed the decimal place-value system — writing numbers with ten symbols, including zero. This is the system the whole world uses today.

The astronomer Aryabhata, born in 476 CE, calculated the value of pi closely, explained eclipses, and suggested that the Earth spins on its axis. Gupta doctors, metal workers and mathematicians were famous across Asia.

Poetry, temples and Nalanda

The poet Kalidasa, often called the Shakespeare of India, is said to have written his plays and poems at the Gupta court. Temple building in stone grew quickly, and some of the finest early Hindu sculpture dates from this age.

At Nalanda, a great university rose where thousands of students studied logic, medicine, astronomy and religion. Scholars came from China, Korea and Tibet. When the empire weakened after attacks by the Hunas, the golden age faded — but its gifts to the world never did.

Did You Know?

Samudragupta was such a keen musician that his gold coins show the warrior-king relaxing with a veena in his hands.
