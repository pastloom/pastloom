History of Cricket

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Cricket is a bat-and-ball game loved across Britain, South Asia, Australia, the Caribbean and beyond. A single Test match can last five days — and still end without a winner.

It began as a children's game in the fields of south-east England more than 400 years ago. From there it travelled with the British across the seas and grew roots in many lands.

A game from the English fields

Cricket grew up in the farming counties of Kent, Sussex and Surrey. A court case of 1598 mentions schoolboys playing 'creckett' — proof the game was already old by then.

In the 1700s it became the game of gentlemen and gamblers alike. The first known laws were written in 1744, and the Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC), founded in 1787, became the game's guardian at Lord's.

Cricket travels the world

British soldiers, sailors and settlers carried cricket wherever they went. It took deep root in Australia, India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, the West Indies and South Africa — places that soon challenged England itself.

In 1877 England and Australia played the first Test match. When Australia won in London in 1882, a mock newspaper notice mourned the 'death' of English cricket and said its ashes would be taken to Australia — giving the famous Ashes series its name.

New forms, new crowds

For most of its history cricket was a slow game of days. One-day matches arrived in the 1900s to suit modern crowds, and the first World Cup was held in 1975.

In 2003 an even faster form, Twenty20 — over in about three hours — was launched in England. It spread like wildfire, and India's IPL league became one of the richest in world sport. The old game had learned to run.

Did You Know?

The longest cricket match in history — England against South Africa in 1939 — lasted nine playing days and was still drawn, because the England team's ship had to sail home.
