The Universal Declaration of Human Rights

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights is a famous document adopted by the United Nations on 10 December 1948. In thirty short articles, it says that every person on Earth is born free and equal, with basic rights that no government should take away.

It is not a law that police can enforce. Instead, it is a promise and a measuring stick — and it has shaped the laws and constitutions of countries all over the world.

Why it was written

World War II and the Holocaust showed how terribly governments could treat their own people. The founders of the United Nations wanted a clear, shared answer: these are the rights every human being has, simply for being human.

The work began in 1946. A committee of members from many countries and cultures — including China, Lebanon, France, Canada, India and the Soviet Union — argued, drafted and redrafted for two years.

The people who wrote it

The chair was Eleanor Roosevelt, widow of the American president, who kept the strong-minded committee working together. A Canadian law professor, John Humphrey, prepared the first full draft, and French lawyer René Cassin shaped it into clear articles.

Charles Malik of Lebanon and P. C. Chang of China helped make sure the words belonged to the whole world, not to one culture. Hansa Mehta of India successfully pressed for the opening words to say all human beings, not all men.

What it says — and what it changed

The Declaration lists rights such as life, freedom and safety; freedom of thought and speech; a fair trial; education; and freedom from slavery and torture. It also says everyone has duties to their community.

Since 1948 it has inspired the constitutions of many new nations, treaties on refugees, women, children and torture, and the work of courts and campaigners everywhere. The day it was adopted, 10 December, is now Human Rights Day.

Did You Know?

The Declaration holds a world record: it has been translated into more than 500 languages and dialects, from Abkhaz to Zulu — more than any other document on Earth.
