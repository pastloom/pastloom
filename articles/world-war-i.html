World War I

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The First World War (1914–1918) was the first global industrial war. It began with an assassination in Sarajevo and, through a web of alliances, pulled in every great power — killing between 16 and 20 million people and destroying four empires.

It was fought with machine guns, poison gas, tanks and trenches, and it changed everything it touched: maps, empires, women's lives, and the fate of the century. In many ways the modern world begins in 1914.

The road to war

By 1914 Europe's great powers were locked in rival alliances — the Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia) against the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) — armed with new industries, empires to defend and generals' timetables that made war hard to stop once started. On 28 June 1914, in Sarajevo, a Bosnian Serb student, Gavrilo Princip, shot dead the heir to Austria-Hungary's throne, Archduke Franz Ferdinand.

Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia; alliance dragged in Russia, Germany, France and — when Germany invaded Belgium — Britain. Within five weeks Europe was at war, and its empires made the war worldwide. Almost everyone expected it to be over by Christmas.

The trenches

Germany's plan to knock out France quickly failed at the Marne in September 1914. Both sides dug in, and a line of trenches grew from the North Sea to Switzerland — 700 kilometres of mud, barbed wire and machine guns, where attacks gained metres at the cost of thousands of lives. At Verdun and the Somme in 1916, battles lasted months and consumed around a million men each; a generation learned new words: no man's land, shell shock, going over the top.

At sea, Britain blockaded Germany while German U-boats hunted shipping. In the air, aircraft fought the first dogfights. Poison gas, tanks and flamethrowers appeared — industry had learned to mass-produce death as efficiently as goods.

A world at war

The war spread far beyond Europe: fighting in Africa's colonies, Gallipoli and Mesopotamia against the Ottoman Empire, Japan seizing German Pacific colonies, and eventually China and Brazil among the declarers. In 1917 two events transformed it: the United States joined the Allies after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, and Russia — broken by casualties and hunger — fell to revolution, leaving the war in the east to a separate peace.

At home, whole societies mobilised: women ran factories, farms and hospitals; rationing, propaganda and censorship reached into every household. It was the first truly total war.

The end and the unquiet peace

In spring 1918 Germany gambled everything on one last offensive; it failed, and the Allies — now reinforced by American troops — pushed forward in the Hundred Days. Germany's allies collapsed one by one, its sailors mutinied, the Kaiser abdicated, and at 11 a.m. on 11 November 1918 the guns fell silent.

The war killed perhaps 16–20 million people and destroyed four empires — German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian. The Treaty of Versailles (1919) redrew Europe's map but left Germany humiliated and unreconciled, while new nations, old grievances and a shattered economy prepared the ground for a second, greater war twenty years later. The First World War is still called the Great War — the catastrophe that made the modern world.

Did You Know?

On Christmas Day 1914, soldiers along parts of the front left their trenches to sing carols, share food and play football with the enemy — the famous Christmas Truce, never repeated on that scale.
