History of Buddhism

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Buddhism began in northern India about 2,500 years ago, in the life of Siddhartha Gautama, who became known as the Buddha. Historians study how his teaching spread from India across Asia — along trade routes, with the support of kings, and through travelling monks.

Over the centuries it developed into several traditions, including Theravada in Sri Lanka and South-East Asia, and Mahayana in China, Korea and Japan. This article tells that history only — what happened, where and when.

Beginnings in India

Siddhartha Gautama was born, tradition says, at Lumbini in what is now Nepal. After years of searching, he taught at Sarnath and gathered followers — monks, nuns and lay people — who memorised his teaching and carried it from town to town.

For its first centuries Buddhism was one of several movements in India. Its early councils, stupas (dome-shaped shrines) and cave monasteries can still be seen at places such as Sanchi and Ajanta.

Ashoka and the spread across Asia

The Mauryan emperor Ashoka, who ruled much of India in the 200s BCE, supported Buddhist monks and, according to tradition, sent missions abroad — including to Sri Lanka, where Buddhism took lasting root.

From about the 1st century CE, monks and merchants carried Buddhist texts and images along the Silk Road into Central Asia and China, and by sea to Burma, Thailand, Cambodia, Vietnam and the islands of Indonesia. From China it later reached Korea and then Japan, where it arrived in the 500s CE.

Later centuries

In India itself, Buddhism slowly declined after about the 1100s, though it remained strong in the Himalayan regions and in Tibet, where a distinct tradition, Vajrayana, developed with the Dalai Lamas as its best-known leaders.

In the 1800s and 1900s, scholars translated Buddhist texts into European languages, and interest spread to the West. Today there are hundreds of millions of Buddhists worldwide, and historic sites such as Bodh Gaya — where the Buddha is said to have gained enlightenment — draw visitors from every continent.

Did You Know?

The great stupa at Borobudur in Indonesia, built about 800 CE, is the largest Buddhist temple in the world — and was hidden under jungle and ash for centuries before being uncovered in the 1800s.
