Ferdinand Magellan

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Ferdinand Magellan was a Portuguese captain sailing for Spain who set out in 1519 to find a westward route to the spice islands of Asia. His expedition became the first to sail all the way around the world.

Magellan himself did not survive the journey — he was killed in the Philippines. But one of his ships, led by Juan Sebastián Elcano, completed the circle in 1522, proving the Earth's true size.

Five ships into the unknown

Magellan had sailed to Asia for Portugal, going east. He believed a passage through or around South America would let Spain reach the Spice Islands by sailing west — but Portugal refused his plan, so he offered it to Spain.

His fleet of five ships crossed the Atlantic and searched the South American coast through a bitter southern winter, surviving a mutiny on the way. One ship was wrecked and another deserted, sailing home to Spain.

The strait and the peaceful sea

In October 1520 the remaining ships found a twisting channel through the tip of South America — the strait that now bears Magellan's name. It took 38 days to thread through it.

Beyond lay a vast, calm ocean. Magellan named it the Pacific — the peaceful sea. But the crossing took over three months, far longer than anyone expected, and the starving crews ate rats and leather to survive.

Death, and the finishing of the circle

In the Philippines, Magellan became involved in a local conflict and was killed at the Battle of Mactan in April 1521. His expedition sailed on under new leaders, reaching the Spice Islands and loading precious cloves.

Only the ship Victoria, captained by Juan Sebastián Elcano, made it home to Spain in September 1522, with 18 exhausted survivors. Their voyage — about three years long — was the first circumnavigation of the Earth.

Did You Know?

The expedition's chronicler, Antonio Pigafetta, kept a detailed diary that survives today. Of the roughly 270 men who sailed in 1519, only 18 completed the full circle of the Earth.
