The Queen of Sheba and Ancient Arabia

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Queen of Sheba is one of the most famous visitors in ancient storytelling. Traditions in the Bible, the Quran and Ethiopian history tell how she travelled to Jerusalem to meet King Solomon, bringing gold, spices and precious stones.

Behind the stories stands a real land: the kingdom of Saba in southern Arabia, in modern Yemen — a rich state grown wealthy on the trade in frankincense and myrrh.

The story of the visit

In the biblical account, the Queen of Sheba heard of Solomon's wisdom and came to test him with hard questions. She arrived with a great caravan of camels loaded with spices, gold and jewels.

Solomon answered all her questions, and the queen, amazed by his palace and court, gave him rich gifts before returning home. The Quran tells a version of the same visit, and Ethiopian tradition names her Makeda.

Saba, the real kingdom

Historians cannot prove the queen's visit, but her homeland was real. The Sabaeans of Yemen built dams, temples and cities, and their great dam at Marib watered farms in the desert.

Saba's merchants controlled the start of the incense route. Their frankincense and myrrh travelled by camel to Petra, Gaza and Damascus, making southern Arabia famous across the ancient world.

Why she is still remembered

In Ethiopia, tradition says Makeda's son by Solomon, Menelik I, founded the line of Ethiopian kings — a story treasured there for centuries.

Whether every detail happened or not, the queen's story shows something true: Arabia's incense wealth was so famous that storytellers from Jerusalem to Ethiopia wove it into their most treasured tales.

Did You Know?

The Sabaeans' Great Dam of Marib was one of the wonders of ancient engineering — it watered about 100 square kilometres of farmland in the desert, and parts of it stood for over a thousand years.
