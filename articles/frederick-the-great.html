Frederick the Great

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Frederick the Great was king of Prussia, the German state he turned into one of the great powers of Europe. He was a brilliant general who survived the Seven Years' War against three empires at once.

He was also a musician and philosopher-king who played the flute, wrote books in French, and called himself 'the first servant of the state.'

A prince who loved books and music

Frederick grew up under a harsh father, the soldier-king Frederick William I, who despised his son's love of music, poetry and French philosophy. At eighteen Frederick tried to flee to England; he was caught, and forced to watch the execution of the friend who had helped him.

The experience hardened him. Outwardly he became the obedient soldier his father wanted — while privately studying war, government and the ideas of the Enlightenment.

The warrior king

In 1740, the year he became king, Frederick seized the rich province of Silesia from Austria and held it through two wars. Then, in the Seven Years' War, Prussia faced Austria, France and Russia together.

Outnumbered, Frederick marched his superbly drilled army from one frontier to another, winning famous victories like Rossbach and Leuthen in 1757. Prussia survived — exhausted, but confirmed as a great power.

The first servant of the state

In peacetime Frederick worked punishing hours on government. He reformed the courts, limited torture, allowed unusual religious tolerance, drained marshes for farms and promoted the humble potato as food against famine.

He corresponded with the philosopher Voltaire and wrote that a ruler is not the master of the state but its first servant. His Prussia became a model of efficient government — and of militarism.

Did You Know?

A famous story says Frederick made potatoes popular by planting them in royal fields and posting guards — so that people would think the crop precious, steal it, and plant it themselves.
