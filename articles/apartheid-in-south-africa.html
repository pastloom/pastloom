Apartheid in South Africa

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Apartheid was the system of strict racial separation and discrimination that ruled South Africa from 1948 to 1994. Under apartheid, the white minority government controlled the lives of the Black majority by law.

After decades of resistance led by people like Nelson Mandela, apartheid ended, and in 1994 all South Africans voted together for the first time.

A system of separation

Segregation existed in South Africa before 1948, but the National Party's election victory that year made it a complete legal system. Laws classified every person by race and fixed where they could live, work, study and travel.

Black South Africans were forced into poor 'homelands' and townships and had to carry pass books to enter white areas. Mixed marriages were banned, and the best land, schools and jobs were reserved for whites.

Resistance grows

The ANC, founded in 1912, led campaigns of protest. In 1960, police shot dead 69 peaceful protesters at Sharpeville, shocking the world. The government banned the ANC, and Mandela was jailed in 1962, spending 27 years in prison.

In 1976, school students in Soweto rose against being taught in Afrikaans; hundreds were killed. Strikes, protests and international boycotts and sanctions pressed the government harder each year, while Mandela, still in prison, became a worldwide symbol of the struggle.

Freedom at last

Facing unrest at home and isolation abroad, President F. W. de Klerk lifted the ban on the ANC and freed Mandela in February 1990. Talks followed, often tense, toward a new constitution.

In April 1994, millions queued for hours to vote in South Africa's first election open to all races. The ANC won, and Nelson Mandela became president — choosing reconciliation, through efforts like the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, over revenge.

Did You Know?

Nelson Mandela's prison number on Robben Island was 46664 — years later, the '46664' name was used for a global campaign against HIV and AIDS in his honour.
