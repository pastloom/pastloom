The Siege of Leningrad

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Siege of Leningrad was one of the longest and deadliest sieges in history. From September 1941 to January 1944 — about 872 days — German and Finnish forces surrounded the Soviet city of Leningrad, cutting off almost all food and fuel.

Around a million civilians died, most of them from hunger and cold. Yet the city never surrendered. Leningrad's suffering and endurance became one of the most powerful stories of the Second World War.

The ring closes

Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, and by September its armies had cut Leningrad off from the rest of the country. Hitler decided the city would not be stormed but starved: no food was to reach its two and a half million people.

Artillery shelled the streets daily, and bombers targeted food warehouses. As winter approached, the city's stores were almost empty.

The hungry winter

The winter of 1941–42 was one of the coldest on record. With no fuel, no light and almost no food, the bread ration for many citizens fell to 125 grams a day — a slice made partly of sawdust. People ate wallpaper paste, leather and glue, and tens of thousands died each month.

Yet the city worked and even created. Factories repaired tanks within sound of the guns, and in August 1942 an orchestra of half-starved musicians performed Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony in the besieged city, broadcast by loudspeaker to the German lines.

The Road of Life and liberation

Across the ice of Lake Ladoga, trucks of the 'Road of Life' carried food in and children out, driving through the dark under shellfire. The road saved hundreds of thousands of lives, though it could never bring enough.

In January 1943 Soviet troops forced a narrow corridor into the city, and on 27 January 1944 the siege was lifted at last. Leningrad had endured 872 days. It was later named a Hero City of the Soviet Union.
