The Hunt for the Lost City of Z

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Colonel Percy Fawcett was one of Britain's most experienced explorers of the Amazon jungle. He became convinced that the ruins of a great ancient city — which he called simply 'Z' — lay hidden in the forests of Brazil.

In 1925 Fawcett walked into the jungle with his son Jack and one friend to find Z. None of them was ever seen again. Their disappearance became one of exploration's greatest mysteries — and modern archaeology has shown that Fawcett may not have been entirely wrong.

The mapmaker who dreamed of Z

Fawcett spent years surveying jungle frontiers for the Royal Geographical Society, living off the land and earning a reputation for toughness. In Brazil's archives he found a Portuguese manuscript from 1753 describing a ruined stone city deep in the interior, and local peoples showed him pottery and earthworks where scholars insisted only small tribes had ever lived.

Most experts of his day believed the Amazon could support nothing more. Fawcett disagreed — and gave his imagined city a single letter: Z.

Into the jungle, 1925

Determined to travel light, Fawcett took only his son Jack and Jack's friend Raleigh Rimmel. They rode and walked east from Cuiabá, sending letters home with returning guides. The last, from a place he called Dead Horse Camp, was dated 29 May 1925. After that, nothing certain was ever heard of them.

Fawcett had left strict instructions that if he failed to return, no rescue should be attempted — the risk was too great. The world ignored him: expedition after expedition went in, and by some counts around a hundred would-be rescuers died in the search. Reported bones, captives' tales and sightings all dissolved on checking.

Was Z real after all?

For decades Fawcett was remembered as a dreamer who died chasing a fantasy. Then, from the 1990s, archaeologist Michael Heckenberger and others excavated the Upper Xingu region of Brazil and found something remarkable: large, planned settlements from before Columbus — towns with plazas, roads, bridges and moats, linked in clusters of 'garden cities'.

Modern laser scanning from aircraft keeps revealing more earthworks under the forest. There was no single stone city called Z, and Fawcett's fate is still unknown. But his central belief — that the Amazon once held real cities — has been proved right.

Did You Know?

Fawcett told his family that if he did not return, no one should come looking for him. Dozens of searchers disobeyed — and the hunt for the lost explorer became almost as deadly as the expedition itself.
