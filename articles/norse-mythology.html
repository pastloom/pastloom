Norse Mythology

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Norse mythology is the collection of stories told by the Norse people of Scandinavia — the world of the Vikings. Its best-known figures are Odin, the one-eyed chief of the gods, Thor with his hammer, and the trickster Loki.

Most of what we know comes from poems and prose written down in Iceland in the 1200s, hundreds of years after the stories were first told aloud around winter fires.

Odin, Thor and the world tree

In the Norse stories, the gods lived in Asgard, joined to the human world, Midgard, by a rainbow bridge. A giant ash tree, Yggdrasil, was said to hold the whole universe together in its branches and roots.

Odin was pictured as a wise, one-eyed wanderer who sought knowledge everywhere. Thor, his son, protected gods and humans with his hammer, and his storms explained thunder. Loki, neither fully god nor giant, caused trouble in story after story.

How the stories survived

The Norse told their myths in poems that skalds — court poets — memorised and performed. After Scandinavia became Christian, around the years 1000 to 1100, the old poems might have been lost.

In Iceland, the writer Snorri Sturluson set the stories down in his Prose Edda, about 1220, so that poets could still understand the old verses. Medieval Icelanders also copied the older poems of the Poetic Edda. These books are our main source for Norse mythology today.

The end of the world — and a new beginning

Norse stories are unusual because the gods themselves were said to face an end: at Ragnarök, gods and giants would fight a last battle, and fire and flood would destroy the world.

Yet the story does not stop there — a new, green world was said to rise from the sea, with a few surviving gods and a new human pair. Writers and artists still retell these tales today, and the English names of weekdays — Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday and Friday — come from Norse gods: Tyr, Odin (Woden), Thor and Frigg.

Did You Know?

Wednesday means 'Woden's day' — Woden was the English form of Odin's name, and Thursday is Thor's day.
