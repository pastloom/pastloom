Ancient Greece

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Ancient Greece was a world of rival city-states — Athens, Sparta, Corinth and a thousand others — that between about 800 and 146 BCE created an astonishing share of the ideas the world still lives by: democracy, philosophy, theatre, history-writing and the Olympic Games.

Greece was small, poor in farmland and always at war with itself. Yet its thinkers asked the biggest questions ever asked — what is justice, what is the universe made of, how should people govern themselves — and we are still answering them.

City-states on a rocky land

Ancient Greece was never one country. Mountains split the land into valleys and islands, and each valley grew its own polis — a city-state with its own government, army and pride. There were more than a thousand of them, from Athens and Sparta to tiny island towns, and Greeks planted colonies from Spain to the Black Sea.

They shared a language, gods, festivals and the Olympic Games — yet fought each other constantly. This mix of rivalry and shared culture made Greece small in size and enormous in influence: competition between cities drove experiments in politics, art and thought.

Athens invents democracy

Athens, the largest city-state, tried something new. Through reforms by Solon (594 BCE) and Cleisthenes (508 BCE), ordinary male citizens gained the right to vote in an assembly, serve on juries and hold office — the world's first democracy, direct and noisy. It excluded women, slaves and foreigners, but the idea that citizens should rule themselves was born here.

Athens also led the Greek defence against the Persian Empire: at Marathon (490 BCE) and Salamis (480 BCE) the Greeks defeated a vastly larger foe — victories that saved their experiment and filled Athens with confidence and silver.

Sparta, war and the golden age

Athens' great rival was Sparta, a militarised state where boys were taken at seven for brutal training and society existed for war. In the 400s BCE Athens built an empire of its own from the anti-Persian league — and the two collided in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE), a generation-long catastrophe that Sparta won and Greece lost.

Yet this same century was Athens' golden age: the Parthenon rose on the Acropolis; Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides invented drama as we know it; Herodotus and Thucydides invented history-writing; and Socrates questioned everything in the marketplace, teaching Plato, who taught Aristotle — the founding chain of Western philosophy.

Alexander and the Greek world

Exhausted Greece fell in 338 BCE to Philip of Macedon — whose son, Alexander the Great, then carried Greek language and culture across an empire from Egypt to India in just twelve years. After Alexander's death in 323 BCE, his generals' kingdoms — the Hellenistic world — spread Greek cities, science and art from Alexandria's great library to Afghanistan.

Rome conquered Greece by 146 BCE but, as the poet Horace said, captive Greece captured its conqueror: Roman education, gods and architecture all drank from Greek springs. Through Rome, and later through Arab scholars who preserved Greek science, the Greek legacy — democracy, geometry, logic, theatre, the Olympics — flows into our world still.

Did You Know?

The Olympic Games began in Greece in 776 BCE and ran for nearly 12 centuries — and Greek is the oldest recorded European language still spoken today.
