The Kingdom of Merina

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Kingdom of Merina was a kingdom in the highlands of Madagascar, the great island off Africa's south-east coast. In the 1800s its kings and queens united almost the whole island under their rule.

From their capital, Antananarivo, Merina rulers built an organised state with schools, industries and an army — until France conquered Madagascar in 1896.

One island, one kingdom

The Merina people farmed rice in terraced fields in Madagascar's central highlands. Around 1800, King Andrianampoinimerina united the Merina and declared his ambition: 'the sea is the boundary of my rice field' — meaning the whole island.

His son Radama I, with British help, trained a modern army and conquered most of Madagascar. Missionaries opened schools and gave the Malagasy language a written form.

Queen Ranavalona and independence

After Radama's death, Queen Ranavalona I ruled from 1828 to 1861. She feared foreign control and pushed out most Europeans, while building local industries — soap, cloth, metalwork and even guns.

Her reign was harsh, and many suffered under forced labour and strict laws. But Madagascar stayed independent while most of Africa was being colonised.

Conquest and memory

Later Merina rulers reopened Madagascar to the world, but France pressed harder. In 1895 French troops took Antananarivo, and in 1896 Madagascar became a French colony; the last queen, Ranavalona III, was exiled.

The Merina kingdom's capital, language and culture still shape Madagascar today — Antananarivo remains the island's capital and largest city.

Did You Know?

King Andrianampoinimerina organised his kingdom like his famous saying about a rice field — he even arranged his twelve wives' homes on twelve sacred hills around the capital.
