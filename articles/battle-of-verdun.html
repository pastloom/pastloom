The Battle of Verdun

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Battle of Verdun was the longest battle of the First World War — and one of the longest in all history. For about ten months in 1916, French and German armies fought over a ring of forts around the French town of Verdun.

More than 700,000 men were killed or wounded. Verdun became the great symbol of French courage and of the terrible cost of the war: the battle where France's soldiers vowed, 'They shall not pass.'

The German plan

The German commander, Erich von Falkenhayn, chose Verdun because he knew France would defend it at any price — its forts guarded the road to Paris, and its name carried centuries of history. His aim was not ground but men: to draw the French army into a trap of artillery fire and, in his cold phrase, bleed it white.

The attack began on 21 February 1916 with one of the heaviest bombardments yet seen. Within days the great Fort Douaumont — left almost undefended — fell to a handful of German soldiers.

The Sacred Road

General Pétain took command and organised the defence around one lifeline: a single road from Bar-le-Duc, soon named the Voie Sacrée — the Sacred Road. Trucks passed along it day and night, at times one every few seconds, carrying men, food and shells.

Pétain also rotated his troops quickly, so that fresh units constantly replaced exhausted ones. In the end, most of the French army passed through the hell of Verdun — and held it.

Ten months of shellfire

The fighting swung back and forth over hills with names like Mort-Homme — Dead Man's Hill. Millions of shells ploughed the ground into a grey moonscape where villages simply ceased to exist.

In October the French retook Douaumont, and when the battle ended in December the lines stood close to where they had begun. At the battlefield today, the Douaumont Ossuary holds the remains of about 130,000 unknown soldiers, French and German together.

Did You Know?

The Douaumont Ossuary at Verdun holds the remains of about 130,000 unknown soldiers — French and German together — whose bodies were never identified.
