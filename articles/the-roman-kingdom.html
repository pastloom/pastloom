The Roman Kingdom

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Rome began as a kingdom. Legend says Romulus founded the city in 753 BCE and became its first king; six more kings followed, the last of them from the Etruscan people.

In 509 BCE the Romans drove out their last king, Tarquin the Proud, and swore never to be ruled by a king again. They built a republic instead — but the kingdom's 244 years shaped Rome's religion, laws and city forever.

Romulus and the first kings

Legend tells that Romulus founded Rome on the Palatine Hill in 753 BCE and ruled as its first king, organising its first senate and army. The kings who followed, the story says, each added something: Numa Pompilius gave Rome its religion and calendar, others its wars, markets and harbour.

Historians cannot prove these stories, but archaeology shows real settlements on Rome's hills in those centuries, growing from villages of huts into a true city.

The Etruscan kings

The last three kings came from the Etruscans, Rome's powerful northern neighbours. Under them Rome became a proper city, with the great temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, the Forum drained and paved, and a huge drain, the Cloaca Maxima, that still works today.

King Servius Tullius was said to have organised the people into voting groups and built a great wall around the growing city.

The fall of the kings

Tradition says the last king, Tarquin the Proud, ruled cruelly, and after a crime by his son the Romans rose up and expelled the royal family in 509 BCE.

Rome then became a republic, led by two elected consuls instead of one king. The Romans hated the word 'king' for the rest of their history — centuries later, Julius Caesar was murdered partly because people feared he wanted a crown.

Did You Know?

Rome's great drain, the Cloaca Maxima, was built under the kings about 2,600 years ago — and parts of it still carry water under the modern city today.
