Athens Against Sparta

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

For 27 years, from 431 to 404 BCE, the two strongest Greek city-states tore the Greek world apart: Athens, rich and powerful at sea, against Sparta, the greatest land army in Greece. Historians call their struggle the Peloponnesian War.

This article follows the war as a story of the two rivals — how Athens' empire and pride, and Sparta's fear of it, led to plague, disaster in Sicily, and the fall of Athens itself. A general who fought in it, Thucydides, wrote its famous history.

Why Greece went to war with itself

After the Persian Wars, Athens grew rich and powerful through its league of allies, and other Greeks began to fear it. Sparta, leader of its own league, saw Athens as a threat that had to be cut down.

Small quarrels between their allies lit the fuse. In 431 BCE Sparta invaded the farmland around Athens, and the long war began. Athens, safe behind its walls, used its navy to raid the coasts.

Plague, and the Sicilian disaster

In 430 BCE a terrible plague struck crowded Athens, killing perhaps a quarter of its people — including its great leader Pericles. The war dragged on with battles, truces and betrayals for years.

In 415 BCE Athens gambled everything on conquering Sicily, far to the west. The expedition ended in total disaster at Syracuse: the fleet was destroyed and thousands of Athenian soldiers died or were enslaved. Athens never recovered its strength.

The fall of Athens

Sparta, now helped by Persian money, built a fleet of its own. In 405 BCE the Spartan commander Lysander destroyed the last Athenian navy at Aegospotami and cut off Athens' food supply.

Starving, Athens surrendered in 404 BCE. Its walls were pulled down to the music of flutes. The war had exhausted all Greece — and within two generations, the Greek cities fell under the control of Macedon.

Did You Know?

Thucydides, our main source for the war, caught the Athenian plague and survived — his description of the disease is so detailed that doctors still argue today about which illness it was.
