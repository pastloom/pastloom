The Australian Gold Rushes

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 1851, gold was found in Australia — first in New South Wales, then in rich fields in Victoria. Within months, ships full of hopeful diggers were sailing from Britain, America, China and Europe.

In ten years Australia's population tripled. The gold rushes built great cities like Melbourne, paid for railways and schools, and pushed Australia towards democracy — but they also brought violence and pushed Aboriginal peoples further from their land.

Gold! The rush begins

Australia had small gold finds before, but officials kept them quiet, fearing disorder. In February 1851 Edward Hargraves found gold near Bathurst and announced it loudly, hoping for a reward. The rush was on.

Months later came far richer finds in the new colony of Victoria, at Ballarat and Bendigo. Victoria's goldfields became among the richest in the world, and Melbourne boomed into a grand city of banks, theatres and wide streets.

Life on the diggings

Diggers lived in tents and mud huts, digging shafts and washing gravel in cradles and pans. A few struck it rich; most found little. Stores, hotels and newspapers followed the gold, and towns rose in months.

About 40,000 Chinese miners came, bringing skill and hard work, but faced racist taxes and violence — at Lambing Flat in 1861 riots attacked Chinese camps. Such racism later fed the 'White Australia' policy. For Aboriginal peoples, the rushes meant more settlers, fences and disease on their lands.

What gold built

Gold money paid for railways, telegraphs, universities and public buildings across the colonies. When surface gold ran out, companies dug deep mines, and towns became permanent cities.

The rushes also changed politics: miners demanded fairness at Eureka in 1854 and soon won the vote. The energy and wealth of the gold age helped unite the colonies into the nation of Australia in 1901.

Did You Know?

The largest gold nugget ever found, the 'Welcome Stranger', was dug up in Victoria in 1869. It weighed about 72 kilograms — and was so big it had to be broken up on a blacksmith's anvil to be weighed.
