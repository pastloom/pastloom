History of Law and Courts

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Laws are the written rules that hold a society together, and courts are the places where judges decide whether the rules were broken. The oldest law codes we know were carved in stone in Mesopotamia almost 4,000 years ago.

From Babylon's stone pillar to Roman law books, English juries and today's international courts, this article follows how law and courts grew — and how the idea of a fair trial slowly developed.

The first written laws

The earliest law codes come from Mesopotamia. The code of Ur-Nammu (about 2100 BCE) set fines for injuries; the later code of Hammurabi, carved on a tall stone pillar for all to see, listed about 282 laws on trade, property, family and punishments.

Written law was a big step: rules were no longer only what a ruler said on the day. In Greece, lawgivers such as Solon wrote laws for Athens, and Rome built a legal system of courts, advocates and written opinions that grew more careful over centuries.

Roman law and medieval courts

In the 500s CE, the emperor Justinian ordered all Roman law to be gathered and organised into great books. Rediscovered in medieval Italy, Roman law became the foundation of legal systems across Europe — the 'civil law' tradition still used in many countries today.

In England a different path grew: royal judges travelled the country, and their decisions built up 'common law'. Trial by jury — ordinary people deciding the facts — developed there in the Middle Ages, and ideas such as 'innocent until proved guilty' slowly took hold.

Law in the modern world

From the 1700s, reformers argued for clear written codes, fair public trials and an end to cruel punishments. Napoleon's code of 1804 spread the civil-law model widely, and constitutions in many countries set limits that even governments must obey.

In the 1900s, law went international: the Nuremberg trials after the Second World War judged crimes against humanity, and world courts were founded at The Hague. Today courts — local, national and international — remain the places where societies argue, peacefully and by rules, about right and wrong.

Did You Know?

Hammurabi's stone pillar, carved about 1750 BCE, still stands today — you can see it in the Louvre Museum in Paris.
