History of Uniforms

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A uniform is a set of matching clothes that shows a person belongs to a group — an army, a school, a hospital or a team. Uniforms help people recognise each other quickly and feel part of one body.

Soldiers were the first to wear true uniforms. From armies, the idea spread to police officers, nurses, sailors, schoolchildren and sports teams all over the world.

Armies learn to dress alike

Ancient armies used matching shields and colours so soldiers could find their own side in battle. Roman legionaries wore standard armour, helmets and red cloaks supplied by the state.

In the 1600s, Europe's growing national armies began dressing whole regiments in one colour with matching coats and hats. A soldier could be recognised — and deserters could be spotted — at a glance.

From bright colours to camouflage

Famous uniforms became national symbols: the British redcoat, the blue coats of American and French soldiers, and the bright dress uniforms still worn at royal ceremonies today.

But bright colours were dangerous once rifles became accurate. In the late 1800s armies switched to dull khaki — a word from Urdu and Persian meaning 'dust-coloured' — and in the 1900s to camouflage patterns that copy leaves, sand and snow.

Uniforms in everyday life

Police forces, founded in the 1800s, wore uniforms so citizens could find help quickly. Nurses adopted caps and dresses, railway staff wore caps and buttons, and postmen and firefighters followed.

Schools took the idea from charity schools, where uniform dress made poor children equal in appearance. Today school uniforms are worn in many countries, and sports teams wear matching colours for the same ancient reason as soldiers: to know your own side.

Did You Know?

Khaki, the dull dust-colour of modern uniforms, was first used by British soldiers in India in the 1840s — some dyed their white uniforms with tea, mud and curry powder to blend in.
