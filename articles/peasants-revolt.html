The Peasants' Revolt

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Peasants' Revolt of 1381 was the biggest uprising of ordinary people in medieval England. Angry about a new tax and unfair laws, thousands of peasants marched on London.

Their leader was a man called Wat Tyler. The young King Richard II, only fourteen years old, met the rebels face to face — but the revolt ended in violence, and its leaders were killed.

Why the peasants rose

After the Black Death killed so many workers, labourers hoped for better wages. Instead, laws froze their pay, and the government charged a poll tax three times.

In 1381, tax collectors met angry resistance in Essex and Kent. Villagers joined together, freed prisoners, and marched towards London demanding fair treatment.

The march on London

The rebels entered London, burned the palace of the king's unpopular uncle, and executed some royal officials. The city was in shock.

King Richard met the rebels and seemed to agree to their demands. But at a second meeting, Wat Tyler was wounded by the Mayor of London and died. Leaderless, the crowds went home.

What happened afterwards

The king took back his promises, and rebel leaders across England were hunted down and executed. The revolt had failed.

Yet the poll tax was abandoned, and lords grew more careful. Historians still see 1381 as the moment England's poorest people first spoke with one loud voice.

Did You Know?

The priest John Ball preached a famous question: 'When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?' — asking why some men should rule others.
