History of Mountaineering

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Mountaineering is the sport and art of climbing mountains. For most of history, people climbed only when they had to — to hunt, to cross passes, or to pray at holy peaks.

In the last 250 years, climbing became something people did for the challenge itself. Ropes, ice axes and brave men and women opened the way from the Alps to the highest summits on Earth.

Climbing for science and wonder

In the 1700s, scientists began climbing mountains to study weather, air and plants. In 1786 two Frenchmen, Michel-Gabriel Paccard and Jacques Balmat, reached the top of Mont Blanc, the highest peak in the Alps.

Their climb is often called the birth of mountaineering. Soon guides in Alpine villages made a living leading visitors up peaks, and climbing clubs were founded — the Alpine Club in London in 1857 was the first.

The golden age of the Alps

In the 1850s and 1860s, climbers raced to make the first ascent of every great Alpine summit. British climbers with Swiss and French guides climbed peak after peak in what is called the golden age of alpinism.

The age ended in drama in 1865, when Edward Whymper's team first climbed the Matterhorn — and four of the seven men fell to their deaths on the way down. The accident shocked Europe and showed the mountain's price.

To the highest places on Earth

Climbers then turned to the greater ranges: the Andes, the Rockies, Africa's Kilimanjaro and Rwenzori, and above all the Himalayas and Karakoram, where all fourteen of the world's 8,000-metre peaks stand.

Everest fell in 1953, and by 1964 all fourteen giants had been climbed. Later climbers sought harder routes, winter ascents and climbs without bottled oxygen — proving that the challenge of the high mountains has never ended.

Did You Know?

When Lucy Walker climbed the Matterhorn in 1871, Victorian rules of dress meant she climbed in a long skirt — and she became the first woman to stand on its famous summit.
