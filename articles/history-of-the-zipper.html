History of the Zipper

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The zipper — also called a zip — fastens clothes and bags with two rows of tiny teeth that lock together when a slider passes over them. We use it in seconds, without thinking.

But the zipper took more than forty years of failed inventions before it worked properly. Only in the 1920s and 1930s did it finally conquer clothing.

The first attempts

Elias Howe, famous for the sewing machine, patented an early automatic fastener in 1851 but never sold it. In 1893, the American inventor Whitcomb Judson presented his 'clasp locker' for high-top boots at the Chicago World's Fair.

Judson's fastener worked — sometimes. It jammed, burst open and embarrassed its wearers, and his company struggled for years to make it reliable.

Sundback makes it work

The breakthrough came from Gideon Sundback, a Swedish-born engineer working in America. He redesigned the fastener completely, with rows of small metal teeth that a slider locked and unlocked smoothly, patenting his improved 'hookless fastener' in 1913 and refining it over the following years.

The First World War gave the new fastener work to do: it appeared on money belts, tobacco pouches and flying suits, where it proved itself in hard use.

The zip conquers clothing

In 1923, the B. F. Goodrich company put the fastener on rubber boots and called it the 'Zipper', after the sound it made. The name stuck — and so did the invention.

In the 1930s, zips appeared on children's clothes, so small hands could dress themselves, and fashion designers like Elsa Schiaparelli put them on elegant dresses. In 1937, zips won the 'battle of the fly' and entered men's trousers. The slow, unreliable fastener of 1893 had become part of almost every garment in the world.

Did You Know?

The word 'zipper' was invented as a brand name in 1923, for rubber boots that closed with a 'zip!' — the name was so good it replaced the invention's real name.
