History of Farming

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Farming is the growing of crops and the keeping of animals for food. It began independently in several parts of the world about 10,000 to 12,000 years ago, as the last ice age ended and the climate grew warmer.

Farming changed human life more than almost any other invention. It gave people settled homes, stored food and growing populations — and, in time, villages, cities and civilisations.

The first farmers

In the Fertile Crescent of the Middle East, people began planting wheat and barley and taming wild goats and sheep. In China, farmers grew rice and millet; in Mexico, maize; in the Andes, potatoes.

Farming spread slowly — carried by travelling farmers and by neighbours learning the skills. Where farming took hold, camps became villages, and villages grew into the first towns.

Ploughs, water and empires

The plough, pulled first by people and then by oxen, let farmers work far more land. Irrigation canals in Egypt and Mesopotamia carried river water to dry fields, and their harvests fed priests, builders and kings.

New tools and methods kept raising harvests: iron ploughshares, horse collars, windmills and crop rotation in medieval times, and new crops from the Americas after 1492.

Farming in the modern age

In the 1800s and 1900s came machines — tractors, harvesters and chemical fertilisers — and in the 1900s new high-yield seeds of the Green Revolution saved many lands from famine.

Today only a small share of people work as farmers, yet they feed about eight billion. The field where a Neolithic farmer first scattered seeds has become the foundation of the whole modern world.

Did You Know?

Almost half of all the food energy eaten by humans today comes from just three farm grasses: wheat, rice and maize.
