Fidel Castro

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Fidel Castro was the Cuban revolutionary who overthrew a dictator in 1959 and then ruled Cuba for nearly fifty years — one of the longest rules of any leader in modern times.

His small island country stood at the centre of world events in 1962, when the Cuban Missile Crisis brought the United States and the Soviet Union closer to nuclear war than ever before or since.

The lawyer who became a rebel

Castro trained as a lawyer and entered politics to fight corruption. When the general Fulgencio Batista seized power in 1952, Castro chose armed revolt. His first attack, on the Moncada barracks in 1953, failed, and he was sent to prison.

Released in an amnesty, he went to Mexico, gathered a small band of followers — including the Argentine doctor Che Guevara — and sailed back to Cuba on the yacht Granma in 1956. From the Sierra Maestra mountains, his guerrillas slowly won the country.

Revolution in power

On 1 January 1959 Batista fled, and Castro entered Havana in triumph. His government built schools and clinics and gave land to the poor — and also silenced opponents, took over businesses and drew Cuba close to the Soviet Union.

The United States, only 145 kilometres away, imposed a trade embargo that still shapes Cuba today. In 1961, Cuban exiles backed by America landed at the Bay of Pigs and were defeated in three days.

The Missile Crisis and later years

In October 1962, American spy planes found Soviet nuclear missiles being installed in Cuba. For thirteen days the world waited on the edge of nuclear war, until the Soviet Union agreed to remove the missiles.

Castro stayed in power through the Cold War and long after it, handing over to his brother Raul in 2008 because of illness. He remains loved by supporters as the man who stood up to America, and condemned by critics for allowing no free elections or free press.

Did You Know?

Castro once spoke at the United Nations for 4 hours and 29 minutes — still one of the longest speeches in the UN's history.
