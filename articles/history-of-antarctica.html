History of Antarctica

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Antarctica is the coldest, driest, windiest continent on Earth — and the last to be found. For centuries mapmakers drew a great southern land they had never seen, and called it Terra Australis, the Southern Land.

Sailors first sighted the real Antarctica only in 1820. Since then it has been a land of explorers, heroes and scientists — a continent with no countries and no permanent people, protected by a treaty that keeps it a place of peace and science.

The land on the maps that nobody had seen

Ancient Greek thinkers reasoned that the world needed a great southern land to 'balance' the north, and mapmakers drew it for centuries. Captain James Cook crossed the Antarctic Circle in 1773 and sailed around the continent's icy fringe, finding only icebergs and seals — no land he could reach.

Then, in a single year, 1820, three expeditions — Fabian von Bellingshausen's Russian ships, Edward Bransfield's British brig and Nathaniel Palmer's American sealer — reported sightings of the mainland. The imagined continent was real.

Sealers, explorers and the Heroic Age

Seal and whale hunters came first, then national expeditions planted flags and mapped coasts. The years 1895–1922 are called the Heroic Age: small teams in wooden ships endured winters of total darkness to push inland.

Its climax was the race to the South Pole. Amundsen's Norwegians, travelling light with dogs and skis, planted their flag on 14 December 1911. Captain Scott's British team reached the pole 34 days later, found the Norwegian tent, and perished on the march back. A few years later Ernest Shackleton's ship Endurance was crushed by ice — and he brought every one of his men home alive.

A continent for science

After the Second World War, rival claims to Antarctic territory threatened conflict. Instead, during the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58, nations cooperated in science there — and in 1959 twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty, banning military activity and setting the continent aside for research.

Today dozens of stations study ice cores that hold Earth's climate history, watch the ozone hole first spotted over Antarctica in 1985, and observe stars and space through the clearest skies on the planet.

Did You Know?

Antarctica holds about 70 percent of the world's fresh water, locked up in ice — yet parts of it are a desert: in the Dry Valleys, scientists believe no rain has fallen for nearly two million years.
