History of Storytelling

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Storytelling is humanity's oldest art. Long before writing, people gathered around fires to hear tales of heroes, gods, animals and ancestors — and remembered them word for word for thousands of years.

Stories taught children how to live, carried a people's history, and entertained everyone. Writing, printing, radio and film all grew out of that first circle around the fire.

Stories before writing

Around campfires, early humans turned hunts, storms and dreams into stories. Painting on cave walls — running animals, handprints, hunts — was one way to fix a tale so others could 'read' it.

Skilled storytellers used rhythm, rhyme and repeated phrases to remember enormous tales. The Greek epics of Homer and the great Indian epics were carried this way, voice to voice, for centuries.

Bards, griots and wandering tellers

Many societies honoured professional storytellers: Celtic bards, Norse skalds, Arabian hakawati in coffee houses, and West African griots, who still memorise genealogies and histories stretching back 700 years.

Stories travelled the Silk Road in the mouths of merchants and monks. The tales of Sinbad, and stories that later filled the Arabian Nights, passed through many lands and languages before anyone wrote them down.

From the fire to the screen

Writing froze stories into books, printing made them cheap, and radio brought the speaking voice back into homes — grandparents of the 1930s listened to radio plays much as their ancestors listened at fires.

Film, television and now podcasts and audiobooks all descend from oral storytelling. Technology changed the fire into a screen, but the oldest pleasure — 'tell me a story' — has never changed.

Did You Know?

A trained West African griot can recite family histories and king lists covering more than 700 years — entirely from memory, as his ancestors did.
