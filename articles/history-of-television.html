History of Television

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Television brings moving pictures into the home. Inventors dreamed of it for decades, and in the 1920s and 1930s the first working televisions flickered to life — small, fuzzy and black-and-white.

After the Second World War, television spread with astonishing speed. It changed news, entertainment, politics and family life more than almost any other invention.

The race to see at a distance

In the 1920s, Scotland's John Logie Baird showed a mechanical television, while in America Philo Farnsworth — a farm boy who dreamed up the idea as a teenager — built an all-electronic system in 1927.

Electronic television won. The BBC began the world's first regular broadcasts in 1936, and stations in America and Germany followed, though sets were rare luxuries with tiny screens — at first only a few hundred people could watch.

Television takes over the home

After the Second World War, factories turned to making televisions, prices fell, and aerials sprouted on rooftops everywhere. In 1953, about 20 million people in Britain watched the coronation of Queen Elizabeth II on TV — many on a neighbour's set, bought specially for the day.

Television carried history into living rooms: coronations, wars, Olympic Games and the first Moon landing in 1969 were watched as they happened by hundreds of millions, and families planned their evenings around favourite programmes.

From three channels to endless choice

Colour, remote controls, video recorders, satellite and cable multiplied what people could watch. Flat screens in the 2000s made sets thin and huge at the same time.

In the 2010s, internet streaming let viewers choose anything, any time, on televisions, computers and phones. The glowing box Baird demonstrated has become a window that never closes.

Did You Know?

Philo Farnsworth got the idea for electronic television at age 14 while ploughing a potato field — the neat rows of the field showed him how an image could be scanned line by line.
