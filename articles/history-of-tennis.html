History of Tennis

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Tennis is played with rackets and a ball over a net, by two players or two pairs. Its biggest stage, Wimbledon, is the oldest tennis tournament in the world.

The game began indoors in medieval France, where players hit the ball with their hands. It took centuries — and a move outdoors onto grass — to become the tennis we watch today.

A game of kings indoors

In the 1100s and 1200s, French monks played a game in monastery courtyards, striking a ball with their palms. It spread to the royal courts of France and England, where kings played 'real tennis' in specially built indoor halls.

Gloves, then rackets with strings, replaced the bare hand. The strange scoring — 15, 30, 40, game — and the word 'love' for zero both come down from those old French courts.

Out onto the lawn

In 1874, Major Walter Wingfield patented a portable game he called lawn tennis, played outside on grass with an hourglass-shaped court. Clubs across Britain took it up at once.

In 1877 the All England Club at Wimbledon held the first championship, mainly to raise money for a croquet lawn roller. The court became a rectangle, the net dropped lower, and lawn tennis soon travelled the world.

Tennis today

For decades tennis was divided between amateurs and professionals. In 1968 the 'Open Era' began, letting everyone compete together — and the great tournaments at Wimbledon, Paris, Melbourne and New York became true world championships.

Great champions, from Rod Laver and Martina Navratilova to Roger Federer and Serena Williams, made tennis one of the world's most watched sports, played on grass, clay and hard courts on every continent.

Did You Know?

Wimbledon still keeps its oldest rule of style: players must wear almost entirely white clothing — a tradition from the 1800s, when sweat stains on coloured clothes were thought improper.
