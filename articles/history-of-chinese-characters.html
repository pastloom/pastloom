History of Chinese Characters

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Chinese characters are one of the oldest writing systems in the world still in daily use. Each character is a small block of strokes that stands for a word or part of a word — there are tens of thousands of them.

They began as pictures scratched on bones more than 3,000 years ago. Because characters carry meaning rather than sound, people who speak different Chinese languages can read the same text.

Pictures on bones

The oldest Chinese writing we can read was carved on ox bones and turtle shells about 1250 BCE. Kings of the Shang dynasty used the heated bones to ask questions of their ancestors, and carved the questions into the shell.

Many early characters are little pictures — of the sun, a horse, a person — that slowly became more abstract and squared as brushes and styles changed.

One script for an empire

When the First Emperor united China in 221 BCE, his government standardised the characters into one seal script for the whole empire — a reform that held China together for 2,000 years.

Later came the clerical script of officials and then regular script, the clear standard form still taught today. Because characters show meaning, officials from regions whose speech differed completely could govern through the same written words.

Characters in the modern age

Printing with thousands of characters was hard, and typewriters and computers at first seemed to threaten the script. Instead, smart input systems let people type sounds and choose characters.

In the 1950s mainland China simplified many characters to fight illiteracy, while Taiwan and Hong Kong kept the traditional forms. Both are read by hundreds of millions today — the world's oldest living script, still at work.

Did You Know?

The character for 'eternity' was the calligrapher's favourite practice word, because its eight strokes contain all the basic brush movements of the art.
