The Reconquista

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Reconquista was the long struggle by which Christian kingdoms of northern Spain and Portugal gradually conquered the Muslim states of al-Andalus, between 711 and 1492. It lasted nearly 800 years — longer than most civilisations — and it shaped Spain, Portugal and their languages.

It ended in 1492, the same year Columbus reached the Americas and Spain became a single power. That single year — Granada's fall, the expulsion of the Jews, and the Atlantic crossing — marks the birth of modern Spain and the start of its world empire.

Al-Andalus

In 711, Muslim armies crossed from North Africa and, in a few astonishing years, conquered almost the whole Iberian Peninsula — the Visigothic kingdom collapsed, and a new civilisation, al-Andalus, rose in its place. Córdoba became one of the largest and most brilliant cities in Europe, a centre of science, philosophy and trade where Muslims, Christians and Jews lived side by side in a working, if unequal, coexistence.

Only in the northern mountains — Asturias, León, Navarre, Aragón and the Catalan counties — did small Christian kingdoms survive. From them, slowly, the frontier began to move south.

The long frontier

For centuries the 'Reconquista' was less a single crusade than a shifting frontier of raids, alliances and settlement — Christian kings fought Muslim rulers, but also each other, and often allied across the religious line. The legendary hero El Cid fought for both sides. In 1085 Alfonso VI of León-Castile took Toledo, the old Visigothic capital, a turning point that alarmed the Muslim world.

North African empires — first the Almoravids, then the Almohads — crossed to defend al-Andalus, and the frontier swung back and forth. Christian victories at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 broke Almohad power, and in the following decades Castile took Córdoba (1236) and Seville (1248), while Aragón took Valencia and Portugal reached the Atlantic coast.

Granada, the last emirate

By 1250 only one Muslim state remained: the Emirate of Granada, in the mountains of the south, surviving as a tribute-paying neighbour of Castile for 250 years. Granada's Nasrid sultans built the Alhambra — the red palace-city above the city, one of the masterpieces of Islamic art — while their merchants traded with Genoa and their farmers made the vega of Granada rich.

Meanwhile the Christian north consolidated: Castile and León united permanently in 1230, and in 1469 the marriage of Ferdinand of Aragón and Isabella of Castile joined the two greatest crowns — the partnership that would create Spain.

1492: the year that changed the world

Ferdinand and Isabella turned on Granada in a final ten-year war. On 2 January 1492, the last sultan, Boabdil, surrendered the keys of the Alhambra. Nearly eight centuries of Muslim rule in Iberia were over; in the same year, Jews who refused conversion were expelled from Spain, and Columbus — sailing for the same monarchs — reached the Americas.

The Reconquista left deep marks: a Spanish identity forged in frontier warfare, a language sprinkled with Arabic words (ojalá — 'God willing' — among them), and architecture, agriculture and scholarship inherited from al-Andalus. Modern historians treat the old simple story — eight centuries of holy war — with care: the reality was a complex, shifting frontier where faith, land and politics mixed.

Did You Know?

The Spanish word 'reconquista' means 'reconquest' — but historians today debate the term itself: the Christian kingdoms also fought each other, and alliances across faiths were common for centuries.
