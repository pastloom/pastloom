Confucius

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Confucius (551–479 BCE) was a Chinese teacher and thinker whose ideas shaped China — and much of East Asia — for more than two thousand years. In an age of war between rival states, he taught that society is healed not by force but by virtue: rulers who are good, families that are dutiful, and people who educate themselves.

He failed in his lifetime to find a ruler who would follow him. After his death, his students' records of his sayings — the Analects — became the foundation of Confucianism, the philosophy that trained China's officials and still echoes in East Asian life today.

A teacher in an age of war

Confucius was born in 551 BCE in the small state of Lu, as China's old Zhou order was collapsing into the chaos of the Spring and Autumn period — rival states, ambitious lords, constant war. His father died young; the family was poor but of old aristocratic stock, and the boy educated himself into a scholar of ritual, music, history and archery.

He held minor government posts, and by his thirties was teaching — revolutionary in itself: he accepted any student who could pay even a bundle of dried meat, declaring that in education there should be no class distinctions. Tradition credits him with 3,000 students, 72 of them close disciples.

The teaching

Confucius did not claim to invent; he said he transmitted the wisdom of the ancients. His ideal was the junzi — the exemplary person — guided by ren, humaneness or benevolence, and by li, the rituals and courtesies that make social life harmonious. 'Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself,' he said — his version of the Golden Rule, centuries early.

Society, he taught, is held together by five relationships — ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, elder and younger, friend and friend — each with duties on both sides. Filial piety, devotion to parents and ancestors, stood at the root: a person loyal at home would be loyal in the state. And rulers must lead by moral example, not punishment: 'Guide them with virtue, and they will have a sense of shame and moreover will become good.'

Failure in life, triumph in death

At about fifty-five, disappointed in Lu, Confucius spent some thirteen years wandering from state to state, offering his counsel to rulers who listened politely and hired him rarely. He returned home to teach and edit the classics, and died in 479 BCE, feeling himself a failure.

His followers disagreed. Disciples compiled the Analects from remembered conversations; thinkers like Mencius developed his ideas; and in the Han dynasty (2nd century BCE) Confucianism became China's official philosophy. For the next two thousand years, China's civil servants were chosen by examinations on the Confucian classics — a teacher with no power in life became the uncrowned ruler of Chinese civilisation, and his thought spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

Confucius today

Confucian values — respect for education, family duty, social harmony, merit over birth — still run deep in East Asian societies, shaping everything from school pressure to corporate culture. China, which attacked Confucius during the Cultural Revolution, now honours him again, naming its global cultural institutes after him.

His sayings remain among the most quoted on Earth — often in forms he never said ('Confucius say…' jokes are a Western invention). The real voice in the Analects is sterner and warmer: a teacher insisting, in a violent age, that character is stronger than swords.

Did You Know?

Confucius may be the most influential teacher in history: for about 2,000 years, almost every official in China — the world's most populous state — was selected by examinations on his teachings.
