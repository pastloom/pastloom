History of X-Rays

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

X-rays let doctors see inside the living body — broken bones, swallowed coins, sick lungs — without any surgery. They were discovered by accident in 1895 and spread around the world within months.

Our library tells how Marie Curie took X-rays to the battlefields of World War I. This article tells the full story, from the first ghostly photograph of a hand to the hospital scanners of today.

The glowing screen

Wilhelm Röntgen was experimenting with electric rays in a darkened room in Würzburg when he noticed a screen across the room glowing — though no light could reach it. Something invisible was passing through solid things.

For seven secret weeks he tested the rays. They passed through paper, wood and flesh, but were stopped by bone and metal. When he X-rayed his wife Bertha's hand, she saw her own skeleton and cried that she had seen her death.

A world sensation

Röntgen published his discovery at the end of 1895, and newspapers everywhere printed the amazing bone pictures. Within a year, doctors were using X-rays to find bullets and set broken bones.

In World War I, Marie Curie equipped cars with X-ray machines — her famous 'petites Curies' — so wounded soldiers could be scanned near the front line, saving countless limbs and lives.

Seeing inside, safely

Early users did not know the rays could harm skin and tissue, and some pioneers suffered burns. Over time, lead shielding, low doses and careful rules made X-rays safe and routine.

In the 1970s, CT scanners combined X-rays with computers to build full 3-D pictures of the body. Röntgen's accidental glow had become one of medicine's most important tools.

Did You Know?

Röntgen refused to make money from his discovery — he took no patent, believing X-rays should belong to all humanity. He donated his Nobel Prize money to his university.
