The Great Depression

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Great Depression was the worst economic crisis of modern times. It began in the United States in 1929 and spread around the world, lasting through most of the 1930s.

Banks failed, factories closed and millions of people lost their jobs and homes. The crisis changed how governments everywhere think about money, work and helping people in hard times.

The crash of 1929

In the booming 1920s, many Americans bought shares hoping to get rich quickly, often with borrowed money. In October 1929, share prices collapsed on Wall Street.

Panic spread to banks. People rushed to withdraw savings, thousands of banks failed, and families lost everything. Spending stopped, factories closed, and joblessness climbed month after month.

Hard times around the world

Because countries traded and lent money to each other, the crisis spread worldwide. In Germany, mass unemployment helped extremist parties, including the Nazis, win support.

In the American plains, drought turned farmland to dust — the Dust Bowl — and thousands of farming families left their homes to search for work.

The New Deal and recovery

President Franklin D. Roosevelt, elected in 1932, launched the New Deal: banks were reformed, and public works gave jobs building roads, dams and schools. Social Security pensions began in 1935.

The Depression taught governments to watch banks closely, insure savings and support people who lose work — protections many countries still have today.

Did You Know?

During the Depression, some American towns printed their own temporary money, called scrip, because real cash and open banks were so hard to find.
