History of Maps

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A map is a picture of the world — or part of it — drawn small enough to hold. The oldest known world map was carved on a clay tablet in Babylon more than 2,500 years ago.

Maps grew with travel and trade. Sailors, soldiers and explorers filled in coastlines and continents, until the whole Earth could be drawn — and today satellites map the planet from space.

The first pictures of the world

Babylonians drew their world on clay, with Babylon at the centre and the ocean around it. Greek scholars went further: Eratosthenes even measured the size of the Earth with surprising accuracy, and Ptolemy used lines of latitude and longitude and reports from travellers.

Ptolemy's Geography, written about 150 CE, guided mapmakers for more than a thousand years — even though it made Asia too big and the Earth too small, a mistake that later encouraged Columbus.

Maps of faith, sea and empire

Medieval European maps placed Jerusalem at the centre and mixed geography with religion, and unknown lands were filled with drawings of sea monsters. Meanwhile, Islamic scholars like al-Idrisi made remarkably accurate world maps, and Chinese mapmakers carved detailed maps in stone.

Sailors used portolan charts — sea maps crisscrossed with compass lines — that showed Mediterranean coasts with surprising accuracy. As European ships crossed the oceans, sea charts became state secrets, maps filled with new continents, and mapmaking became a tool of empire.

The modern map

In 1569, Mercator's projection let sailors plot straight courses, though it stretches lands near the poles — Greenland looks as big as Africa, though Africa is really about fourteen times larger. The word 'atlas' for a book of maps comes from Atlas, the Greek titan who held up the sky.

By the 1800s, surveyors had filled in nearly every coastline. Aeroplanes, satellites and GPS have now mapped almost every corner of Earth, and digital maps update in real time — the clay tablet of Babylon has become the glowing map in every phone.
