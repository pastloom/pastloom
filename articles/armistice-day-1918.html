Armistice Day 1918

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

On 11 November 1918, the fighting of World War I stopped. That morning, Germany and the Allied countries signed an armistice — an agreement to stop fighting — in a railway carriage in a French forest.

The guns fell silent at 11 o'clock in the morning: the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. The day is still remembered every year with silence, poppies and ceremonies for the soldiers who died.

The carriage in the forest

By the autumn of 1918, Germany was losing the war. Its allies had given up, its people were hungry, and American soldiers were arriving in France in huge numbers. A German team led by Matthias Erzberger crossed the lines under a flag of truce.

Marshal Foch, the Allied commander, received them in his railway carriage at Compiègne. The terms were harsh, and after days of talks the Germans signed in the early hours of 11 November. The news raced along the front by telephone and telegram.

The eleventh hour

Strangely, the fighting went on until the exact hour agreed. Soldiers on both sides were killed on that last morning — thousands were wounded or killed on a day when everyone knew the war was ending.

At 11 o'clock, the guns stopped. Men climbed out of the trenches in disbelief. In London, Paris and New York, crowds filled the streets, church bells rang, and strangers hugged and sang.

Remembering ever since

An armistice is not a peace treaty — the war was formally ended by the Treaty of Versailles in 1919. But 11 November became the day of memory: Britain and Commonwealth countries call it Remembrance Day, and the United States marks it as Veterans Day.

At 11 o'clock, many countries keep two minutes of silence. Red poppies, worn since 1921, stand for the soldiers who died — inspired by the poppies that grew among the graves in Flanders fields.

Did You Know?

The same railway carriage was used again in 1940, when Hitler forced France to surrender in it at the very same spot — he had it brought out of its museum for revenge.
