The Arab Spring

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 2011, a wave of protests swept across the Arab world, toppling rulers who had held power for decades. It began in Tunisia, when a poor street seller named Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire in despair at corruption and humiliation — and his act lit a fire across the region.

Within weeks, the presidents of Tunisia and Egypt had fallen, and protests had spread to Libya, Syria, Yemen, Bahrain and beyond. The Arab Spring raised hopes of democracy, but its results were very different from country to country.

Tunisia and Egypt: rulers fall

Bouazizi's death in January 2011 brought Tunisians onto the streets, and President Ben Ali — in power for 23 years — fled the country. Inspired, Egyptians filled Cairo's Tahrir Square for 18 days until President Hosni Mubarak, ruler for 30 years, resigned in February 2011.

The protesters were mostly young, connected by phones and social media, and their demands were simple: work, dignity, honest government and an end to police cruelty.

Libya, Syria and Yemen: protest turns to war

In Libya, protests became a civil war; with help from NATO air strikes, rebels overthrew and killed Muammar Gaddafi, but Libya stayed divided and violent for years.

In Syria, President Bashar al-Assad answered protests with force, and the country sank into a terrible civil war that killed hundreds of thousands and drove millions from their homes. Yemen, too, fell into war after its president stepped down.

What the Arab Spring changed

Tunisia became the one clear democracy to emerge, writing a new constitution and holding free elections — though it later faced economic and political troubles.

Elsewhere, old-style rule returned or wars continued, and many people spoke of an 'Arab Winter'. Even so, 2011 proved that rulers once thought untouchable could be challenged by ordinary people — a lesson the region has not forgotten.

Did You Know?

In Egypt, protesters organised so much through one Facebook page — 'We Are All Khaled Said', named after a young man killed by police — that the government shut down the internet for five days. The protests only grew.
