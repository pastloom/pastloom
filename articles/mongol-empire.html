Mongol Empire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Mongol Empire was the largest connected land empire in history. In the 1200s, under Genghis Khan and his heirs, Mongol horse-archers conquered from China to Hungary — an empire of some 23 million square kilometres, ruling about a third of humanity.

Feared as destroyers, the Mongols also brought a century of peace, the Pax Mongolica, in which trade, ideas and inventions flowed safely along the Silk Road from Asia to Europe.

Temujin unites the steppe

The Mongols were nomad herders of the Central Asian steppe, divided into feuding tribes. Temujin, born about 1162, survived a brutal childhood — his father poisoned, his family outcast — and by talent, loyalty and ruthlessness united the tribes. In 1206 an assembly proclaimed him Genghis Khan, 'universal ruler'.

He built an army like no other: every man a horse-archer with several remounts, organised in units of ten, promoted by merit, bound by strict discipline and the Yassa law code. Then he turned outward.

The conquests

The Mongols conquered with speed, terror and brilliant siegecraft learned from Chinese engineers. Northern China fell by 1215; the Khwarezmian Empire of Persia was destroyed in 1219–21 after its ruler seized a Mongol caravan; Russia's principalities fell in 1237–40, and in 1241 Mongol armies smashed knights in Poland and Hungary — stopping, some say, only because the Great Khan died and the princes rode home to elect his successor.

Cities that resisted were annihilated; cities that surrendered were taxed and left in peace. Baghdad's fall in 1258 ended the Abbasid caliphate. Southern China, defended for decades, finally fell to Kublai Khan in 1279.

The Pax Mongolica

Under the Mongol peace, Eurasia was connected as never before. The Yam relay system — stations with fresh horses, food and lodging — sped messengers and merchants across the empire. The Silk Road became safe enough for a Venetian teenager, Marco Polo, to travel to China and serve Kublai Khan.

Trade, technology and ideas flowed: Chinese printing, gunpowder and the compass moved west; Persian astronomy and medicine moved east. The Mongols protected religions of every kind and employed talent from every nation they ruled.

Four khanates and the long legacy

The empire was too vast for one ruler. After Mongke Khan's death in 1259, it split into four khanates: Yuan China under Kublai, the Ilkhanate in Persia, the Golden Horde on the Russian steppe, and the Chagatai Khanate in Central Asia. They fought each other as often as their neighbours.

One by one they fell — Yuan to the Ming in 1368, the others over the following centuries — but their mark remained: a unified China, a reshaped Russia and Persia, and a Eurasia stitched together. The Mongols proved a small, organised people could remake the whole map of the world.

Did You Know?

Marco Polo's book about Mongol China was so full of wonders that many readers called him 'Marco Millions' — they thought he was exaggerating. Much of what he wrote turned out to be true.
