The Golden Age of Piracy

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Golden Age of Piracy was a short, wild period — roughly 1650 to 1730 — when thousands of pirates hunted treasure ships across the Caribbean and the Atlantic.

It produced the pirates we still picture today: Blackbeard, the Jolly Roger flag, pirate havens like Nassau — and mostly, short lives that ended on the gallows.

Why the age began

European empires poured silver and gold out of the Americas, and Spain's treasure fleets made tempting targets. When wars ended, navies dismissed thousands of trained sailors — men who knew ships, guns and the sea lanes, and now had no wages.

Some became buccaneers, hunting from island bases like Tortuga and Port Royal. Others took the 'sweet trade' of piracy outright, electing their captains and dividing loot by agreed shares — fairer rules, in some ways, than the brutal discipline of naval ships.

Blackbeard and the pirate republic

Around 1716–18, pirates ran Nassau in the Bahamas as their own rough republic. From there sailed Charles Vane, Calico Jack, and the most theatrical pirate of all: Edward Teach, Blackbeard, who wove slow-burning fuses into his beard so smoke wreathed his face in battle.

Anne Bonny and Mary Read served in Calico Jack's crew and fought as fiercely as any man aboard. But the age was already closing: in 1718 the governor Woodes Rogers arrived at Nassau with the king's pardon in one hand and warships behind him. Blackbeard refused pardon and died fighting that same year, sword in hand.

The gallows end the age

Governments stopped tolerating pirates once peace made trade too valuable to risk. Naval patrols hunted the pirate ships, and captured crews were tried and hanged in rows at harbour mouths.

Bartholomew Roberts, the last great captain, was killed in 1722, and his crew's trial in Africa was the largest pirate trial of the age. By about 1730 the Golden Age was over — leaving behind the skull-and-crossbones, the treasure maps of storybooks, and legends far larger than the rough, brief lives behind them.

Did You Know?

Pirate crews voted for their captains and could vote them out again — and their written rules often promised payment for injuries, like an early form of insurance.
