The Mamluk Sultanate

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Mamluk Sultanate ruled Egypt and Syria from 1250 to 1517. Its rulers were mamluks — men who had been bought as boys, trained as soldiers, and freed to serve as an elite army.

The Mamluks are famous for two great rescues of the region: they stopped the Mongol advance at Ain Jalut in 1260, and they ended the Crusader states in the Holy Land.

Soldiers who became kings

For generations, the rulers of Egypt bought boys from the steppes north of the Black Sea and from the Caucasus, raised them as Muslims, and trained them as cavalry. These were the mamluks — owned, yet elite.

In 1250, as a Crusader invasion struck Egypt, the mamluks seized power for themselves. From then on, each sultan had himself risen through the mamluk ranks.

Saving the land from the Mongols

In 1260 the Mongols, who had destroyed Baghdad two years earlier, marched into Syria. The Mamluk army met them at Ain Jalut in Palestine and won a famous victory.

It was a turning point: the Mongol advance westward stopped for good. Under Sultan Baybars, the Mamluks then retook the remaining Crusader ports, ending with Acre in 1291.

Golden Cairo and the Ottoman end

Mamluk sultans filled Cairo with mosques, schools, hospitals and tombs covered in carved stone and inlay. The city became the leading centre of the Islamic world after Baghdad's fall.

But plague, rising prices and Portuguese ships stealing the spice trade weakened the state. In 1517 the Ottoman sultan Selim I defeated the Mamluks and added Egypt and Syria to his empire.

Did You Know?

A mamluk sultan could not pass his throne to his son in the normal way — the top job usually went to the strongest mamluk commander, so nearly every sultan had started life as a bought boy soldier.
