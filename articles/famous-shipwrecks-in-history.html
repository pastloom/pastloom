Famous Shipwrecks in History

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The sea keeps its wrecks. Around the world, an estimated three million shipwrecks rest on the ocean floor — Roman cargo ships, Spanish treasure galleons, steam liners and warships, each one a time capsule from the day it sank.

Some wrecks are famous tragedies, some are treasure chests, and some have become museums. Together they tell the story of ships — and of the people who sailed them.

Wrecks as time capsules

Cold water and soft mud can preserve a wreck astonishingly well. When the Mary Rose was lifted from the Solent in 1982, she brought up longbows, cannons, musical instruments, a surgeon's tools — and the skeleton of the ship's dog.

Sweden's Vasa, raised in Stockholm harbour in 1961, is even more complete: a whole 17th-century warship, now the centrepiece of her own museum and one of the most visited attractions in Scandinavia.

Treasure and tragedy

Other wrecks are famous for what they carried or whom they cost. The Spanish galleon Nuestra Señora de Atocha went down in a hurricane in 1622 loaded with silver and gold; Mel Fisher's team found her main treasure in 1985, after sixteen years of searching.

The Titanic, found in 1985 by a team led by Robert Ballard, lies broken in two in the deep Atlantic — too fragile to raise, visited only by submersibles. The Lusitania, sunk by a U-boat off Ireland in 1915 with the loss of 1,198 lives, remains a war grave and a warning.

Protecting the wrecks

For years, anyone with a boat could loot a wreck. Today UNESCO's 2001 convention protects underwater heritage, many navies guard their sunken warships as graves, and archaeologists survey wrecks with robots before touching anything.

Wrecks have a second life too: sunk ships become reefs, sheltering fish, corals and divers' cameras. The sea's museums are open — but only to those who visit gently.

Did You Know?

The Vasa was preserved so well in the cold, dark Baltic that when she was raised in 1961, even the carved wooden sculptures on her stern — lions, kings and Roman emperors — looked almost new after 333 years underwater.
