Women of the Space Race

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The famous faces of the Space Race were men — Gagarin, Armstrong, Aldrin. But women were part of the race from the start: calculating flight paths by hand, writing the software that flew Apollo, and, in the Soviet Union, flying to space itself.

This companion to our Space Race article tells their story: the first woman in space, and the women whose mathematics carried astronauts safely to the Moon and back.

The human computers

Before electronic computers were trusted, NASA employed teams of mathematicians — many of them women — to calculate flight paths with pencils, slide rules and adding machines. They were known, simply, as computers.

At Langley, Black women such as Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan and Mary Jackson did this vital work while facing the unfair separation of the segregation era. Johnson became so trusted that in 1962 astronaut John Glenn refused to fly until she personally checked the electronic computer's numbers for his orbit.

Tereshkova flies

The Soviet Union scored another first on 16 June 1963, when Valentina Tereshkova, a 26-year-old factory worker and skilled parachutist, flew alone aboard Vostok 6. She circled the Earth 48 times — more orbits than all American astronauts flown up to that day combined.

No other woman flew in space for nineteen years. The Soviet Union sent its second woman cosmonaut only in 1982, and the United States its first, Sally Ride, in 1983.

Software that saved Apollo

Margaret Hamilton led the team that wrote the guidance software for Apollo. Her code was designed to ignore lower-priority alarms and keep flying — and during Apollo 11's descent to the Moon, that is exactly what it did when warning lights flashed.

Hamilton is credited with popularising the very term software engineering. The women of the race — calculators, coders and one young cosmonaut — had helped win it, even if the headlines of the time rarely said so.

Did You Know?

Before his 1962 flight, John Glenn asked engineers to get the girl — meaning Katherine Johnson — to run the orbital numbers by hand. Only when she confirmed them did he say he was ready to fly.
