Egyptian Art and Architecture

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Egyptian art followed the same clear rules for nearly 3,000 years: people shown with heads and legs from the side but shoulders facing forward, important figures drawn bigger, and everything filled with colour and symbols.

Egyptian builders raised some of the most lasting buildings on Earth — pyramids, temples with giant columns, towering obelisks and statues carved from single blocks of stone.

The rules of Egyptian art

Egyptian artists were not trying to copy nature exactly. They followed sacred rules so that every figure could be recognised at once and would last forever in the afterlife.

A standing figure was drawn as a puzzle of the clearest views: head in profile, eye facing forward, shoulders square, legs striding. Colours had meanings too — gold for the sun and the flesh of gods, green for new life, black for the rich soil of the Nile.

Temples in stone

A great temple began with two massive sloping towers called pylons, carved with scenes of the king defeating Egypt's enemies. Behind them came open courts and dark halls of huge columns, shaped like papyrus plants and lotus flowers.

Deeper inside, the temple grew darker and quieter, ending in a small shrine where the god's statue lived. Only priests and the pharaoh could enter there.

Statues and obelisks

Egyptian sculptors carved statues meant to last for eternity — calm, strong and facing straight ahead. The colossal statues of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel are each about 20 metres tall.

Obelisks were cut from a single piece of red granite, covered in hieroglyphs and tipped with gold to catch the first light of the sun. Many were later taken to Rome, Paris, London and New York, where they still stand today.

Did You Know?

Egyptian artists first drew a grid of squares on a wall, then sketched the figure to fit set proportions — the same grid system was used for thousands of years.
