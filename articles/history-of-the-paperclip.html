History of the Paperclip

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The paperclip is a small loop of bent wire that holds sheets of paper together without piercing or gluing them. It is so simple that we forget somebody had to invent it.

In fact, no single person did. In the late 1800s, dozens of inventors patented clips of different shapes — and the oval 'Gem' clip we know today won the contest.

Before the clip

For centuries, papers were kept together with straight pins, wax, ribbon or thread — or by cutting a slit in the sheets and threading them. Pins rusted and tore the paper, and the growing offices of the 1800s, drowning in letters and forms, needed something better.

Inventors answered with a flood of ideas. Samuel Fay patented a wire ticket fastener in 1867, and dozens of rival clips followed — triangles, ovals, and shapes like tiny pretzels.

A crowd of inventors

The winner was the 'Gem' clip — a double oval of wire, produced by the Gem Manufacturing Company in Britain by the 1890s. In 1899, William Middlebrook patented a machine that could bend wire into Gem clips quickly and cheaply.

Norway proudly honours Johan Vaaler, who patented a clip in 1901. His design, however, was different from the Gem and less practical, and the Gem shape was already in use — the paperclip is a lesson in how inventions often have many parents.

A tiny symbol

During the Second World War, occupied Norway turned the paperclip into a secret badge: wearing one on your coat showed quiet resistance and unity, until the occupiers banned it.

The clip's fame has never quite faded. Giant paperclips stand as monuments, and in 2005 a Canadian, Kyle MacDonald, famously traded one red paperclip, step by step, all the way up to a house.

Did You Know?

During the Second World War, Norwegians wore paperclips on their clothes as a secret sign of resistance — the humble clip became so provocative that wearing one could get you arrested.
