The Oracle of Delphi

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

High on the slopes of Mount Parnassus in Greece stood Delphi, the most famous holy place of the ancient Greek world. There, in the temple of the god Apollo, a priestess called the Pythia gave answers from the gods.

For more than a thousand years, kings, cities and ordinary people travelled to Delphi before making big decisions — going to war, founding a colony, or passing a law. No place in Greece was more respected.

The navel of the world

The Greeks believed Delphi was the centre of the world. In their story, Zeus released two eagles from opposite ends of the earth, and they met at Delphi. A round stone called the omphalos marked the spot.

Around Apollo's temple grew a whole sacred town: treasuries built by grateful cities, statues, a theatre and a stadium where the Pythian Games — second only to the Olympics — were held every four years.

How the oracle spoke

A person with a question brought gifts and sacrifices, then asked the Pythia. She sat inside the temple on a three-legged stool, and her answers were shaped into verses by priests.

Her replies were famous for being hard to read two ways. When King Croesus of Lydia asked if he should attack Persia, he was told he would 'destroy a great empire'. He attacked — and destroyed his own empire. The oracle had been right, in the cruellest way.

Why Delphi mattered

Delphi was more than religion. Because every Greek city sent messengers there, the priests heard news from everywhere, and their advice helped shape wars, laws and new colonies across the Mediterranean.

The oracle faded as Christianity spread, and the last known answer was given in the 390s CE. Today the ruins of Delphi, in their stunning mountain setting, are a World Heritage Site — and still feel like a place where the world once came to listen.

Did You Know?

Before a battle or a voyage, even mighty Sparta and Athens sent official messengers to Delphi — the trip, the sacrifices and the waiting could take weeks, so generals had to plan their wars around the oracle's calendar.
