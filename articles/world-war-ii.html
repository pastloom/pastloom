World War II

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

World War II was the largest and deadliest war in human history. From 1939 to 1945 it spread across Europe, Africa, Asia and the Pacific Ocean, drew in more than one hundred million soldiers, and killed more than seventy million people — most of them civilians.

It began with Nazi Germany's invasion of Poland and ended with Germany and Japan defeated and in ruins. Out of its ashes came a changed world: the United Nations, the division of Europe between East and West, the end of the old colonial empires, and the beginning of the atomic age.

How the war began

The war grew out of the bitterness left by World War I. Germany, humiliated and poor after 1918, turned to Adolf Hitler and his Nazi Party, who promised to make Germany powerful again and blamed its troubles on enemies within and without. Through the 1930s Hitler rebuilt the army and seized Austria and Czechoslovakia, while Britain and France, desperate to avoid another war, gave way.

On 1 September 1939 Germany invaded Poland. This time Britain and France declared war. In Asia the fire was already burning: Japan had invaded China in 1937 in a vast, brutal war of its own.

The war spreads across the world

At first Germany seemed unstoppable. Using fast-moving tanks and aircraft — blitzkrieg, 'lightning war' — it conquered Poland, Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, Belgium and France in 1939–40. Britain survived the Battle of Britain in the air, but stood alone. In June 1941 Hitler made his fatal gamble and invaded the Soviet Union, opening a gigantic eastern front.

In December 1941 Japan attacked the American fleet at Pearl Harbor, bringing the United States into the war and making it truly global. Fighting now stretched from the Atlantic convoys and the deserts of North Africa to the jungles of Burma, the islands of the Pacific and the skies over Europe. In the lands Germany occupied, the Nazis carried out the Holocaust — the murder of six million Jews, alongside millions of other victims.

The turning points

In 1942–43 the tide turned. The Soviet Union destroyed a German army at Stalingrad after months of street fighting; the Allies defeated Germany and Italy in North Africa; and American forces began pushing Japan back across the Pacific, island by island.

On 6 June 1944 — D-Day — the Western Allies landed in Normandy, France, in the largest seaborne invasion in history. Paris was freed that summer. From the east, the Red Army drove through Poland into Germany itself, while Allied bombing reduced German cities to rubble.

The end, and the world it made

In April 1945 Soviet troops reached Berlin; Hitler took his own life, and Germany surrendered in May. The war in Europe was over. In the Pacific, Japan fought on until the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. Japan surrendered in September, and the war was finally finished.

The cost defied imagination: more than seventy million dead, whole cities destroyed, and millions homeless across Europe and Asia. The victors founded the United Nations in 1945 to prevent another such war, put Nazi leaders on trial at Nuremberg, and divided Europe between a democratic West and a communist East — the beginning of the Cold War that shaped the next half-century.

Did You Know?

World War II was the first war in which more civilians than soldiers died — including six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust.
