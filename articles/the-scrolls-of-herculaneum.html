The Scrolls of Herculaneum

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In a seaside villa buried by Vesuvius, archaeologists found a Roman library: about 1,800 papyrus scrolls, turned black as charcoal by the volcano's heat.

For 250 years no one could read them without destroying them. Now X-ray scanners and artificial intelligence are reading the scrolls without opening them — and the first lost words have already appeared.

A library in a buried villa

When workers tunnelling into Herculaneum in the 1750s reached a grand seaside villa, they found scrolls everywhere — on shelves, in boxes, even in a bundle, as if someone had tried to rescue them. At first the blackened rolls were mistaken for lumps of coal, and some were thrown away.

The villa — named the Villa of the Papyri after its library — may have belonged to the family of Julius Caesar's father-in-law. Most of its books are works by the philosopher Philodemus, written in Greek: guides to living a calm and happy life.

The struggle to read them

The scrolls were so fragile that unrolling them usually turned them to dust. In 1753 the Vatican librarian Antonio Piaggio built a gentle machine that peeled a scroll open millimetre by millimetre with silk threads — it could take months to open a single scroll.

Over the centuries, hundreds of scrolls were damaged by impatient methods: cut in half, soaked in chemicals, or crumbled to pieces. Even so, patient work recovered thousands of lines of lost Greek writing found nowhere else on Earth.

Reading without opening

In the 2000s, scientists began scanning sealed scrolls with powerful X-ray scanners, mapping the rolled layers hidden inside. The ink, made of soot, is nearly invisible in the scans — until machine learning was trained to detect its faint texture.

In 2023, in a worldwide competition called the Vesuvius Challenge, students and researchers read the first whole word from a scroll no one had opened: 'purple'. Whole passages have followed, and historians hope lost works of philosophy, poetry and history are waiting inside the rest.

Did You Know?

Some scrolls were found packed together as if someone, even as the volcano erupted, had tried to save the library — we will never know who they were, or whether they escaped.
