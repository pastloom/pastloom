History of Medicine

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Medicine is as old as human pain. For thousands of years, healers used herbs, prayer and simple surgery — and some of their plant remedies really worked: willow bark, for example, is the ancestor of aspirin. Slowly, step by step, medicine became a science.

In the last two hundred years, vaccines, germ theory, anaesthesia and antibiotics have saved more lives than almost any other inventions in history.

Healers of the ancient world

Egyptian doctors set bones, stitched wounds and wrote their knowledge on papyrus scrolls. In India, Ayurvedic doctors performed surgery, and in China healers developed acupuncture and herbal medicine.

In Greece, Hippocrates taught that disease has natural causes — not punishments from the gods — and doctors still honour his name today. The Roman-era doctor Galen wrote books that guided medicine for over a thousand years, and in the Islamic Golden Age, scholars such as Ibn Sina gathered Greek, Indian and Persian medicine into great books: his Canon of Medicine was used in European universities for about 600 years.

Medicine becomes a science

In the Islamic Golden Age, hospitals in Baghdad and Cairo treated patients from all walks of life. In Europe, the printing press later spread medical knowledge fast.

In the 1500s, Vesalius mapped the human body from real dissection, and Harvey showed how the heart pumps blood around the body. Medicine was learning to look and test, not just to guess.

Germs, vaccines and pain-free surgery

In 1796, Edward Jenner showed that a mild disease, cowpox, could protect people from deadly smallpox — the first vaccination. In the 1800s, Louis Pasteur proved that tiny germs cause disease and decay.

Soon after, Joseph Lister used antiseptics to keep wounds clean, and new anaesthetics let surgeons operate without pain. Hospitals became places of healing instead of places of fear.

The age of antibiotics and beyond

In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed that a mould — penicillin — killed bacteria. Turned into a medicine in the 1940s, it saved millions of soldiers and civilians.

X-rays, blood transfusions, insulin, transplants and modern vaccines followed. In 1980, the World Health Organization declared smallpox wiped out — the first disease in history defeated by medicine.

Did You Know?

Surgeons in ancient India could rebuild a damaged nose using skin from the forehead — a method European doctors later learned from Indian practice.
