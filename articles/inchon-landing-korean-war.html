The Inchon Landing: The Bold Gamble of the Korean War

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In September 1950, the Korean War seemed almost lost. United Nations forces held only a small corner of South Korea. Then General Douglas MacArthur made one of the boldest bets in military history: a sea landing far behind enemy lines, at the port of Inchon.

Our main article on the Korean War tells the whole story of the war. This companion zooms in on that single landing — why almost every expert said it would fail, and how it changed the war in a few days.

A landing everyone feared

Inchon looked like the worst possible place to land an army. When the tide went out, it left kilometres of deep mud where ships would strand and soldiers would sink. When it came in, landing craft had only a few hours before the water fell again.

There were also high sea walls to climb, a narrow twisting channel to enter, and a fortified island, Wolmi-do, guarding the harbour. Navy officers listed every danger — and MacArthur answered that surprise was worth it all, because Inchon was the last place the enemy would expect.

The day of the landing

On 15 September 1950, warships and aircraft first pounded Wolmi-do, and Marines captured the island on the morning tide. That evening, on the high tide, the main force stormed over the sea walls into Inchon itself, lit by burning warehouses.

The gamble worked. North Korean forces, whose supply lines ran through Seoul, were suddenly cut off. UN troops pushed inland, and by the end of September, Seoul — the South Korean capital — was back in Allied hands. The North Korean army in the south collapsed.

Victory — and the warning that followed

MacArthur's forces raced north, crossing into North Korea and heading toward the Chinese border. It looked as if the war would end quickly, with Korea united.

Then, in late November 1950, China sent a huge army across the border, driving the UN forces back south. The war settled near the old dividing line and ground on until the 1953 armistice — the story told in our main Korean War article.

Did You Know?

The Inchon landing could only be attempted on a few days each month, when the tide was at its highest. MacArthur's chosen date, 15 September, was picked by the sea itself — miss it, and the fleet would wait a whole month.
