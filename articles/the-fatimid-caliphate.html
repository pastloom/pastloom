The Fatimid Caliphate

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Fatimid Caliphate was a state that ruled North Africa, Egypt and parts of Syria and Arabia between 909 and 1171. It was a rival to the Abbasid caliphs of Baghdad.

The Fatimids founded the city of Cairo in 969 and made it their capital. Cairo — and its great university, al-Azhar — still carries their mark today.

Rise in North Africa

The Fatimids began as a movement in North Africa that opposed Abbasid rule. In 909 their leader took the title of caliph, setting up a second caliphate beside Baghdad's.

From their base in modern Tunisia and Algeria, Fatimid armies and fleets grew stronger until, in 969, their general Jawhar conquered Egypt almost without a battle.

Cairo, a new capital

Beside the older Egyptian city of Fustat, the Fatimids built a walled royal city and named it al-Qahira — Cairo, 'the Victorious'. Its palaces, mosques and markets made it a rival to Baghdad itself.

In 970 they founded al-Azhar as a mosque and teaching centre. Over the centuries it grew into one of the most famous centres of learning in the Islamic world.

Trade, tolerance and decline

Fatimid Egypt grew rich on trade between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean, and its ports welcomed merchants of many faiths. Jews and Christians held high posts in its government.

Later, weak caliphs, army struggles and the arrival of the Crusades shrank the state. In 1171 the vizier Saladin ended the Fatimid Caliphate and returned Egypt to Abbasid loyalty.

Did You Know?

Cairo's name, al-Qahira, means 'the Victorious' — and the planet Mars, called al-Qahir in Arabic, was shining brightly on the night the city's foundations were laid, or so the story goes.
