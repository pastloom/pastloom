History of Dolls

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Dolls are among the oldest toys in the world. Egyptian children played with flat wooden 'paddle dolls' more than 3,500 years ago, and dolls have been made in every land since — of clay, wood, rags, wax, porcelain and plastic.

A doll is more than a toy. Across history, dolls have been companions, teaching tools, religious figures and fashion models in miniature.

Dolls of the ancient world

Egyptian paddle dolls — flat wooden figures with hair of clay beads — were placed in tombs and played with in life. Greek girls played with clay dolls with jointed limbs, and, like Roman girls, dedicated their dolls to a goddess when they married.

In Japan, beautiful hina dolls became the heart of the Girls' Day festival, displayed in tiers in imperial dress — dolls not for play, but for blessing and display.

Dolls that taught and travelled

For centuries, most dolls were homemade: corn husks, carved wood, stuffed rags. A rag doll cost nothing and was loved no less for it.

In the 1600s and 1700s, Paris sent jointed 'fashion dolls' across Europe and to America, dressed in the newest styles, so that dressmakers abroad could copy French fashion stitch by stitch — dolls as messengers of taste.

The doll industry

In the 1800s, factories in France and Germany made dolls with porcelain heads, real hair and eyes that opened and closed. They were treasures of rich nurseries, while poor children still hugged rag dolls.

Plastic changed everything in the 1900s. Barbie, launched in 1959, became a global phenomenon, and dolls of every nation and story now fill toy shops. The materials changed — the child's imagination did not.

Did You Know?

In the 1700s, life-size fashion dolls travelled from Paris to London and even to America by ship — they were so important that they were sometimes allowed through blockades during wars.
