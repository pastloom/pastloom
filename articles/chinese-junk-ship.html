The Chinese Junk Ship

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The junk is China's classic sailing ship, used for more than a thousand years on rivers, coasts and open oceans. With its fan-like sails stiffened by bamboo battens, it could sail closer to the wind than most ships of its age.

Junks carried silk, tea and porcelain across Asia, formed the backbone of Zheng He's treasure fleets, and used safety inventions — watertight compartments and the stern rudder — long before Europe adopted them.

A clever design

A junk's sails are divided by bamboo battens into panels, like a fan. The design spreads the wind's force, lets sailors shorten sail quickly in a storm, and keeps working even with holes in the cloth.

Inside the hull, walls called bulkheads divide the ship into watertight compartments. If one section is holed, the others keep the ship afloat — an idea Marco Polo described with amazement, and European shipbuilders only adopted centuries later.

Ships of trade and empire

Junks of every size crowded China's ports: small river craft, coastal traders, and great ocean-going ships that sailed to Japan, South-East Asia and India on the monsoon winds.

The largest junks of all were the Ming treasure ships, with up to nine masts, which carried Zheng He's fleets to Africa. Chinese merchants in junks built trading communities across South-East Asia.

The junk in the modern world

Steamships and modern trade pushed the great ocean junks aside in the 1800s and 1900s, but smaller junks never disappeared.

Fishing junks still work Asian waters, and the red-sailed junk has become the symbol of Hong Kong — a reminder of the centuries when Chinese sailors ruled the eastern seas.

Did You Know?

When a replica junk named the Keying sailed from China to New York and London in 1846–48, crowds in both cities paid to walk her decks — she was one of the wonders of the age.
