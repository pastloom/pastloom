The Steam Engine

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The steam engine was the machine that powered the Industrial Revolution. First built to pump water from mines, it grew to drive factories, locomotives and ships — and for two hundred years it set the pace of the modern world.

Its story runs from Thomas Newcomen's clanking mine pump of 1712, through James Watt's great improvements, to the railway age. And it never truly ended: most electricity today is still generated by steam spinning a turbine.

Steam wakes up

Steam had fascinated inventors since ancient times — Hero of Alexandria spun a ball with steam jets in the 1st century CE — but it was a toy. The first useful steam engine was built in 1712 by Thomas Newcomen in England: a huge, lumbering machine that pumped water out of coal mines.

Newcomen's engine burned enormous amounts of coal, but at a coal mine fuel was almost free, and flooded mines were a desperate problem. For sixty years his engines, clanking day and night, drained Britain's mines deeper than ever before — and the deeper the mines, the more coal for the engines.

James Watt makes it efficient

In the 1760s a Scottish instrument-maker, James Watt, repaired a model Newcomen engine and saw its great waste: the cylinder was heated and cooled with every stroke. His answer, the separate condenser (patented 1769, sold from 1776 with his partner Matthew Boulton), kept the cylinder hot and cut fuel use by about three-quarters.

Watt and Boulton engines could now afford to work anywhere — and Watt added the sun-and-planet gears and flywheel that turned the engine's push into smooth rotation. Steam could drive machinery, not just pumps. The unit of power, the watt, still carries his name.

Steam moves the world

Soon steam escaped the factory. In 1804 Richard Trevithick ran the first steam locomotive on rails in Wales; in 1829 George Stephenson's Rocket won the famous Rainhill trials, and railways began to stitch countries together. Steamships — Fulton's Clermont steamed up the Hudson in 1807 — freed ships from the wind.

Factories no longer needed rivers for water power; they moved to coalfields and ports, and mill towns exploded into cities. Steam cut journey times from weeks to days, carried food and goods cheaply, and set the standard times and timetables we still live by. It was the driving heart of the Industrial Revolution.

The long reign ends — quietly continuing

In the late 1800s, rivals appeared: the internal combustion engine, the diesel, and electricity. By the mid-1900s steam locomotives had vanished from most railways, and factories ran on electric motors.

Yet steam never really retired. In power stations around the world, heat — from coal, gas, oil or nuclear fission — still boils water, and the steam still spins turbines to make most of humanity's electricity. Every time you switch on a light, there is a good chance a descendant of Newcomen's engine did the work.

Did You Know?

Most of the world's electricity is still made with steam — water boiled by coal, gas or nuclear heat spins the turbines in power stations today.
