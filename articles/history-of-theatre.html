History of Theatre

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Theatre — actors performing a story live before an audience — began in ancient Greece about 2,500 years ago, at festivals honouring the god Dionysus. The Greeks built great open-air theatres and invented tragedy and comedy.

Since then, every culture has shaped theatre in its own way: Sanskrit drama in India, Noh in Japan, travelling players in medieval Europe, Shakespeare's Globe, and the musicals and theatres of today.

Greece invents the play

At festivals for Dionysus in Athens, choruses sang and danced stories of gods and heroes. Tradition credits Thespis, about 534 BCE, as the first actor — the first person to step out of the chorus and speak as a character. That is why actors are still called thespians.

Writers such as Sophocles created tragedies like Oedipus Rex, exploring fate and human error, while Aristophanes mocked politicians in comedies. Stone theatres seating thousands were built into hillsides, with acoustics so fine that a whisper on stage could be heard in the back row.

Theatre around the world

Rome copied Greek theatre and added its own spectacles. In India, Sanskrit drama flourished — Kalidasa's plays of love and kingship are still performed. China developed opera forms mixing song, dance and acrobatics, and Japan created the slow, masked Noh theatre in the 1300s, and later the lively Kabuki.

In medieval Europe, churches staged Bible stories, and travelling players performed in market squares. When public theatres reopened in the 1500s, London boomed: the Globe opened in 1599, and Shakespeare's company played to crowds of 3,000 — kings, merchants and apprentices standing in the yard for a penny.

The modern stage

In the 1800s, gaslight and then electric light transformed the stage, and playwrights such as Ibsen and Chekhov wrote realistic plays about ordinary life. The 1900s added directors, new acting methods, musicals on Broadway and in London's West End, and experimental theatre everywhere.

Cinema and television took over much storytelling, but live theatre survives and renews itself — in grand opera houses, village halls, street festivals and school stages, wherever actors and audience share the same air, as they first did in Athens.

Did You Know?

At Shakespeare's Globe, the cheapest place cost one penny — you stood in the open yard, and if it rained, you got wet. These standing playgoers were called 'groundlings'.
