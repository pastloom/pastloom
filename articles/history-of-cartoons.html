History of Cartoons

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A cartoon is a drawing that makes people laugh — or think — with a joke, an exaggeration, or a clever idea. Cartoons appear in newspapers, magazines, books and now on screens everywhere.

The funniest cartoons often carry a serious point. For hundreds of years, cartoonists have poked fun at kings, politicians and everyday foolishness — sometimes when saying the same thing in words was dangerous.

Drawings that bite

Artists have drawn mocking pictures since ancient times, but cartoons grew powerful with printing. In the 1700s, English artists like William Hogarth sold prints that laughed at drunks, gamblers and corrupt politicians.

In 1843, London's Punch magazine began calling its funny drawings 'cartoons', and the name stuck. Cartoonists drew kings with huge heads and tiny bodies — and readers loved seeing the powerful made small.

Comic strips and picture jokes

By the 1890s, newspapers in America filled their Sunday pages with colour comic strips. The Yellow Kid, a grinning boy in a yellow nightshirt, became one of the first cartoon stars, and strips like Katzenjammer Kids followed.

Single-panel jokes and daily strips became part of breakfast around the world, and characters like Tintin and later manga heroes in Japan turned cartoons into whole books of adventures.

Cartoons learn to move

In the early 1900s, cartoonists discovered that many drawings shown quickly one after another seem to move — the animated cartoon was born, leading to the film cartoons of Disney and many others.

Today cartoons live in newspapers, books, television and phones. Political cartoonists still comment on every big event — proof that a small drawing can carry a very big idea.

Did You Know?

The Yellow Kid of 1895 was so popular that the sensational newspapers fighting over him gave us the phrase 'yellow journalism'.
