History of Moscow

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Moscow, the capital of Russia, is first mentioned in the year 1147 — a small wooden town on a river bend. From that small start it grew into the heart of the largest country on Earth.

At its centre stands the Kremlin, a red-walled fortress of palaces and golden-domed cathedrals, and beside it Red Square and the colourful St Basil's Cathedral.

The rise of Moscow

In 1147 Prince Yuri Dolgorukiy invited an ally to meet him in a small town called Moscow — the first mention of the city in history. Wooden walls rose on the hill where the Kremlin stands today.

In the 1200s Mongol armies conquered the Russian lands, and Moscow's princes slowly grew rich and strong by collecting tribute for the Mongols — then turned against them. Under Ivan the Great in the late 1400s, Moscow united the Russian lands, threw off Mongol rule, and its princes took the title Tsar. St Basil's Cathedral, with its swirling coloured domes, was built in the 1550s.

Fire, emperors and revolution

In 1712 Peter the Great moved the capital to his new city, St Petersburg, and Moscow became the old, beloved second city. In 1812 Napoleon's army occupied it — and Muscovites' fires, and the fire that swept the wooden city, forced the French into a disastrous retreat.

In 1918, after the Russian Revolution, Moscow became the capital again — this time of the Soviet Union. In 1941 Nazi armies came within sight of the city in the Battle of Moscow, but were driven back in the winter snow. Grand metro stations, built like underground palaces, showed Soviet pride.

Moscow today

When the Soviet Union ended in 1991, Moscow became the capital of Russia. Skyscrapers rose in a new business district, while the Kremlin and Red Square were protected as a World Heritage site.

Today Moscow is a vast city of about 13 million people, Russia's centre of government, business and culture — still growing outward from the little wooden town of 1147.

Did You Know?

Moscow's metro stations are so grand — with chandeliers, marble and mosaics — that they are nicknamed the palaces of the people.
