The Iron Age

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Iron Age was the time when people learned to make tools and weapons from iron. It began around 1200 BCE in the Middle East and slowly spread across Europe, Africa and Asia.

Iron was cheaper and more common than bronze, so ordinary farmers — not only kings — could own strong metal tools. This changed farming, war and everyday life.

Learning to work iron

Iron is harder to work than bronze. Smiths had to heat the ore in very hot furnaces, then hammer the spongy metal again and again to make it strong.

The Hittites of Anatolia were among the early masters of iron. After their empire fell around 1200 BCE, the knowledge spread widely.

Iron and everyday people

Because iron ore is found in many places, iron tools became affordable. Iron ploughshares cut heavy soils, iron axes cleared forests faster, and farming expanded into new lands.

In war, larger armies could now be equipped with metal weapons. Peoples such as the Celts spread across Europe with iron swords, fine jewellery and hillforts.

The end of prehistory

In many regions the Iron Age ends when written history begins — for example with the rise of Rome in Europe or the early empires of India and China.

Iron never disappeared. It remained the main metal of tools and weapons for more than 2,000 years, until the age of steel and steam.

Did You Know?

The Iron Age is not over everywhere at the same date — in some parts of the world, people moved from stone tools almost directly to iron brought by traders.
