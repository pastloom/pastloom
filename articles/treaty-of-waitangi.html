The Treaty of Waitangi

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Treaty of Waitangi is New Zealand's founding document. It was signed on 6 February 1840 between the British Crown and Maori chiefs at Waitangi, in the Bay of Islands.

The treaty was meant to bring peace: Britain would govern, and Maori would keep their lands and rights. But the English and Maori versions of the text said different things — and arguments about what was promised continue in New Zealand to this day.

Why a treaty was written

By the 1830s British settlers, traders and missionaries were arriving in New Zealand, and lawlessness worried both Maori chiefs and the British government. Britain decided to take control — but wanted Maori agreement first.

Lieutenant-Governor William Hobson arrived in January 1840. The treaty was drafted in English in a few days and translated into Maori overnight by the missionary Henry Williams and his son.

Two texts, two meanings

On 6 February 1840, after a day of debate, chiefs began signing the Maori text. In English, Maori gave 'sovereignty' to the Queen. But the Maori word used, kawanatanga, meant something more like 'governorship' — a weaker thing.

In return, the Maori text promised tino rangatiratanga: that chiefs would keep full authority over their lands, villages and treasures. Many chiefs believed they were gaining a governor to control settlers — not giving away their country.

Broken, then remembered

As settlers poured in, governments ignored the treaty. Land was bought unfairly or confiscated after the New Zealand Wars, and in 1877 a judge even called the treaty 'a simple nullity'.

In the 1970s Maori protests — including a famous land march on Parliament in 1975 — forced New Zealand to look again. The Waitangi Tribunal was created to investigate broken promises, and since then the Crown has settled many claims with land, money and formal apologies. The treaty is now honoured as the nation's founding promise.

Did You Know?

The first chief to sign, Hone Heke, later became famous for cutting down the British flagstaff at Kororareka four times in protest — he felt the treaty was already being broken.
