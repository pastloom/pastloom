The Agricultural Revolution

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Agricultural Revolution was the moment humans stopped only hunting and gathering, and began farming — planting crops and taming animals. It began about 12,000 years ago in the Middle East and, slowly, it changed human life more than almost any other event in history.

Farming gave us villages, cities, surplus food and eventually civilisation itself. Every town, every loaf of bread and every meal in the world today grows out of that first decision to plant a seed and stay to watch it grow.

The first farmers

For almost all of human history, people lived by hunting animals and gathering wild plants. Then, about 12,000 years ago, as the last Ice Age ended and the climate warmed, people in the Fertile Crescent — the crescent of good land running through modern Turkey, Syria and Iraq — began planting seeds on purpose: wheat and barley first, then peas and lentils.

They also tamed animals: sheep and goats, then cattle and pigs. Farming was not invented once. In China people domesticated rice and millet; in Mexico, maize and beans; in the Andes, potatoes; in Africa, sorghum and yams — separate inventions on separate continents.

What farming changed

Farming changed everything. Hunters must move with the seasons, but farmers stay in one place — so villages appeared, then towns, then cities. A good harvest produced more food than the farmers themselves needed, and that surplus fed potters, priests, soldiers, builders and kings.

From surplus came storage, trade, taxes, writing and civilisation itself. But the bargain had a price: early farmers worked harder than hunters, ate a narrower diet, and — crowded together with their animals — suffered new diseases. Skeletons suggest the first farmers were often shorter and less healthy than the hunters before them.

Farming spreads and improves

From its first centres, farming spread slowly — carried by migrating farmers and copied by their neighbours — until most of the world's people lived by it. For thousands of years the tools barely changed: the plough, the sickle, the ox and the waterwheel did the work.

Then, in Britain in the 1700s, a second agricultural revolution began: farmers rotated crops to rest the soil, enclosed fields, bred bigger animals, and sowed with machines like Jethro Tull's seed drill (1701). harvests grew, freeing workers to leave the land for the new factories — farming fed the Industrial Revolution.

The Green Revolution and today

In the 1900s came tractors, chemical fertilisers and irrigation on a giant scale. Then, in the 1960s, the Green Revolution: scientists led by Norman Borlaug bred short, strong wheat and rice that produced far more grain. Harvests in Mexico, India and beyond multiplied, and famines that had been predicted did not happen.

Today farming feeds eight billion people — more than a hundred times the population of the first farming age. The questions have changed: how to farm without exhausting the soil, the water and the climate. But every meal still begins with the same ancient invention, made beside the first wheat fields 12,000 years ago.

Did You Know?

Farming began independently in at least seven different parts of the world — people in the Middle East, China, Mexico and Africa all invented it separately, without knowing of each other.
