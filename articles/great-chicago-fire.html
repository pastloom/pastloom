The Great Chicago Fire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Great Chicago Fire burned for two days in October 1871 and destroyed the heart of Chicago, one of America's fastest-growing cities. About 300 people died, and a third of the city — some 100,000 people — lost their homes.

Yet Chicago refused to die. From the ashes rose a new city of stone and steel, including the world's first skyscrapers. The fire is remembered as both a terrible disaster and the beginning of modern Chicago.

A dry city of wood

Chicago in 1871 was built mostly of wood — wooden houses, wooden barns, even wooden pavements and sidewalks. After weeks without rain, the whole city was as dry as tinder.

On the night of 8 October, a fire started in a small barn behind the O'Leary family's cottage. A newspaper story later claimed Mrs O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern, but the reporter admitted he invented details, and the true cause is still unknown.

Two days of fire

Strong winds drove the flames from street to street faster than firefighters could follow. The fire jumped the Chicago River twice and burned the business district, the courthouse and grand hotels.

It finally died out early on 10 October, helped by rain. Survivors picked through smoking ruins where 100,000 people had lost everything. Help poured in from around the world, including books from Britain to restart the city's library.

The city that rose again

Chicago rebuilt with astonishing speed, this time under strict new rules favouring brick and stone. Architects experimented with steel frames, and in 1885 Chicago opened the Home Insurance Building, often called the first skyscraper.

Twenty-two years after the fire, the city proudly hosted the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, showing the world its rebirth. Fire Prevention Week is still held every October in America in memory of the blaze.

Did You Know?

On the very same night as the Chicago fire, an even deadlier fire destroyed the town of Peshtigo in Wisconsin, killing more than 1,000 people — but Chicago's fame overshadowed it.
