History of Iran

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Iran, long known in the West as Persia, has one of the oldest continuous histories in the world. More than 2,500 years ago, Cyrus the Great founded the Persian Empire — then the largest empire on Earth.

Iran became a centre of Islam, poetry and science, and its language and culture spread far beyond its borders. In 1979 a revolution turned the country into an Islamic republic.

Ancient Persia

Farmers and kingdoms lived in Iran thousands of years before the Persians. The Elamites built cities in the south-west while Egypt's pyramids were still young.

In 550 BCE Cyrus the Great united the Persians and Medes and built an empire from Turkey to India, famous for its roads, tolerance and organised rule. Alexander the Great conquered it, but Persian culture survived under new dynasties such as the Parthians and Sasanians.

Islamic Iran

Arab armies brought Islam in the 600s, yet Iran kept its own language — Persian (Farsi) — and gave the Islamic world great poets like Ferdowsi, Rumi and Hafez, and scholars in medicine and astronomy.

After the Mongol invasions destroyed much of the land, the Safavid Empire (1501–1736) reunited Iran, made Shia Islam its faith, and built the beautiful city of Isfahan with its blue-tiled mosques.

Modern Iran

In the 1900s the Pahlavi shahs tried to modernise Iran quickly, and oil — found in 1908 — made the country important to the world. Many people opposed the shah's harsh rule and Western influence.

In 1979 huge protests forced the shah to leave, and Ayatollah Khomeini led Iran to become an Islamic republic. A long war with Iraq followed in the 1980s, and Iran's relations with the West remain tense today.

Did You Know?

A clay cylinder of Cyrus the Great, kept today in the British Museum, is sometimes called the first charter of human rights, because it records his policy of respecting the customs and religions of conquered peoples.
