The French Resistance

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

When Germany defeated France in 1940 and occupied most of the country, many French men and women refused to accept defeat. They formed the French Resistance — secret networks that spied on the enemy, printed forbidden newspapers, rescued Allied airmen and attacked German forces.

Resistance work was desperately dangerous: members faced arrest, torture and execution if caught. Yet by 1944 the Resistance had grown into a large movement that played a real part in freeing France.

Small beginnings

In June 1940, from London, General Charles de Gaulle broadcast an appeal to the French to keep fighting. At first only a handful answered: students who chalked slogans, printers who produced illegal newspapers with names like Combat and Libération, railway workers who passed information to London.

Escape lines guided shot-down Allied airmen through France and over the Pyrenees mountains into Spain — a journey of hundreds of kilometres, hiding by day and walking by night.

Moulin and the united Resistance

In 1943, de Gaulle's envoy Jean Moulin brought the quarrelling Resistance groups together in a National Council of the Resistance. Weeks later Moulin was arrested, tortured and killed without betraying his comrades; he became the movement's greatest martyr.

As Germany began seizing young Frenchmen for forced labour, thousands fled to the hills and formed armed bands — the maquis. The Resistance was no longer a handful of heroes but a hidden army.

Liberation

When the Allies landed in Normandy in June 1944, the Resistance rose: railway lines were blown up across France, delaying German reinforcements, and intelligence from its networks guided the liberators.

In August 1944 Paris rose in revolt and was liberated. After the war, France honoured its resisters — while quietly remembering that the full story, of courage and collaboration side by side, was more complicated than the legend.

Did You Know?

Resistance couriers — very often women, because they attracted less suspicion — carried secret messages hidden in bicycle frames, shopping baskets and even loaves of bread.
