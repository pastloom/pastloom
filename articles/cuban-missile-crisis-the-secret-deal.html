The Cuban Missile Crisis: The Secret Deal That Ended It

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Our main article on the Cuban Missile Crisis tells how Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba brought the world to the edge of war in October 1962. This companion article tells the hidden half of the story: the secret bargain that ended the crisis.

In public, the Soviet Union simply agreed to take its missiles home. In private, the United States quietly promised to remove its own missiles from Turkey — a trade that was kept secret for about twenty years.

Two letters, two messages

On 26 October, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev sent a long, emotional letter: he would remove the missiles if America promised not to invade Cuba. The next day, a second, harder letter arrived in public, adding a new demand — American missiles must also leave Turkey.

President Kennedy's advisers were split. His brother Robert suggested a clever answer: reply to the first letter as if the second had never arrived — while quietly settling the Turkey question in private.

The Saturday night meeting

On the evening of 27 October — the most dangerous day of the crisis, when an American spy plane had been shot down over Cuba — Robert Kennedy secretly met Soviet ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin in Washington.

He delivered the bargain: the Soviet missiles must go, America would promise not to invade Cuba, and the Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be removed within months. But there was one strict condition: the Turkey trade could never be announced. If Moscow spoke of it, the offer would be denied and withdrawn.

What the bargain built

Khrushchev accepted, and on 28 October he announced the withdrawal on Moscow radio. By April 1963, the American Jupiters were gone from Turkey, explained away as old weapons being replaced.

Both leaders had been frightened by how close the world came to disaster. In 1963 they set up a direct hotline between Washington and Moscow and signed the first treaty limiting nuclear tests — direct children of the secret deal.

Did You Know?

Only a small circle of American officials knew about the Turkey trade. Even many senior leaders learned the full truth only years later, when memoirs and documents finally revealed it.
