History of Myanmar

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Myanmar, also known for many years as Burma, lies between India, China and Thailand. Its plains are watered by the great Irrawaddy River.

Its first great kingdom, Pagan, built thousands of temples that still cover the plain of Bagan. Britain ruled Myanmar as a colony until independence in 1948.

Pyu cities and the glory of Pagan

The Pyu people built walled cities in the Irrawaddy valley more than 1,500 years ago, trading with India and China and adopting Buddhism.

In 1044 King Anawrahta united the valley into the Pagan Kingdom. For over 200 years its kings and people built thousands of temples at Bagan — a forest of brick pagodas that still stretches to the horizon. Mongol invasions ended Pagan's power in 1287.

New dynasties and wars with neighbours

New kingdoms rose and fell. The Toungoo dynasty reunited the country in the 1500s and briefly ruled the largest empire in South-East Asia's history. The Konbaung dynasty followed in 1752, founding Mandalay as its last capital in 1857.

Myanmar often fought its neighbour Siam (Thailand). But a new enemy was coming: the British in India fought three wars against Burma between 1824 and 1885 and took the whole country.

British Burma, independence and hard years

Britain ruled Burma as part of British India until 1937, then as a separate colony. Yangon (Rangoon) became a great rice-exporting port, and many Indians came to work there.

The nationalist hero Aung San led the country to independence on 4 January 1948 but was assassinated months before it. After a coup in 1962 the army ruled for nearly fifty years. A move towards democracy in the 2010s raised hopes, but a military coup in 2021 brought conflict back. Myanmar's many peoples still hope for a peaceful, united future.

Did You Know?

At its height, the plain of Bagan held more than 10,000 Buddhist temples, pagodas and monasteries. Over 2,000 still stand today — one of the greatest sights in Asia.
