Titanic

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Titanic was the most famous ship in history — the largest and most luxurious liner of her age, called 'unsinkable' by the press. On her very first voyage, in April 1912, she struck an iceberg in the North Atlantic and sank, killing about 1,500 of the 2,224 people aboard.

Her sinking was not just a tragedy but a turning point: it rewrote the safety rules of the sea — lifeboats for everyone, round-the-clock radio, and an ice patrol that still works today.

The ship of dreams

At the start of the 1900s, crossing the Atlantic was big business, and Britain's White Star Line built three giant sister ships — Olympic, Titanic and Britannic — to rule the route. The Titanic was the largest moving object humans had ever built: 269 metres long, with a swimming pool, gymnasium, grand staircase and electric lifts.

The press called her 'practically unsinkable' — she had a double bottom and 16 watertight compartments, and could float with any four flooded. She carried 20 lifeboats — more than the law required, but enough for only about half the people aboard. Nobody thought she would need them all.

The maiden voyage

The Titanic left Southampton on 10 April 1912, calling at Cherbourg in France and Queenstown in Ireland before heading for New York. Aboard were some of the richest people in the world in first class — millionaires like John Jacob Astor and Benjamin Guggenheim — and hundreds of emigrants in third class, travelling to start new lives in America.

On Sunday 14 April, the ship received several wireless warnings of icebergs ahead. The night was bitterly cold, the sea glassy calm — the worst conditions for spotting ice, since no waves broke at an iceberg's base. Speed stayed high: about 22 knots.

The sinking

At 11:40 p.m. the lookouts spotted an iceberg dead ahead. The ship turned, but too late: the berg scraped along her starboard side, buckling plates and popping rivets across six compartments — one more than she could survive. The 'unsinkable' ship was doomed within minutes, though it took 2 hours and 40 minutes to sink.

Confusion and hope slowed the evacuation; many early lifeboats left half-empty. There were not enough boats anyway — the law, written for smaller ships, had never imagined the Titanic. At 2:20 a.m. on 15 April she broke in two and sank. About 1,500 people died in the freezing water; the liner Carpathia rescued some 710 survivors from the boats.

What the Titanic changed

The disaster shocked the world and changed the sea forever. Within two years, new rules required enough lifeboats for everyone aboard, 24-hour radio watches, and the International Ice Patrol to track North Atlantic icebergs — which still guards those waters today. No passenger ship has been lost to an iceberg since.

The wreck was found in 1985, nearly 4 km down, and the Titanic's hold on the imagination has never loosened: books, expeditions, and the 1997 film — for years the highest-grossing ever made. The ship that symbolised human confidence became its most famous warning about pride.

Did You Know?

The Titanic's wreck was not found until 1985, 73 years after it sank. It lies in two pieces on the ocean floor, slowly being eaten by iron-loving bacteria — scientists expect it to disappear within decades.
