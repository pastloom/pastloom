How Roman Aqueducts Worked

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A Roman aqueduct was a water channel that ran gently downhill for many kilometres, carrying spring water into a city using gravity alone — no pumps, no engines.

This article explains how the system worked, step by step: from the spring in the hills to the fountains, baths and homes of the city.

Finding the spring and setting the slope

Engineers began by finding a clean spring or stream in the hills, higher than the city. Then surveyors marked a route that fell very gently and steadily — sometimes only a few tens of centimetres per kilometre.

They used simple tools: a long water level called a chorobates, plumb lines and sighting rods. If the slope was too steep, the water would rush and damage the channel; too flat, and it would stop flowing.

The journey of the water

Most of the channel ran underground or at ground level in a covered stone trough, keeping the water clean, cool and safe from thieves. Where a valley blocked the way, the channel crossed on the famous arches — or, if the valley was deep, in pipes that dipped down and up again, working by water pressure.

Along the route, settling tanks let sand sink out, and inspection shafts let workers climb down to clean the channel of the stone-like crust that slowly built up inside.

Arriving in the city

In the city, the water poured into a distribution tank called a castellum. From there, pipes carried it first to the public fountains — so everyone had drinking water — then to the public baths, and last to private houses, whose owners paid a tax based on the size of their pipe.

Rome's water commissioner Frontinus wrote proudly about the system about 97 CE. Stealing water by secretly tapping a pipe was a serious crime, punished with fines.

Did You Know?

Roman water pipes were often stamped with the name of the owner — archaeologists have used these stamps to work out who lived in some of the houses of Pompeii.
