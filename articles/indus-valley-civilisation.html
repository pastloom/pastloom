Indus Valley Civilisation

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Indus Valley Civilisation was one of the first great civilisations in the world. It grew along the Indus River, in the lands of present-day Pakistan and north-west India, more than 4,500 years ago.

Its two largest cities were Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Their streets were laid out in neat straight lines, their houses had bathrooms and covered drains, and their traders sold goods as far away as Mesopotamia.

Planned cities

The Indus cities were built to a plan. Streets crossed at right angles, houses were made of baked brick, and almost every house had a bathroom and a drain.

Mohenjo-daro even had a large public bath, called the Great Bath, and granaries for storing grain. Few ancient cities were so carefully organised.

Seals and trade

Indus traders made small stone seals carved with animals and picture signs. The signs may be a form of writing — but no one has been able to read them yet.

Indus seals and beads have been found in Mesopotamia, showing that their ships and caravans traded across a very long distance.

Why the cities faded

Around 1900 BCE, the great cities began to shrink. Rivers may have changed course, rains may have failed, and trade may have dried up — experts still argue about the exact cause.

But the people did not vanish. Farming villages carried on in the region, and many of their crafts and customs lived on in later Indian life.

Did You Know?

Mohenjo-daro had covered drains running under its streets — one of the first drainage systems in the world, built more than 4,000 years ago.
