The Big Bang

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Big Bang is the scientific story of how the universe began — about 13.8 billion years ago, everything we know was compressed into an incredibly hot, dense state, which has been expanding and cooling ever since into galaxies, stars, planets and us.

It sounds like myth, but it rests on hard evidence: galaxies are rushing apart exactly as the theory predicts, and the whole sky glows faintly with the cooled light of that first fireball.

A universe that moves

For most of history, people assumed the universe had always existed, still and eternal — even Einstein believed that. But in the 1910s–20s, astronomers noticed something odd: the light of distant galaxies was stretched towards red, meaning they were racing away from us. The farther a galaxy, the faster it fled.

In 1927 the Belgian priest-physicist Georges Lemaître proposed running the film backwards: if everything is flying apart, long ago everything must have been together — the universe began from a single 'primeval atom'. In 1929 Edwin Hubble's measurements confirmed the expansion. The universe had a birthday.

The hot beginning

The early universe was not an explosion in space but the expansion of space itself, everywhere at once. In its first seconds it was unimaginably hot and dense — a fireball in which even atoms could not form. As it expanded and cooled, energy became particles, particles became hydrogen and helium, and after about 380,000 years the fog cleared and light could travel freely for the first time.

Gravity then did slow, patient work over billions of years: gas clouds collapsed into stars and galaxies, stars forged heavier elements in their cores and scattered them in supernova explosions. Everything around you — the carbon in your body, the iron in your blood — is stardust from those ancient furnaces.

The echo of creation

The strongest proof came by accident. In 1965, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, testing a sensitive antenna, found a faint microwave hiss coming from every direction — impossible to remove, even after cleaning pigeon droppings from the dish. It was the cosmic microwave background: the cooled afterglow of the hot early universe, predicted years earlier by Big Bang theorists.

Satellites have since mapped that afterglow in exquisite detail, pinning the universe's age to about 13.8 billion years and revealing the seeds from which galaxies grew. A rival 'steady state' theory, which held the universe had no beginning, could not explain the background glow — and faded away.

What we still don't know

The Big Bang model is now the backbone of cosmology — but it is unfinished. Galaxies spin too fast for their visible matter, implying invisible 'dark matter'; the expansion is accelerating, driven by a mysterious 'dark energy' that makes up most of the universe. And the theory cannot yet say what the universe expanded from, or whether time itself began at the beginning.

Thirteen point eight billion years after a hot, dense start, one species on one small planet has measured the age of everything. The story of how we learned the universe had a beginning is one of science's greatest detective stories — and it is still being written.

Did You Know?

The Big Bang theory was first proposed by a Catholic priest, Georges Lemaître. Einstein at first resisted it — he had added a 'cosmological constant' to his equations to keep the universe still, and later called it his greatest blunder.
