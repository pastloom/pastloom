The Tiwanaku Empire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Tiwanaku Empire was one of the first great empires of South America. It grew around the city of Tiwanaku, near Lake Titicaca in modern Bolivia, high in the Andes mountains.

From about 600 to 1000 CE, Tiwanaku's power and religion spread across Bolivia, Peru, Chile and Argentina — centuries before the Inca built their more famous empire in the same mountains.

A capital beside the great lake

Tiwanaku stood near the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, the highest large lake in the world. Its builders raised great stone platforms, sunken courts and the Akapana, a huge stepped pyramid.

Its most famous treasure is the Gate of the Sun — a doorway carved from a single block of stone, covered with a calendar-like god surrounded by winged figures.

Farming the cold highlands

Life at nearly 4,000 metres is cold and hard, yet Tiwanaku farmers fed a large city. In the lake shallows they built raised beds with canals between them, which protected potato crops from frost.

Caravans of llamas carried food, wool and goods between the highlands and distant colonies in warm valleys — linking mountain, desert coast and jungle edge into one system.

Collapse and Inca memory

Around 1000 CE, a long drought may have dried the raised-bed farms, and the empire faded. Its cities emptied, but local peoples kept farming and worshipping in its lands.

When the Inca rose centuries later, they treated Tiwanaku as a sacred place — in their stories, the creator god made the first people from stone beside Lake Titicaca.

Did You Know?

Some stones at Tiwanaku weigh more than 100 tonnes, and they were fitted together so carefully that the ruins still puzzle engineers today.
