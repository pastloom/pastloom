The Appian Way

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Appian Way was Rome's first great road — the 'Queen of Roads'. Built from 312 BCE, it ran south from Rome to Capua and later all the way to Brindisi, the port for ships to Greece and the East.

Roman roads were built to last: many were used for 2,000 years, and you can still walk and cycle on the Appian Way's original stones today.

Building the Queen of Roads

In 312 BCE, Rome was fighting in southern Italy and needed to move soldiers and supplies fast, in all weathers. The censor Appius Claudius ordered a road like none before: straight where possible, drained, paved with hard stone, and built on a deep foundation.

Roman road-builders dug a trench, filled it with layers of stone, gravel and concrete, and topped it with large, closely fitted paving blocks. Side ditches carried away rain, and the surface curved slightly so water ran off.

Life along the road

Every Roman mile — about 1,480 metres, from mille passus, a thousand paces — a milestone told travellers the distance and who maintained the road. Inns, stables and posting stations served messengers and officials.

Outside Rome's walls, where burial inside the city was forbidden, wealthy families built grand tombs along the Appian Way, so every traveller would pass them and remember their names.

The road in history

Armies marched down the Appian Way to wars in the south and east; grain, wine and marble travelled up it to Rome. In 71 BCE the road witnessed Rome's cruelty too, when 6,000 captured followers of the slave leader Spartacus were crucified along its length.

Later, Christian catacombs were dug beneath its edges. Today the first stretch from Rome is a peaceful park of pine trees, tombs and ancient stones, protected as one of Italy's treasures.

Did You Know?

The word 'mile' comes from the Roman mille passus — a thousand double-paces of a marching soldier. A Roman mile was about 1,480 metres, a little shorter than the modern mile.
