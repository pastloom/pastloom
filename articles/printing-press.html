The Printing Press

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The printing press is the machine that made books cheap — and in doing so, changed the world. Before it, every book was copied by hand and cost a fortune. After Johannes Gutenberg built his press in Germany around 1440, a single workshop could print hundreds of copies in the time a scribe needed for one.

Within fifty years, presses in hundreds of towns had printed millions of books. Knowledge escaped from monasteries and universities into ordinary hands, fuelling the Reformation, the scientific revolution, and eventually newspapers and mass education.

A world of handwritten books

Before printing, every book in Europe was copied by hand. A single Bible could take a team of scribes years to finish, and it cost as much as a house. Books were treasures locked in monasteries and universities, and most people never saw one.

Printing itself was older than Gutenberg. In China, printers carved whole pages into woodblocks, and by the 1000s Chinese and Korean craftsmen were already experimenting with movable type — Korea printed a book with metal type in 1377, decades before Gutenberg. But in Europe, the pieces had not yet come together.

Gutenberg's workshop in Mainz

Around 1440, in the German city of Mainz, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg combined those pieces into a system. He carved letter moulds so hundreds of identical metal letters could be cast quickly, set them in frames, used a thick oil-based ink that stuck to metal, and pressed paper against the type with a adapted wine press.

His masterpiece, finished about 1455, was a magnificent printed Bible — about 180 copies, each looking almost like a handwritten book. Gutenberg himself made little money and lost his workshop in a lawsuit, but his system could not be uninvented.

The press spreads across Europe

Printers carried the new craft from city to city. By 1480 there were presses in more than 200 towns; by 1500, perhaps ten million printed books existed in Europe — more than all the handwritten books produced in the previous thousand years.

Printed books were still not cheap, but they were ten or a hundred times cheaper than before. For the first time, students, merchants and lawyers could own books. Reading quietly spread from a tiny elite to a growing public.

The book changes the world

Cheap print changed everything it touched. When Martin Luther challenged the Church after 1517, his arguments were printed in thousands of pamphlets and read across Germany within weeks — the Reformation was the first great event powered by the press.

Scientists could now share exact diagrams, maps and tables with readers in other countries, and errors could be corrected in the next edition. Newspapers, novels and eventually mass education all grew from Gutenberg's workshop. Many historians rank the printing press among the most important inventions in human history.

Did You Know?

By 1500, only about fifty years after Gutenberg, printing presses had produced more books than all the scribes of the previous thousand years combined.
