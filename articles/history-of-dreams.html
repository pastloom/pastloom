History of Dreams

Every night, the sleeping brain builds strange little films called dreams. People have wondered what dreams mean for as long as we have records — ancient kings kept dream interpreters, and one of the oldest books in the world is a guide to dreams.

Today scientists can watch the dreaming brain at work, but dreams still keep some of their ancient mystery.

Messages from the gods

In ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt, dreams were serious news. Kings dreamed of wars and harvests, priests slept in temples hoping for a healing dream, and the Epic of Gilgamesh turns on warning dreams.

Egyptian scribes wrote dream books listing what images meant — dreaming of bread was good, of beer even better. In the Bible and the Quran, dreams carry guidance and prophecy to figures like Joseph and Jacob.

Dream books and interpreters

The Greek writer Artemidorus travelled the ancient world collecting dreams and their outcomes, then wrote a five-book guide that people consulted for over a thousand years. Dream interpreters worked in markets across Asia and Europe.

In 1900, Sigmund Freud argued that dreams express hidden wishes and fears. His ideas shaped art, film and literature — even where scientists later disagreed with his details.

What science says about dreams

In 1953 researchers discovered REM sleep: a stage when the eyes dart about, the brain is almost as active as when awake, and vivid dreams unfold. We pass through this stage several times a night.

Modern science links dreams with memory and emotion — the brain sorting the day's experiences. Why we dream is still debated, which would not surprise the ancient interpreters at all.

Did You Know?

The Egyptian dream book written about 4,000 years ago lists dreams in two columns — good and bad — with the bad ones written in red ink.
