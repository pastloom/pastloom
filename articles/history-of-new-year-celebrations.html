History of New Year Celebrations

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

New Year is one of the oldest celebrations on Earth. People in Babylon were already holding New Year festivals more than 4,000 years ago — though their year began in spring, not in January.

Different cultures still begin the year on different days: 1 January, the Chinese New Year in winter, Nowruz at the spring equinox, and many more. Each one marks the same hope — a fresh start.

New Year in Babylon and Rome

Babylon's Akitu festival lasted eleven days at the spring planting. The king's power was renewed, statues of gods were carried through the streets, and people promised the gods they would repay debts and return borrowed things — the first New Year resolutions.

Rome at first began its year in March. In 45 BCE Julius Caesar's new calendar fixed 1 January as the start, honouring Janus, god of doors and beginnings, whose two faces looked to the past and the future at once.

Many new years, one world

Because calendars differ, the world celebrates many new years. Chinese New Year follows the lunar calendar, with family dinners, red envelopes and dragon dances. Persian Nowruz begins exactly at the spring equinox and is more than 3,000 years old.

Jewish Rosh Hashanah, the Islamic New Year, and Hindu festivals like Diwali and Ugadi each open a year in their own season. When Britain and its colonies finally adopted 1 January in 1752, they were among the last in Europe to do so.

Midnight, fireworks and fresh starts

Modern New Year is a global television moment: fireworks over Sydney Harbour, the falling ball in New York's Times Square (first dropped in 1907), and bells ringing in temples and churches.

The customs are mixed old and new — Spain's twelve grapes eaten at midnight, Denmark's leaping from chairs, Scotland's first-footing visits. Under them all sits the Babylonian idea: end the year honestly, and begin the next one better.

Did You Know?

September was once a common New Year month — Ethiopia still begins its year in September, and its calendar is about seven years behind the Western one.
