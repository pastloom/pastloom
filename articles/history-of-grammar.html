History of Grammar

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Grammar is the set of rules that holds a language together — how words are built and how sentences are made. Every language has grammar, but writing the rules down is a craft with its own long history.

The first great grammar was written in ancient India more than 2,300 years ago, and it was so precise that modern linguists still admire it. Grammar books later shaped schools across the whole world.

Panini and the science of language

In ancient India, the scholar Panini described the Sanskrit language in a system of nearly 4,000 compressed rules — a machine-like grammar of astonishing accuracy.

Sanskrit grammar was a high science because sacred texts had to be recited perfectly. Panini's work, from about the 4th century BCE, is still studied today.

Grammar in Greece, Rome and schoolrooms

Greek scholars sorted words into classes — noun, verb and the rest — and Roman teachers copied their system for Latin. The textbooks of Donatus and Priscian ruled European classrooms for a millennium.

For centuries, 'grammar' simply meant Latin grammar, and grammar schools were schools that taught Latin. Only in the 1500s did grammars of living languages like English begin to appear.

Rules and rebellion

The 1700s brought rule-books for English, such as Robert Lowth's of 1762, which fixed ideas like 'never end a sentence with a preposition' in the minds of teachers.

Modern linguists mostly describe language as it is actually spoken, rather than commanding how it should be. The argument between rule-makers and describers — begun in Panini's time — continues in every classroom today.

Did You Know?

Panini's grammar is so exact and logical that computer scientists have compared it to a programming language — written more than two thousand years before computers.
