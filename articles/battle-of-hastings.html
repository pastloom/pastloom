The Battle of Hastings

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Battle of Hastings, fought on 14 October 1066, is one of the most famous battles in English history. Duke William of Normandy crossed the sea from France and defeated King Harold II of England, whose army had just marched the length of the country.

William — remembered as William the Conqueror — became king, and the Norman Conquest that followed changed England's language, laws and rulers forever. It was the last time England was successfully invaded by a foreign army.

Two claims to one crown

When King Edward the Confessor died in January 1066 without a child, Harold Godwinson was crowned king of England. But William of Normandy claimed Edward had promised him the throne, and Harold had once sworn to support him.

William prepared a great invasion fleet. Meanwhile Harold faced another invasion in the north, by the king of Norway. He defeated the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge on 25 September — then heard that William had landed in the south.

A long day on Senlac Hill

Harold's exhausted army marched south at speed and took position on a ridge, forming a solid shield wall. William's men — archers, infantry and knights on horseback — attacked uphill all day without breaking it.

Twice the Normans pretended to flee, drawing English soldiers out of the wall in pursuit, then turned and cut them down. Late in the day Harold was killed — legend says by an arrow in the eye — and without their king, the English army finally broke.

England transformed

William was crowned king in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066. Over the following years the Normans replaced England's lords with their own, built great stone castles across the land, and brought the French language to the court.

In 1086 William ordered the Domesday Book, a survey of almost everything in his new kingdom. English slowly absorbed thousands of French words — one reason modern English has such a huge vocabulary. The story of the conquest is told in pictures on the famous Bayeux Tapestry.

Did You Know?

William's invasion fleet included about 700 ships, and his knights even brought ready-made wooden forts in pieces to build as soon as they landed.
