History of Uzbekistan

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Uzbekistan, in the heart of Central Asia, holds two of the most beautiful cities of the old world — Samarkand and Bukhara, jewels of the Silk Road.

The conqueror Timur made Samarkand his capital in the 1300s and filled it with blue-tiled wonders. Uzbekistan became independent from the Soviet Union in 1991.

Sogdiana and the coming of Islam

Long ago this land was Sogdiana and Bactria — farming oases and trading towns between Persia and China. Alexander the Great marched through, and Sogdian merchants became the great middlemen of the early Silk Road.

In the 700s Arab armies brought Islam, and the region flowered: scholars like al-Khwarizmi, whose name gives us the word 'algorithm', and the physician Ibn Sina were born here.

Timur and the golden age of Samarkand

Mongol armies wrecked the cities in the 1200s, but in 1370 the conqueror Timur — Tamerlane — chose Samarkand as his capital and rebuilt it in glory, bringing artists and craftsmen from across his empire.

Under Timur's descendants, Samarkand and Herat enjoyed a renaissance of poetry, painting and astronomy. The prince Ulugh Beg built an observatory and charted the stars with amazing accuracy. Later, Uzbek khanates ruled from Bukhara and Khiva.

Russian rule and independence

In the 1800s the Russian Empire took the region, and in Soviet times Uzbekistan became a cotton-growing republic — with a heavy cost, as rivers were drained and the Aral Sea shrank into desert.

Uzbekistan became independent on 1 September 1991. Today visitors come from around the world to see the Registan square in Samarkand and the old streets of Bukhara, while the country works to save what is left of the Aral Sea.

Did You Know?

The word 'algorithm' comes from the name of the scholar al-Khwarizmi, who was born in Khiva, in today's Uzbekistan, more than 1,200 years ago.
