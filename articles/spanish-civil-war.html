The Spanish Civil War

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Spanish Civil War was fought from 1936 to 1939 between Spain's elected Republican government and army rebels, called Nationalists, led by General Francisco Franco.

The war split Spanish families and towns in two. Other countries poured in weapons and soldiers, and many people see the war as a dark rehearsal for the Second World War.

A country divided

Spain in the 1930s was split between those who wanted change — workers, and supporters of the Republic — and those who feared it: landowners, much of the army and the Church.

In July 1936, army officers rose against the government. They expected a quick victory, but workers and loyal soldiers resisted, and the rising became a full civil war.

The world joins in

Hitler's Germany and Mussolini's Italy sent Franco planes, guns and troops. German aircraft bombed the town of Guernica in 1937, killing many civilians — an attack the artist Pablo Picasso turned into one of the most famous paintings in the world.

The Soviet Union sent aid to the Republic, and about 35,000 volunteers from many countries — the International Brigades — travelled to Spain to fight fascism. Britain and France, hoping to avoid a bigger war, refused to help the Republic.

Franco's victory

Better supplied and more united, the Nationalists ground their way to victory. Madrid fell in March 1939, and the war ended with perhaps half a million dead and hundreds of thousands driven into exile.

Franco ruled Spain as a dictator for 36 years, until his death in 1975. Only then did Spain begin its road back to democracy.

Did You Know?

Ernest Hemingway and George Orwell both went to Spain during the war — Orwell fought and was wounded, and wrote about it in his book Homage to Catalonia.
