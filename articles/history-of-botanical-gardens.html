History of Botanical Gardens

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A botanical garden is a garden where plants are collected, named and studied like books in a library. The first true botanical gardens were created in Italy in the 1540s, so doctors could learn which plants healed and which poisoned.

Over time they became far more than medicine gardens: explorers filled them with plants from every continent, and today they protect rare plants from disappearing forever.

Medicine gardens of Italy

In the 1500s, a doctor's most important tools were plants — but a wrong plant could kill instead of cure. Universities needed gardens where students could study living plants carefully, in every season.

Luca Ghini created the first such garden at Pisa in 1544, and Padua followed in 1545. Padua's garden still grows in its original circle today, with a palm tree planted in 1585 that is still alive.

Plants from every corner of the world

As ships crossed the oceans, botanical gardens became the headquarters of plant science. Explorers sent back seeds and cuttings — tea, rubber, coffee, cinchona (for malaria medicine) — and gardens tested how to grow them in new lands.

To keep tropical plants alive in cold Europe, gardeners built ever bigger glasshouses. Kew Gardens near London, growing from 1759, became the most famous of all, with its great Palm House of iron and glass.

Gardens that guard the future

Today there are thousands of botanical gardens, and their job has changed again. Many plant species are threatened by deforestation and climate change, so gardens grow rare plants as living insurance.

Seed banks linked to gardens, such as Kew's Millennium Seed Bank, freeze seeds from wild plants around the world. The medicine garden of 1544 has become an ark for the plant kingdom.

Did You Know?

The world's oldest botanical garden tree, the palm at Padua planted in 1585, was already famous centuries ago — the German writer Goethe studied it in 1786 and built ideas about how plants grow from watching its leaves.
