History of Insurance

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Insurance is a shared shield: many people each pay a little money into a common pot, so that when disaster strikes one of them — a fire, a shipwreck, an illness — the pot pays for the damage.

No one can stop storms and fires. Insurance is humanity's clever answer: spread the risk so thin that no single disaster can ruin a family.

Sharing the risk of the sea

The Code of Hammurabi, nearly 4,000 years old, already let a merchant cancel a loan if his goods were stolen — an early form of insurance. Greek and Roman shipowners shared losses when a ship went down.

In the 1300s, merchants of Genoa in Italy wrote the first true insurance contracts for ships and cargo. If the ship arrived, the insurer kept the fee; if it sank, he paid for the loss.

Fire, coffee houses and Lloyd's

In the 1680s, London's ship merchants gathered at Edward Lloyd's coffee house to swap shipping news — and to insure each other's voyages. That coffee house grew into Lloyd's of London, the most famous insurance market in the world.

After the Great Fire of London in 1666, fire insurance companies appeared, each with its own fire brigade and a metal 'fire mark' on insured houses. Insurance stepped off the ships and into the streets.

Insurance for everyone

In the 1700s and 1800s, mathematicians learned to calculate risk from tables of births and deaths, and life insurance became a serious business. Friendly societies let working families insure against sickness and burial costs for pennies a week.

In the 1900s, governments added national insurance — pensions, unemployment and health cover for all. Today insurance quietly stands behind almost everything, from a family car to a rocket launch.

Did You Know?

Lloyd's of London began in a coffee house. Ship owners and merchants met there for shipping news, and began sharing the risk of voyages over their coffee — the meetings grew into the world's most famous insurance market.
