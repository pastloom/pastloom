History of Traffic Lights

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Red, amber, green — the whole world knows what traffic lights mean. These simple coloured signals were invented because busy streets became dangerous chaos when horses, carts, bicycles and then cars all fought for the same road.

The first traffic light appeared in London in 1868 — and it exploded. The lights we trust today grew from that risky beginning.

A policeman and a gas lamp

In 1868, London's streets around Parliament were jammed and deadly. Railway engineer J. P. Knight borrowed the railway's signal idea: a tall post with red and green gas lamps and moving arms, worked by a policeman.

It worked well — until January 1869, when leaking gas exploded and injured the operator. The experiment stopped, and London waited almost 60 years for traffic lights to return.

Electric lights in America

The motor car made signals essential. In 1914 Cleveland, Ohio installed the first electric red-and-green traffic light. In Detroit in 1920, police officer William Potts added the amber 'get ready' light, creating the three-colour pattern the world still uses.

Inventor Garrett Morgan, who also created a safety hood for firefighters, patented an early three-position signal in 1923 and sold the rights to General Electric — helping the design spread across America.

Lights that think

For decades, lights changed on fixed timers, wasting time on empty roads. Modern systems count cars with sensors and cameras and adjust themselves second by second, keeping whole cities moving.

The colours never changed: red, amber and green are understood by drivers everywhere on Earth — one of the few truly universal languages humans have invented.

Did You Know?

The world's first traffic light stood outside the Houses of Parliament in London — but after it exploded, Britain distrusted traffic lights so much that it did not install electric ones until 1927.
