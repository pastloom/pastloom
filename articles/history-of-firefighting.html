History of Firefighting

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Fire was the terror of every old city: wooden houses, open flames and narrow streets meant one spark could burn a whole district. Fighting it is one of the oldest public services in the world.

From Rome's night watchmen to today's fire engines, this is the story of the people who run towards the flames everyone else flees.

Rome's watchmen of the night

After a great fire in 6 CE, Emperor Augustus organised the Vigiles — thousands of men who patrolled Rome at night with buckets, axes and pumps, watching for flames and catching criminals too.

When Rome fell, organised firefighting vanished for a thousand years. Medieval towns relied on church bells, bucket chains of neighbours, and pulling down houses to starve the fire of fuel.

Insurance brigades and steam power

The Great Fire of London in 1666 destroyed much of the city. Afterwards, insurance companies formed their own brigades — but they protected only houses carrying their company's fire mark, sometimes watching uninsured houses burn.

Hand pumps grew stronger, and in 1829 a steam-powered fire engine arrived in London. Cities slowly accepted that fire protection was a public duty, and municipal brigades replaced the insurance teams.

The modern firefighter

The 1900s brought motor engines, tall ladders, hydrants, breathing apparatus and radios. Firefighters became rescuers too — at road crashes, floods and collapsed buildings, not only fires.

Dalmatian dogs, once used to guard horse-drawn engines, survive as the service's mascot. The heart of the job is unchanged since the Vigiles: be ready, day and night, for the alarm bell.

Did You Know?

After the Great Fire, London's insurance brigades would check a burning house for their company's fire mark plaque before unrolling their hoses — no mark could mean no water.
