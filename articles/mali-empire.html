Mali Empire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Mali Empire was one of the richest states of the medieval world. From about 1235, its emperors — the mansas — ruled a vast West African realm built on gold: Mali's mines supplied much of the gold of Europe and the Islamic world.

Its most famous ruler, Mansa Musa, is still often called the richest man in history, and its city of Timbuktu became a legend of wealth and learning, with libraries of hundreds of thousands of books.

Sundiata, the Lion King

In the early 1200s the Sosso king Sumanguru oppressed the Mandinka people of the upper Niger. Their prince Sundiata Keita — the 'Lion King', whose story griots (hereditary storytellers) still sing — gathered allies and defeated Sumanguru at the Battle of Kirina, about 1235. From that victory rose the Mali Empire.

Sundiata founded his capital at Niani and organised an empire of provinces ruled by appointed governors under the mansa, the emperor. His charter of rights, the Kouroukan Fouga, is still remembered in Mali as one of the world's oldest declarations of social rules. Islam, long present through traders, spread among the court while traditional beliefs continued in the countryside.

Mansa Musa and the golden pilgrimage

Mali's most famous emperor, Mansa Musa, came to the throne about 1312. Mali's goldfields — Bambuk and Bure — produced perhaps half the gold in circulation in the Old World, and Musa was fabulously rich. In 1324 he made the hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, and the world noticed.

His caravan crossed the Sahara with tens of thousands of followers and camel-loads of gold. In Cairo he gave and spent so generously that the price of gold there reportedly fell for a decade. News of the rich king of Mali reached Europe, where mapmakers drew him enthroned, holding a golden nugget — an image of African wealth that endured for centuries.

Timbuktu: city of gold and books

Returning from Mecca, Musa brought scholars and an Andalusian architect, and raised Timbuktu from a trading camp into a city of learning. Under Mali and its successor Songhai, Timbuktu's Sankore and other schools taught astronomy, law, mathematics and medicine; its libraries eventually held hundreds of thousands of manuscripts, copied and traded like gold.

The empire's wealth ran on two trades: gold northward across the Sahara in exchange for salt — worth nearly its weight in gold in the south — and the river trade of the Niger. Mali at its height stretched from the Atlantic coast to the bend of the Niger, some 1.2 million square kilometres.

Decline and the griots' memory

After Musa, weaker mansas, civil wars and pressure from neighbours — the Tuareg seized Timbuktu in 1433, and the rising Songhai Empire took the east — shrank Mali through the 1400s. A Moroccan invasion destroyed Songhai in 1591, and the old imperial order of the Niger bend ended; a rump Mali state lingered in the south into the 1600s.

But Mali never vanished from memory. The griots kept Sundiata's epic alive for 800 years until it was written down in the 1900s, and in 1960 the new nation of Mali took the empire's name — choosing, like Zimbabwe, to name a modern country after a medieval African glory.

Did You Know?

Mansa Musa's pilgrimage caravan reportedly included 60,000 people and camels carrying hundreds of pounds of gold — he gave away so much in Cairo that the price of gold there stayed depressed for years.
