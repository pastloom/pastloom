Julius Caesar

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Julius Caesar was a Roman general and politician whose victories and ambition destroyed the Roman Republic and paved the way for the Roman Empire. His conquest of Gaul made him rich, famous and beloved by his soldiers; his march on Rome made him its master.

As dictator he reformed the calendar, the Senate and the government of the provinces. But his power frightened Rome's senators, and on 15 March 44 BCE a group of them stabbed him to death — a murder that plunged Rome into thirteen more years of civil war.

The rising general

Caesar was born in 100 BCE into a noble but not very rich Roman family. He was clever, charming and ruthlessly ambitious, and he rose through Roman politics by spending borrowed money on games and buildings that made him popular with the crowd.

In 60 BCE he joined Pompey and Crassus, Rome's two other strong men, in an alliance called the First Triumvirate. With their backing Caesar won the command he wanted: governor of Gaul, with an army of his own — the springboard for everything that followed.

Conquering Gaul

For nine years, from 58 to 50 BCE, Caesar campaigned across Gaul — roughly modern France, Belgium and parts of Germany and Switzerland. He defeated tribe after tribe, crossed the Rhine into Germany, and twice sailed to Britain, an island at the edge of the known world.

The wars were brutal: perhaps a million Gauls died and another million were enslaved, and the final revolt under Vercingetorix ended at the siege of Alesia in 52 BCE. But Gaul became a Roman province, Caesar became immensely rich, and his legions were loyal to him personally. His own book about the wars, still read today, made sure Rome heard his version of the story.

Civil war and dictatorship

Back in Rome, the Senate — led by Pompey, now Caesar's enemy — ordered him to give up his army. Instead, in January 49 BCE, Caesar crossed the little Rubicon river into Italy with his legion. 'The die is cast,' he is said to have remarked. It was civil war.

Within four years he had defeated his enemies in Italy, Greece, Egypt, Africa and Spain. Pardoning many former foes, he became dictator for life. He reformed the calendar into the Julian calendar we almost still use, extended Roman citizenship, planned great buildings and colonies, and filled the Senate with his supporters — which many senators saw as kingship in all but name.

The Ides of March

On 15 March 44 BCE — the Ides of March — Caesar entered a Senate meeting unguarded. Around sixty conspirators, led by Brutus and Cassius and including men he had pardoned, surrounded him with daggers hidden in their togas and stabbed him twenty-three times. He fell dead at the foot of Pompey's statue.

The murderers called themselves liberators, but they had no plan for what came next. Rome erupted. Caesar's adopted heir, the young Octavian, and his general Mark Antony hunted the conspirators down, and after thirteen years of civil war Octavian emerged as Augustus, the first emperor. The republic Caesar was accused of killing died with his killers.

Did You Know?

The month of July is named after Julius Caesar — and his Julian calendar, with its leap years, was used in parts of Europe for more than 1,600 years.
