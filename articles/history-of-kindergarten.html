History of Kindergarten

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Kindergarten is the school for the youngest children, where learning happens through play, songs, stories and building with blocks. The word is German and means 'children's garden' — a place where children grow like plants, with a teacher as the gardener.

Kindergarten was invented in Germany in the 1830s by Friedrich Fröbel. His idea that small children learn best by playing changed education all over the world.

Froebel and the children's garden

Friedrich Fröbel believed small children were not tiny adults. They learned, he said, by touching, building, singing and playing — not by sitting still with books. In 1837 he opened a school for young children in the German town of Bad Blankenburg.

In 1840 he gave it the name Kindergarten, a garden of children. He designed special play materials called Gifts: soft balls, wooden cubes, cylinders and blocks that children could stack, split and rebuild to discover shapes and numbers by themselves.

An idea travels the world

Froebel's students carried his idea across Europe, even though Prussia banned kindergartens for a time in 1851, fearing new ideas. German families who moved to America took kindergarten with them.

Margarethe Schurz opened America's first kindergarten in Wisconsin in 1856, teaching in German. In 1860 Elizabeth Peabody opened the first English-language one in Boston, and public schools slowly adopted kindergartens from the 1870s.

Kindergarten today

Today, kindergarten is the normal first step of school in most countries, usually for children aged four to six. Classrooms still use Froebel's core ideas: learning through play, blocks, drawing, songs and time outdoors.

Modern kindergartens add letters, numbers and early reading, but the heart of Froebel's garden remains — a gentle place where children grow at their own pace, guided rather than pushed.

Did You Know?

The wooden blocks in almost every kindergarten today descend directly from Froebel's 'Gifts' — and they shaped history in a surprising way: the famous architect Frank Lloyd Wright said playing with Froebel blocks as a child inspired his life's work.
