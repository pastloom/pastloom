History of Trampolines

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A trampoline is a sheet of strong fabric stretched on springs in a frame, made for bouncing high into the air. It was invented in 1936 by an American gymnast, George Nissen, who got the idea from watching circus acrobats fall into safety nets.

From circus training it spread to pilots, astronauts, back gardens — and finally to the Olympic Games.

Bouncing through the ages

People bounced for fun long before 1936. Inuit communities held the blanket toss, throwing a person high from a walrus-skin blanket during celebrations — partly for joy, partly to spot game and whales from the air.

Circus performers, too, used springy beds and nets in comic and acrobatic acts. But there was no standard bouncing apparatus that anyone could buy and train on.

Nissen builds the trampoline

George Nissen was a teenage gymnast and diver in Iowa. Watching trapeze artists drop into nets and bounce back up, he wondered: why not build a frame for bouncing on purpose? In 1936, working in his garage with coach Larry Griswold, he stretched canvas over a steel frame — the first trampoline.

Nissen demonstrated his invention tirelessly, even performing with a kangaroo on a trampoline in Central Park. During the Second World War, trampolines trained pilots in orientation and body control, and later astronauts used them too.

Back gardens and the Olympics

In the 1950s trampolines jumped into public life: trampoline centres opened across America, and backyard trampolines became a classic family purchase — along with new safety rules about nets, pads and one jumper at a time.

Competitive trampolining grew under gymnastics, and in 2000 it entered the Olympic Games at Sydney. Nissen, by then 86, lived to see his garage invention become an Olympic sport.

Did You Know?

To promote his invention, George Nissen once bounced on a trampoline alongside a trained kangaroo named Victoria in New York's Central Park — a photograph that newspapers around the world happily printed.
