The Plague of Athens

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 430 BCE, in the second year of the Peloponnesian War, a terrible disease struck Athens, the greatest city of ancient Greece. Packed inside its walls with refugees from the countryside, the city had no defence against the sickness, and it raged for years.

The historian Thucydides, who caught the disease and survived, left a chilling description of it — one of the most famous accounts of an epidemic ever written. Athens never fully recovered, and its great leader Pericles was among the dead.

A city behind its walls

Athens' war strategy was to abandon the countryside to Sparta's army, bring everyone inside the city walls and the long walls to the sea, and rely on its navy and imported food. The plan made military sense — but it crowded tens of thousands of refugees into a hot, unprepared city.

The disease arrived by ship through Athens' port, Piraeus, reportedly after spreading from Ethiopia through Egypt. It began with a burning heat in the head, red eyes and foul breath, then attacked the chest and stomach.

Thucydides tells the story

Thucydides described the plague with a doctor's eye, hoping future readers might recognise it if it ever returned. Doctors could not help, he wrote, and the disease killed the strong and the weak alike.

Worse than the sickness, he said, was the collapse of law and kindness. People, seeing the pious and the wicked die alike, stopped fearing gods or laws. Bodies were left unburied or thrown onto strangers' funeral pyres, and the dying filled the temples where they had taken shelter.

Athens weakened forever

Among the dead were thousands of soldiers Athens could not replace — and Pericles himself, the city's leader for a generation, died in 429 BCE. Athens fought on bravely for more than twenty years, but it never regained its former strength, and it finally lost the war to Sparta in 404 BCE.

Scientists still debate what the disease was. Studies of teeth from a mass grave found traces suggesting typhoid fever, but other scholars argue for smallpox, measles or even an Ebola-like fever. After nearly 2,500 years, the Plague of Athens keeps its central mystery.

Did You Know?

Thucydides wrote that birds and animals that fed on dead bodies either stayed away from them — or died after eating, and that the city's dogs vanished during the plague.
