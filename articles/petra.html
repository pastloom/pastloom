Petra

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Petra is an ancient city carved into pink and red rock cliffs in the desert of Jordan. Its most famous building, the Treasury, has a grand doorway cut straight into the stone of a canyon wall.

Petra was the capital of the Nabataeans, an Arab people who grew rich by controlling trade routes across the desert about 2,000 years ago.

The city in the rock

Visitors reach Petra by walking through the Siq, a narrow, winding gorge with cliffs rising on both sides. At the end, the path suddenly opens onto the Treasury — a temple-like front carved into glowing pink rock.

Beyond it lies a whole city: a theatre, temples, royal tombs, streets and houses, most of them cut into the cliffs. At its height, perhaps 30,000 people lived in Petra.

Masters of desert water

The Nabataeans were brilliant engineers of water. In a land of rare rain, they built dams, channels and hidden tanks that caught every drop of flood water and stored it safely.

This hidden water let their caravans cross the desert carrying frankincense, myrrh, silk and spices from Arabia and Asia to the Mediterranean. Petra grew rich on this trade.

Decline and rediscovery

Rome took over Petra in 106 CE, and trade slowly moved to other routes. A great earthquake in 363 CE damaged the city, and over the centuries it was abandoned, known only to local Bedouin people.

In 1812 the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, disguised as a pilgrim, became the first modern outsider to describe it. Today Petra is Jordan's most famous treasure and one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

Did You Know?

Petra's Treasury is not a treasury at all — it was most likely a royal tomb. Its name comes from a Bedouin legend that an Egyptian pharaoh hid his treasure in the stone urn at its top.
