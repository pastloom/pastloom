History of New York City

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

New York City is the largest city in the United States — and one of the youngest great cities in this library. Four hundred years ago it was a small Dutch trading post called New Amsterdam, on land long home to the Lenape people.

Today New York is a world centre of money, media, theatre and ideas, famous for its skyline of skyscrapers and for the Statue of Liberty standing in its harbour.

New Amsterdam and New York

The Lenape people lived around the harbour for thousands of years before Europeans arrived. In 1524 the explorer Giovanni da Verrazzano sailed into the bay, and in 1626 the Dutch founded New Amsterdam at the tip of Manhattan Island.

In 1664 English ships took the town without a battle, and it was renamed New York. It grew as a busy port, and in the 1770s it stood at the centre of the American Revolution — George Washington's army lost the city to Britain, but won the war, and New York briefly served as the first capital of the United States.

The immigrant city

The Erie Canal, opened in 1825, linked New York to America's heartland and made it the country's greatest port. Millions of immigrants arrived by ship — many through Ellis Island — from Ireland, Italy, Germany, Eastern Europe and beyond, each group adding languages, foods and neighbourhoods.

In the late 1800s the city answered crowding by building upward. Steel frames and lifts created the skyscraper, and towers like the Empire State Building (1931) made New York's skyline the most famous in the world. Five boroughs joined into one great city in 1898.

New York today

New York became the home of the United Nations after the Second World War, and the centre of world finance on Wall Street. On 11 September 2001, terrorist attacks destroyed the twin towers of the World Trade Center and killed nearly 3,000 people — the city mourned, and rebuilt, raising One World Trade Center on the site.

Today more than 200 languages are spoken in New York's streets. From a Lenape shore to a Dutch village to a giant of glass and steel, no city on Earth has changed so much in so short a time.

Did You Know?

The Statue of Liberty arrived from France in 1885 in 350 separate pieces packed in crates, and was assembled on its island like a giant puzzle.
