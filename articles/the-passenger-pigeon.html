The Passenger Pigeon

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The passenger pigeon was once perhaps the most numerous bird on Earth. Its flocks in North America were so huge that they darkened the sky for hours — billions of birds moving together like a river in the air.

Yet people hunted them without limit for cheap meat. In less than a hundred years the species fell from billions to zero. The last one, named Martha, died in a zoo in 1914.

Rivers of birds

Early naturalists could hardly believe what they saw. One flock was described as a column of birds so long that it took hours to pass overhead, and the sound of their wings was like thunder.

The pigeons nested together in giant colonies. A single nesting forest could hold millions of birds, with dozens of nests in one tree.

The great hunt

In the 1800s, professional hunters followed the flocks using the new railways and telegraph wires. Birds were shot, netted and smoked out of trees, then shipped by the barrel to city markets.

At the same time, the great eastern forests the pigeons needed were being cut down. By the 1890s, the rivers of birds had shrunk to scattered handfuls.

From billions to none

Martha, the last passenger pigeon, died in Cincinnati Zoo in 1914. Her body was frozen in ice and sent to a museum, where it is still kept.

The shocking loss helped wake America up. New laws to protect migrating birds followed, and the passenger pigeon became a permanent warning: no species is too common to lose.

Did You Know?

When Martha, the last passenger pigeon, died in 1914, her body was frozen inside a block of ice and sent to a museum in Washington.
