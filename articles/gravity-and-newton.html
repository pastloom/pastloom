Isaac Newton and the History of Gravity

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Gravity is the force that pulls everything towards everything else — the reason apples fall, the Moon circles the Earth, and our feet stay on the ground. For most of history it was simply 'how things are'. Then, in 1687, Isaac Newton explained it with one law that worked for the whole universe.

Newton's idea — that the same force moves a falling apple and the planets — was one of the greatest leaps science ever made. Two centuries later Einstein deepened it, and gravity still guides every spacecraft we fly.

Falling through history

Long before Newton, thinkers wondered why things fall. Aristotle taught that heavy objects seek their natural place at the centre of the universe — an explanation that satisfied people for nearly two thousand years. In the 1500s–1600s, Galileo dropped balls, rolled them down slopes and showed that objects fall at the same accelerating rate whatever their weight.

Meanwhile Kepler discovered that the planets move in ellipses, and astronomers measured the heavens with new precision. The pieces of the puzzle lay on the table: falling apples obeyed rules, and so did planets. Nobody had yet seen they were the same rules.

Newton and the apple

Isaac Newton was a quiet, intense young Cambridge scholar. In 1665–66, when plague closed the university, he worked at home in Woolsthorpe — and in that 'year of wonders' he developed calculus, studied light, and began thinking about gravity. He later told the story himself: watching an apple fall in the garden, he wondered whether the force pulling it down might reach as far as the Moon.

In 1687, urged on by his friend Edmond Halley, Newton published the Principia Mathematica. Its law of universal gravitation said every object attracts every other with a force that grows with their masses and weakens with the square of the distance between them. One equation described the apple, the Moon, the tides and the planets.

The clockwork universe

Newton's law was a triumph. It predicted Halley's comet, which returned on schedule in 1758; it explained the tides and the shape of the Earth; and in 1846 astronomers used it to find the planet Neptune — discovered with a pencil, from wobbles in the orbit of Uranus, before any telescope saw it.

For two hundred years, physics seemed complete: the universe ran like clockwork, and Newton was its prophet. Engineers and navigators built an industrial world on his equations — and in the space age, mission planners still use them to thread probes between planets.

Einstein and beyond

Cracks appeared slowly: Mercury's orbit drifted in a way Newton's law could not quite explain. In 1915 Albert Einstein published general relativity, describing gravity not as a force but as the curving of space and time by mass. Newton's law emerged as an excellent approximation — superb for apples and rockets, imperfect at the extremes.

Einstein's version predicted bending starlight (confirmed in 1919), black holes, and gravitational waves — ripples in space itself, detected at last in 2015. Gravity, the oldest familiar force, remains the deepest mystery: the one force physics still cannot unite with the rest.

Did You Know?

Newton was born in the year Galileo died, and Einstein published his gravity theory in the year Newton's ideas had reigned for 228 years — the torch of gravity passed between giants.
