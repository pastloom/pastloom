The Eruption of Mount St Helens

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

On the morning of 18 May 1980, Mount St Helens, a volcano in Washington State, USA, erupted in the deadliest volcanic explosion in American history. The whole north side of the mountain slid away in the largest landslide ever recorded, and a blast of hot gas and ash flattened forests for miles.

Fifty-seven people died, including a scientist watching the volcano from a ridge nearby. The eruption taught scientists how to watch volcanoes more closely and save lives in the future.

The mountain that grew a bulge

For two months before the eruption, small earthquakes shook Mount St Helens and steam blasts burst from its crater. Scientists watched as the north flank of the mountain swelled outward by up to 1.5 metres a day, pushed by magma rising inside.

Authorities closed the area closest to the volcano, though some residents and loggers were allowed into an outer zone. A young volcanologist, David Johnston, manned an observation post on a ridge about 10 km away, reporting the mountain's every move.

Thirty seconds that changed a landscape

At 8:32 am on 18 May, an earthquake shook the bulge — and the entire north side of the mountain simply slid away. Johnston radioed his famous last message: Vancouver! Vancouver! This is it! He was never found.

With the pressure released, the volcano exploded sideways. A cloud of hot gas, ash and rock raced across the countryside at more than 500 km per hour, snapping huge trees like matchsticks. Ash fell across several states, turning day to night in towns hundreds of kilometres away, and mudflows of melted snow and debris destroyed bridges and homes.

Nature returns, science advances

In the years after 1980, scientists were amazed by how quickly life returned: plants pushed through the ash, elk grazed the blast zone, and a new lava dome slowly grew inside the crater.

The disaster changed volcano science. Mount St Helens became one of the best-monitored volcanoes on Earth, and lessons learned there — about bulges, sideways blasts and early warnings — have helped predict eruptions and evacuate people safely in many countries since.

Did You Know?

Innkeeper Harry Truman, 83, refused to leave his lodge by Spirit Lake despite all warnings, saying the mountain and lake were part of him. He died in the eruption, and his story became one of its most famous.
