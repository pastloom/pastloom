History of Castles

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A castle is a strong home built for war. In the Middle Ages, kings and lords built castles to guard their land, control roads and rivers, and shelter people during attacks.

The first castles were quick forts of wood and earth. Later, builders raised great castles of stone with towers, moats and walls so thick that some still stand after 800 years.

Wooden forts on hills of earth

The Norman lords who conquered England in 1066 built castles in a hurry: a wooden tower on a steep earth mound (the motte), with a fenced yard (the bailey) below. A small team could raise one in weeks.

Wood burns, so lords soon rebuilt in stone. The stone keep — a massive square tower with walls several metres thick — became the castle's last refuge and its proudest sight.

Castles at their greatest

By the 1200s, the finest castles had rings of walls, one inside another, round towers that gave archers a view in every direction, and gates protected by portcullises and holes for dropping stones on attackers.

Edward I of England built a chain of mighty castles in Wales, such as Conwy and Caernarfon. In the Crusader lands, Krak des Chevaliers guarded a mountain pass and could hold out for years.

Why castles faded

A castle's worst enemy arrived with gunpowder. Heavy cannons could batter down even thick stone walls, as armies proved in the 1400s and 1500s.

Kings also grew stronger and kept their own armies, so private castles were less needed. Many became ruins or country houses — but their towers still teach us how people lived, ruled and fought in the Middle Ages.

Did You Know?

Some castle stairs twist so that a right-handed swordsman climbing up could not swing his sword freely, while the defender above him could — the stairs themselves were a weapon.
