The Garamantes

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Garamantes were a remarkable people who built a kingdom in the middle of the Sahara Desert, in what is now south-west Libya. They flourished from about 500 BCE to 700 CE.

With no rivers and almost no rain, they farmed the desert by digging hundreds of kilometres of underground water tunnels — one of the great engineering achievements of the ancient world.

A kingdom in the sand

When Herodotus wrote about the Garamantes around 430 BCE, he described a numerous people who hunted in four-horse chariots. Rock carvings across the Sahara still show those chariots.

Their towns stood around desert oases, with mud-brick houses, temples, cemeteries and a capital at Garama — a real city in one of the driest places on Earth.

Water from underground

The Garamantes' secret was the foggara: tunnels dug by hand from distant hills, carrying groundwater by gravity to their fields. Vertical shafts for digging and air lined the routes like dotted lines across the desert.

At their height, the tunnels ran for hundreds of kilometres in total and watered fields of wheat, barley, grapes, figs and date palms — a green kingdom fed by hidden water.

Desert trade — and the water runs out

The Garamantes controlled routes between the Mediterranean coast and inner Africa. Roman goods — wine, oil, glass and pottery — flowed south through their markets.

Rome alternately traded and fought with them, and Roman expeditions marched deep into the Sahara with Garamantian guides. Their chariots and later camels made them masters of the desert crossing.

The foggaras tapped water laid down thousands of years earlier, and it was not replaced. As the water table slowly fell, tunnels had to be dug ever deeper.

By about 700 CE the kingdom faded, its fields abandoned to the sand. From the air today, the dotted lines of its dead tunnels still cross the desert — the fingerprints of the Garamantes.

Did You Know?

Seen from an aeroplane, the Garamantes' tunnel lines still stripe the Libyan desert — some archaeologists counted about 600 foggaras, with shafts stretching over 100,000 in total.
