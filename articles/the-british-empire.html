Empires & Kingdoms

The British Empire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The British Empire was the largest empire in history. By the early 1900s, Britain — a rainy island off Europe — ruled about a quarter of the world's land and a quarter of its people, from Canada and India to Australia, Africa and the Pacific. People called it 'the empire on which the sun never sets', because somewhere in it, it was always daylight.

It was built over three centuries by trade, sea power, settlement and conquest — and it ended within a generation after the Second World War, as its colonies won independence. Its legacy is everywhere: the English language, parliaments, railways and cricket, alongside the scars of conquest and slavery.

Trade posts become an empire

The empire began not with armies but with merchants. In 1600 the East India Company was founded to trade with Asia; in 1607 English settlers founded Jamestown in America; traders and planters followed in the Caribbean. Through the 1600s–1700s, Britain's growing navy and merchant fleet fought the Dutch, French and Spanish for the world's trade routes.

Two events changed everything. In 1757 the East India Company's army won the Battle of Plassey and began conquering India — a private company taking a subcontinent. And in 1763 Britain won the Seven Years' War, taking Canada from France. Britain lost the American colonies in 1783, but turned east: India, and soon Australia (settled from 1788), became the empire's new centre of gravity.

The Victorian empire

In the 1800s Britain industrialised first, and its empire industrialised with it: steamships, railways, telegraphs and repeating rifles shrank distances and widened the power gap. The East India Company ruled India until the great Rebellion of 1857, after which the British Crown took over directly and Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India in 1876.

The empire spread across Africa in the 'Scramble' of the 1880s–90s, when European powers carved up the continent in a generation: Egypt, Sudan, Nigeria, Kenya, Rhodesia and South Africa all came under the flag, alongside Malaya, Burma, Hong Kong and dozens of Pacific islands. Millions of Britons emigrated to Canada, Australia and New Zealand, building settler societies that transformed those lands — at devastating cost to their Indigenous peoples.

Empire at war

The empire fought the First World War as a world coalition: over a million soldiers came from India alone, with Canadians, Australians, New Zealanders, South Africans and Africans serving on every front. The war stretched the empire to its greatest size — it took over German and Ottoman territories — but also exhausted it and stirred independence movements everywhere.

The Second World War strained it further: Britain stood alone in 1940–41, Singapore fell to Japan in 1942 in the empire's worst defeat, and Indian, African and Caribbean troops again served worldwide. Victory in 1945 left Britain victorious, bankrupt — and facing peoples who had fought for freedom abroad and demanded it at home.

The end of empire

In 1947 India and Pakistan won independence — the empire's heart gone in a single year, amid a partition that displaced millions. Then came the 'wind of change': Ghana in 1957, Nigeria in 1960, Kenya in 1963, and colony after colony through the 1960s–70s, until Hong Kong's return to China in 1997 closed the story. Most former colonies joined the Commonwealth, a voluntary association of nations.

The British Empire's legacy is argued over to this day. It spread English to some 1.5 billion speakers, along with parliamentary government, common law and global trade networks — and it also rested on conquest, slavery (abolished across the empire only in 1833), famines and divided peoples. Both truths belong in its history.

Did You Know?

At the empire's height, Britain ruled so much of the Earth that the sun was always shining on some part of it — even today, with its remaining territories, the sun technically never sets on British soil.
