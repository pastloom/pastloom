The Ming Dynasty: Treasure Ships and Great Walls

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Ming dynasty ruled China from 1368 to 1644. Founded by a poor peasant who became a monk and then a rebel leader, it drove out the Mongols and made China strong and proudly Chinese again.

The Ming gave the world the Forbidden City, rebuilt the Great Wall in stone, and sent the largest wooden ships ever built on voyages as far as Africa.

A peasant becomes emperor

Zhu Yuanzhang was born into poverty and orphaned by famine and plague. He became a Buddhist monk, then joined rebels fighting the Mongol Yuan dynasty.

In 1368 he took the throne as the Hongwu Emperor. He rebuilt farming, planted millions of trees, and ruled with an iron hand from his capital at Nanjing.

Treasure ships and a new capital

The Yongle Emperor moved the capital to Beijing and built the Forbidden City as his palace. He also sent the eunuch admiral Zheng He on seven great voyages between 1405 and 1433.

Zheng He's treasure ships — far larger than the ships Columbus would later sail — visited South-East Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa, trading and showing China's power. Then, suddenly, the voyages stopped and the great fleets were left to rot.

The wall and the fall

Fearing the Mongols, the Ming rebuilt the Great Wall in stone and brick — the famous wall tourists walk on today is mostly Ming work.

But silver shortages, famine, corruption and rebellion weakened the dynasty. In 1644 rebels took Beijing and the last Ming emperor took his own life; the Manchus then conquered China and founded the Qing dynasty.

Did You Know?

Ming emperors were buried in splendid tombs near Beijing guarded by a road of stone animals and officials — the Sacred Way — which still stands today.
