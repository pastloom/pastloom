History of Bicycles

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The bicycle is the most efficient machine ever made for moving a person — with your own legs, it carries you three or four times faster than walking. It began in 1817 as a wooden machine with no pedals at all.

Through strange shapes — including the famous penny-farthing with its giant front wheel — the bicycle became the safe, chain-driven machine we know, and gave ordinary people, especially women, a new freedom to travel.

Running machines and pedals

In 1817, the German baron Karl Drais built a two-wheeled wooden machine you pushed along with your feet. Crowds laughed, then copied it — the idea of balancing on two wheels had arrived.

In the 1860s, French makers added pedals to the front wheel. To go faster, makers made that wheel bigger and bigger, creating the penny-farthing — thrilling, but a fall from its high saddle could break bones, and riders in long skirts could hardly ride at all.

The safety bicycle changes everything

In 1885, John Kemp Starley's Rover 'safety bicycle' put two equal wheels, a chain driving the rear wheel, and a low saddle together. Dunlop's air-filled tyres in 1888 made it comfortable.

The safety bicycle was cheap, reliable and open to everyone. In the 1890s a cycling craze swept the world: clubs, races, maps and road repairs all followed the bicycle — and in China, sturdy roadsters like the Flying Pigeon later became the classic bicycle of everyday life.

Freedom on two wheels

For women, the bicycle meant independent travel for the first time — and helped change dress, as long skirts gave way to practical cycling clothes. Campaigners called the bicycle a freedom machine.

Cars later pushed bicycles aside in some countries, but they never vanished. Today bicycles carry commuters, racers, children and delivery riders — and electric motors now lend tired legs a helping push.

Did You Know?

In the 1890s, American women's rights campaigner Susan B. Anthony said the bicycle had done more to free women than anything else in the world.
