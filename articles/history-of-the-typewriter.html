History of the Typewriter

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The typewriter put neat, fast writing on every desk. Before it, business letters and books were written out by hand in ink — slow work that only trained clerks could do well.

Its keyboard, designed in the 1870s to stop metal arms from jamming, is still under your fingers today: the strange order of letters we call QWERTY.

Writing by machine

Inventors had dreamed of writing machines since 1714. Early models were slow curiosities — one was built to let blind people write, and the Danish 'writing ball' of 1870 looked like a metal hedgehog.

In Milwaukee, America, Christopher Latham Sholes and his partners finally made a practical design: keys struck inked ribbon through metal arms called typebars, printing one letter at a time.

Remington and QWERTY

The gun-makers E. Remington and Sons began selling the Sholes machine in 1874. Its keyboard arranged letters in the QWERTY order — not for speed of fingers, but to keep busy typebars apart so they would not clash and jam.

Offices loved the machine's neat, readable letters. Mark Twain bought one proudly, and typing pools of trained typists — many of them women entering office work for the first time — became a feature of every big company.

The keyboard outlives the machine

Electric typewriters in the 1900s made typing lighter and faster; IBM's golf-ball Selectric of 1961 became an office classic. Then computers arrived, and the typewriter itself faded away within a generation.

Yet QWERTY survived the machine it was designed for. Every phone and laptop keyboard still carries Sholes's 1870s solution to a problem of clashing metal arms.

Did You Know?

Mark Twain paid $125 for his Remington typewriter in 1874 — and claimed to be the first author to send a publisher a typewritten manuscript.
