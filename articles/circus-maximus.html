Circus Maximus

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Circus Maximus was the largest sports stadium of the ancient world — a huge racetrack in Rome where chariots raced before crowds of up to 150,000 people, more than any modern stadium holds.

Chariot racing was Rome's favourite sport for over a thousand years. Its star drivers became rich celebrities, and its four teams — Reds, Blues, Greens and Whites — had fans as passionate as any today.

The great racetrack

The Circus lay in a natural valley that Romans used for races from the earliest times. Julius Caesar rebuilt it grandly, and later emperors made it a vast stone stadium with seats on three sides and the imperial box overlooking the track.

Down the middle ran a long barrier, the spina, decorated with statues and an Egyptian obelisk. At one end stood the starting gates; at the other, turning posts where the most dangerous crashes happened.

Race day

On a race day, many races might be run. Chariots burst from the gates together, drivers standing with the reins tied around their waists — if a chariot crashed, a driver had to cut himself free with a knife or be dragged.

Seven laps around the barrier decided the winner. Crowds roared for their colour, bets were placed, and a winning driver received a palm branch, a wreath — and prize money that could make him wealthy.

Stars and fans

The greatest drivers were superstars. Gaius Appuleius Diocles, who raced in the 100s CE, won 1,462 races and earned a fortune that some have called the largest of any athlete in history.

Fans' loyalty could turn violent: arguments between team supporters sometimes ended in riots. Racing continued in the Circus until 549 CE; today the site is a long green park where Romans still jog around the outline of the ancient track.

Did You Know?

One of the obelisks from the Circus Maximus's central barrier now stands in Rome's Piazza del Popolo — it was already over 1,000 years old when the Romans brought it from Egypt.
