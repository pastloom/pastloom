Florence Nightingale and the Birth of Modern Nursing

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Florence Nightingale is famous as the Lady with the Lamp in the Crimean War. This second article tells the other half of her story — how she used numbers, charts and training schools to create modern nursing after the war ended.

Nightingale proved with statistics that most soldiers had died from preventable disease, not battle wounds. Her reforms changed hospitals around the world, and her nursing school of 1860 became a model copied on every continent.

The power of numbers

After returning from Crimea, Nightingale gathered army health records and showed that deaths from disease in barracks and hospitals far outnumbered deaths in battle — even in peacetime.

To make politicians understand, she invented clear visual charts, including her famous polar area diagram, sometimes called the Nightingale rose. It is still taught today as a landmark in the history of data visualisation.

Designing healthy hospitals

Nightingale advised on hospital buildings across Britain and beyond: wards needed fresh air, sunlight, clean water and space between beds. Her ideas shaped the pavilion style of hospital design used for decades.

She also campaigned for clean water and sanitation in India and for better health in workhouses and army barracks, working from her rooms in London even while often ill herself.

The Nightingale school

Money donated by a grateful public formed the Nightingale Fund. In 1860 it opened the Nightingale Training School at St Thomas' Hospital in London, where nursing became a trained, respected profession for women.

Graduates of the school — Nightingale nurses — carried her system to hospitals in Britain, Australia, America and beyond. Modern nursing, with formal training and records, begins here.

Did You Know?

Florence Nightingale wrote about 200 books and reports and thousands of letters — much of her reform work was done by writing, from her sickbed.
