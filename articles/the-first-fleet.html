The First Fleet

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 1787, eleven British ships set sail for the far side of the world. They carried convicts — men and women punished for crimes in Britain — to build a new prison colony in Australia.

After a journey of eight months and about 24,000 kilometres, the First Fleet reached Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788. That landing began European settlement in Australia — and began hard times for the Aboriginal peoples who had lived there for tens of thousands of years.

Why Britain sent the fleet

Britain's prisons were overflowing, and after losing its American colonies in 1783 it could no longer send convicts there. The government chose Botany Bay in New South Wales — land Captain Cook had claimed for Britain in 1770.

The fleet carried convicts, marines, officers, and supplies: tools, seeds, animals and food for two years. Governor Arthur Phillip commanded the little fleet and the colony it would found.

Eight months at sea

The ships crossed the Atlantic to Rio de Janeiro, then sailed to Cape Town, then braved the wild Southern Ocean to Australia. Remarkably few people died on the way — a success for Phillip's careful planning.

They reached Botany Bay on 18–20 January 1788, but Phillip found its soil and water poor. He explored north and chose Sydney Cove in Port Jackson — 'the finest harbour in the world', he wrote — and the fleet moved there on 26 January.

A colony begins

The early years were desperate. The settlers knew little of Australia's soil and seasons, and nearly starved before more ships arrived in 1790. Slowly Sydney grew into a town, and free settlers followed the convicts.

For Aboriginal peoples, 1788 was the start of invasion: disease, violence and the loss of land followed across the continent. That is why 26 January is celebrated by some Australians as Australia Day and mourned by others as Invasion Day.

Did You Know?

The youngest convict on the First Fleet was John Hudson, a chimney sweep aged about nine, transported for stealing clothes and a pistol.
