History of Table Tennis

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Table tennis — the game the world also calls ping-pong — is the biggest participation sport on Earth, played by hundreds of millions of people.

It began as an after-dinner parlour game in Victorian England, with books for a net and a champagne cork for a ball. It ended up as an Olympic sport ruled by the masters of China.

Tennis after dinner

In the 1880s, English officers and gentlemen, rained off the tennis lawns, improvised the game indoors on dining tables. A row of books stood as the net; a rounded champagne cork served as the ball. The game was nicknamed 'whiff-whaff' and 'ping-pong' for the sounds it made.

Manufacturers turned the craze into boxed sets, and in 1901 the 'Ping-Pong' name was trademarked in America. New celluloid balls and pimpled rubber bats made the game faster and cleverer.

World championships and spin wizards

In 1926 the International Table Tennis Federation was founded and the first World Championships were held in London. Hungary's Viktor Barna became the sport's first superstar, and players from Japan dazzled the 1950s with new grips, sponge bats and spinning serves.

The game also made history off the table: in 1971, friendly matches between American and Chinese players — 'ping-pong diplomacy' — helped open relations between the USA and China after decades of silence.

China's golden game

From the 1980s, China made table tennis its own, producing champion after champion through a vast system of schools and training halls. When the sport joined the Olympics in 1988, China soon came to dominate the medal table as few nations have dominated any sport.

Yet table tennis remains everyone's game — a table, two bats and a ball can fit in any home, school or park, and the world's fastest rallies still begin with a game after dinner.

Did You Know?

'Ping-pong diplomacy' was real history: an accidental friendship between American and Chinese players at the 1971 World Championships led to the US team's famous visit to China — and helped change the politics of the whole world.
