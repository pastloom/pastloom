The Search for Noah's Ark

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The story of Noah's Ark — a great boat that saved a family and pairs of animals from a world-covering flood — is one of the most famous stories on Earth. In the Bible, the ark comes to rest on 'the mountains of Ararat'.

For centuries, travellers and explorers have climbed Mount Ararat in eastern Turkey hoping to find the ark's remains. This article tells the history of that search. The story itself is a matter of faith and tradition; no scientific evidence for the ark has ever been found.

The flood story and its roots

The ark story belongs to a family of ancient flood tales. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, the hero Utnapishtim builds a boat to survive a flood sent by the gods; the Babylonian tale of Atrahasis is older still. The Genesis account of Noah shares their shape — one righteous man, one vessel, a new beginning.

Jewish, Christian and Muslim traditions all honour the story — Noah is Nuh, a prophet, in the Quran. Genesis places the ark's landing in 'the mountains of Ararat', the old kingdom of Urartu, in what is now eastern Turkey.

Centuries of climbers and claims

Medieval travellers repeated that the ark still rested on Ararat, visible from afar but unreachable — Marco Polo wrote of it around 1300. As climbing improved, expeditions multiplied: in the 1800s and 1900s, soldiers, missionaries and adventurers reported timbers, sightings and even photographs, none of which survived checking.

In 1949 a US Air Force photograph showed a strange shape on Ararat's western plateau — the 'Ararat anomaly' — which satellite pictures have never resolved into a boat. In 1955 a French explorer, Fernand Navarra, brought down wood he said came from the ark; tests on its age gave disputed results.

The search in modern times

In the 1970s and 1980s, searcher Ron Wyatt promoted the Durupınar site, a boat-shaped formation near Ararat, as the ark's fossilised hull. Geological studies found it to be a natural rock formation — but the site is now a visitor centre, and expeditions to Ararat itself continue, some with official permits, to this day.

The ark has also been rebuilt from the story's measurements: Dutch builder Johan Huiberts launched a full-size replica in 2012 that toured as a floating museum, and a huge ark park opened in Kentucky in 2016. Whether as faith, tradition or legend, the search for Noah's Ark remains one of history's longest-running quests — even though its treasure, unlike Troy or Tutankhamun's tomb, has never been found.

Did You Know?

Johan Huiberts built his full-size ark after dreaming of a flood in 2006. The finished vessel was about 119 metres long — built to the proportions given in Genesis, scaled up — and heavy enough to need 25 barges to move it.
