Honey Bees in History

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Humans have loved honey for thousands of years — so much that Stone Age hunters climbed cliffs and trees to rob wild bees' nests. The ancient Egyptians became the first true beekeepers, and ever since, bees have given us honey, wax for candles, and something even more valuable: pollination for our crops.

The story of bees runs from cave paintings to modern farms, and it is not finished — today the survival of bees has become one of the great questions of farming and nature.

Honey hunters of the Stone Age

Long before farming, humans risked their lives for honey. A famous cave painting at Cuevas de la Araña in Spain, about 8,000 years old, shows a figure hanging on ropes beside a wild bees' nest, reaching in while bees swarm around — one of the oldest pictures of a human at work.

Honey was precious: the only strong sweetness most early people ever tasted, a medicine for wounds, and an ingredient of mead, one of humanity's first alcoholic drinks. Wild honey hunting is still practised today in places such as Nepal, where hunters climb cliffs for the nests of giant bees.

The first beekeepers

The Egyptians were the first great beekeepers, about 4,500 years ago. Temple pictures show keepers calming bees with smoke and stacking clay hives, and honey appears in Egyptian medicine, offerings and tombs — jars of it were placed beside Tutankhamun for the afterlife.

The Greeks and Romans kept bees in hives of straw, clay and wood, and studied them closely: Aristotle wrote careful observations of bee life. For the ancient world, bees were also a symbol — of hard work, order and sweetness — and their wax lit temples and homes as candles.

Monasteries, sugar and the modern hive

In medieval Europe, monasteries were centres of beekeeping, needing wax for church candles and honey for sweetening and mead. Wax was so valuable it was paid as rent and taxes. But when cheap sugar arrived from colonies after 1500, honey lost its place as the main sweetener.

The real revolution came in 1851, when the American Lorenzo Langstroth designed a hive with movable frames spaced exactly as bees like them — the 'bee space'. Frames could be lifted out, inspected and harvested without destroying the colony. Almost every modern hive in the world still follows his design.

The bees we depend on

Today beekeeping is a global industry, but honey is the smaller gift. The greater one is pollination: as bees visit flowers, they carry pollen, allowing plants to make seeds and fruit. A large share of the world's food crops — apples, almonds, berries, coffee and many more — depend partly on bees and other pollinators.

That is why the recent decline of bee populations, from pesticides, disease and lost wildflowers, worries farmers and scientists so much. Protecting bees, people now understand, means protecting our own food supply — a lesson the honey hunters of the Stone Age never had to learn.

Did You Know?

Archaeologists have found jars of honey in ancient Egyptian tombs more than 3,000 years old — and honey is so resistant to bacteria that such honey is said to remain unspoiled.
