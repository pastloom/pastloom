Great Zimbabwe

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Great Zimbabwe was the capital of a rich African kingdom that flourished between about 1100 and 1450 CE. Its people — ancestors of the Shona of modern Zimbabwe — built the largest ancient stone structures in sub-Saharan Africa, without a drop of mortar.

At its height the city held perhaps 18,000 people and traded gold and ivory as far as China. Its ruins were so impressive that European visitors once refused to believe Africans had built them — one of archaeology's most famous injustices, and now a point of national pride.

A city of stone without mortar

'Zimbabwe' means 'houses of stone' — and Great Zimbabwe earns the name. Across a valley and hilltop, its builders fitted granite blocks together with such precision that the walls stand today, some 11 metres high and 5 metres thick, without any mortar at all. The technique is called dry-stone walling, and here it reached monumental scale.

The site has three parts: the Hill Complex, a royal and ritual stronghold among great boulders; the Valley Ruins of elite homes; and the Great Enclosure, whose huge circular wall and mysterious conical tower remain the largest ancient structure in sub-Saharan Africa.

Gold, cattle and world trade

Great Zimbabwe grew rich on its land: cattle herds, fertile farms, and control of the goldfields between the Limpopo and Zambezi rivers. Gold and ivory travelled down to the port of Sofala on the Indian Ocean coast, where Arab and Swahili merchants loaded them for Arabia, Persia, India and China.

In return came luxuries found by archaeologists in the ruins: glass beads from the Middle East, Persian pottery and Chinese porcelain — proof that this inland African capital was plugged into a trading world that spanned an ocean. At its peak, the city may have housed 10,000–18,000 people.

The mystery that wasn't

When European explorers publicised the ruins in the 1870s, Victorian prejudice could not accept that Africans had built them. Writers credited the Phoenicians, the Queen of Sheba — anyone but the local Shona. Treasure-hunters looted the site, destroying evidence, while the myth served colonial propaganda.

Careful archaeology in 1905 and 1929 settled the question: Great Zimbabwe was built by Africans, in the medieval period, stone by African stone. The eight soapstone birds found there — half human, half bird of prey — are masterpieces of Shona art, and one stands today on Zimbabwe's flag.

Decline and rebirth as a symbol

By about 1450 the city was fading — historians debate why: exhausted goldfields, overgrazed and over-farmed land, drought, or trade shifting north to the Zambezi. Power moved to successor states like Mutapa and Torwa, and Great Zimbabwe became a quiet ruin, remembered in Shona tradition.

In 1980, when the country won independence, it took its very name from the ruins: Zimbabwe. Great Zimbabwe, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, had become what it remains — the stone proof of a great African civilisation, built by the ancestors of the people who live around it still.

Did You Know?

The country of Zimbabwe is named after Great Zimbabwe — 'houses of stone'. One of its carved soapstone birds appears on the national flag and banknotes.
