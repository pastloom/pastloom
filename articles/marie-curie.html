History of Science

Marie Curie

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Marie Curie was a scientist who discovered two new elements and won the Nobel Prize twice — the first person ever to do so. She was born in Poland in 1867, when women were rarely allowed to study science.

She moved to Paris, worked in a cold shed with her husband Pierre, and found that some materials gave off a strange invisible energy. She called it radioactivity — and it changed medicine and physics for ever.

A shed full of science

Marie Curie — born Maria Skłodowska — left Poland to study in Paris, where she met the scientist Pierre Curie. They married and worked together in a leaky shed, stirring huge pots of ore by hand.

From tons of rock they extracted tiny amounts of two new elements: polonium, named for her homeland Poland, and radium, which glowed in the dark. The work was slow, dirty and dangerous — and no one yet knew that radiation could harm them.

Two Nobel Prizes

In 1903, Marie and Pierre Curie shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for their work on radioactivity. Marie was the first woman ever to win a Nobel Prize.

In 1911, she won a second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for discovering radium and polonium. She remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

Medicine and a lasting cost

Radium was soon used to treat cancer, and Curie's work led to X-ray machines that saved soldiers' lives in the First World War. She drove one of the first mobile X-ray units to the front herself.

But years of handling radioactive materials damaged her health. She died in 1934 of an illness linked to radiation. Her notebooks are still so radioactive today that they are kept in lead-lined boxes.
