The History of Guns

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Guns began in China, where gunpowder — a mixture of saltpetre, charcoal and sulphur — was discovered by alchemists by the 800s CE and turned into weapons over the following centuries. From fire-lances and hand cannons, the idea travelled west and grew into muskets, rifles, revolvers and machine guns.

Few inventions have changed history more. Guns levelled the power of armoured knights, decided the fate of empires from Panipat to the Somme, and remain at the centre of arguments about war, safety and freedom today.

Gunpowder in China

Chinese alchemists, searching for an elixir of immortality, found the opposite: by the 800s CE they had gunpowder. By 904 soldiers were using 'flying fire' in battle; the Song dynasty, fighting desperate wars, developed fire-lances — bamboo, then metal tubes on spears that spat flame and pellets — bombs, rockets and, by the 1200s, true guns: metal barrels firing projectiles, like the hand cannon in this article's picture.

The Mongol conquests carried gunpowder knowledge across Asia; through the Islamic world it reached Europe by the late 1200s. Within a century, cannon were battering castles from France to China — and castle walls, and the knights behind them, began a long decline.

Cannons decide empires

In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II hauled the largest cannon yet built against Constantinople's thousand-year-old walls — and the walls lost. Gunpowder empires rose on the new weapons: the Ottomans, Safavids and gun-armed Mughals in Asia; in Europe, kings who could afford cannon and professional musketeers crushed rebellious nobles and built centralised states.

Hand weapons evolved slowly: matchlock muskets (lit by a smouldering cord), then the flintlock (a spark from flint and steel) that served from the 1600s to the early 1800s. Battles became lines of musketeers firing volleys — weapons were powerful but slow, inaccurate and useless when wet.

The industrial revolution of the gun

The 1800s transformed guns utterly. Percussion caps made ignition reliable; rifled barrels and conical bullets made shots accurate at hundreds of metres; breech-loading and metal cartridges made reloading fast. In America, Colt's revolvers and Winchester's repeating rifles — made with interchangeable parts in factories — put rapid fire in one hand.

By the century's end came smokeless powder and the machine gun: at the Somme in 1916, machine guns and artillery — guns grown to industrial scale — killed on a scale the world had never seen. The gun had travelled from a bamboo tube to the defining weapon of an age in seven centuries.

Guns in the modern world

In the 1900s guns became fully automatic and universal: the AK-47 (1947) alone was produced in the tens of millions, arming armies, revolutions and militias on every continent. Hunting, sport shooting and collecting remain major uses, and in some countries — above all the United States, whose constitution protects gun ownership — firearms are woven into ideas of freedom and self-defence.

They are also at the heart of fierce modern debates about crime, safety and war. What is not debated is their historical weight: from the fall of Constantinople to the independence of nations, the gun has been one of history's great levers — for better and for worse, an invention no account of the past can leave out.

Did You Know?

Gunpowder was discovered by Chinese alchemists who were trying to invent the opposite — an elixir of eternal life. A 9th-century text warns them the mixture had burned off eyebrows and blown up a house.
