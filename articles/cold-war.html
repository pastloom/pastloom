Cold War

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Cold War was a long struggle between the United States and the Soviet Union after World War II. It lasted from about 1947 to 1991.

It is called cold because the two sides never fought each other directly in a big war. Instead they raced to build weapons, win allies and reach space first — while the whole world feared a nuclear war.

A world split in two

After World War II, Europe was divided. The west followed the United States and democracy, while the east was controlled by the Soviet Union and communism. Winston Churchill called this dividing line the Iron Curtain.

Both sides built huge armies and thousands of nuclear weapons. Each side was afraid to attack, because a war could destroy both of them — and much of the world.

Crises and proxy wars

The Cold War turned hot in other countries. American and Soviet-backed forces fought through local wars in Korea and Vietnam, where millions died.

In 1962, the Cuban Missile Crisis brought the world closer to nuclear war than ever before, when Soviet missiles were placed in Cuba, close to the United States. After tense days, both sides stepped back.

The race to space and the end

The rivalry also pushed both sides into space. The Soviet Union launched the first satellite and the first person, and the United States answered by landing the first people on the Moon.

In the late 1980s, Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev opened his country to change. Communist governments fell across Eastern Europe, and in 1991 the Soviet Union itself broke apart. The Cold War was over.

Did You Know?

During the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union kept a special telephone link between their leaders — the famous hotline — so a mistake would not start a nuclear war.
