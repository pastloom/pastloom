The Battle of Midway

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Battle of Midway, fought from 4 to 7 June 1942 in the Pacific Ocean, was the turning point of the Second World War at sea. Japan planned a trap to destroy the United States Navy's aircraft carriers near Midway Island — but American codebreakers had read Japan's secret messages and set a trap of their own.

In a few astonishing minutes on 4 June, American dive-bombers sank three Japanese carriers, and a fourth followed. Japan lost ships and skilled pilots it could never replace.

Six months of defeat

After the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941, Japan had won victory after victory across the Pacific. Admiral Yamamoto planned to finish the job: lure America's remaining carriers into a battle near Midway and destroy them, then use the island as a base.

What Yamamoto did not know was that American codebreakers in Hawaii, led by Commander Joseph Rochefort's team, had partly broken the Japanese naval code. They even confirmed the target by having Midway broadcast a fake message about water problems — and listening as the Japanese repeated it.

Five minutes that changed the war

On the morning of 4 June, Japanese planes attacked Midway Island. American carrier planes attacked the Japanese fleet in waves; torpedo squadrons were almost wiped out, but they pulled Japanese fighter cover down low.

Then, at about 10:22, American dive-bombers arrived high above, almost unopposed, at the very moment Japanese decks were crowded with planes being refuelled and rearmed. Within minutes the carriers Akagi, Kaga and Soryu were burning wrecks. The fourth carrier, Hiryu, was sunk later that day after its planes helped doom the American carrier Yorktown.

The tide turns

Japan had lost four of its best carriers and, just as importantly, hundreds of its most experienced pilots. America's shipyards, by contrast, were about to launch carriers by the dozen.

Midway did not end the Pacific war — nearly three more years of hard fighting followed. But Japan was on the defensive from that day on. Historians rank Midway among the most decisive naval battles in history.

Did You Know?

The American codebreakers' trick is famous: Midway radioed in plain language that its water plant had broken down, and Japanese messages soon reported that the target — codenamed AF — was short of water, proving AF was Midway.
