History of Universities

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A university is a community of teachers and students devoted to learning at the highest level. Learning centres are ancient — Nalanda in India taught thousands of students 1,500 years ago — but the university as we know it took shape about 900 years ago.

From Bologna and Oxford, universities spread across the world. Their gowns, degrees and ceremonies still carry the marks of their medieval beginnings.

Ancient seats of learning

Long before Europe's universities, students travelled far to study at places like Nalanda in India, where thousands lived and debated philosophy, medicine and astronomy, and at schools in Baghdad, Cairo and Timbuktu.

In 859, Fatima al-Fihri founded al-Qarawiyyin in Fez, Morocco — recognised as the oldest existing educational institution in the world. Learning was a treasure whole cities were proud of.

The medieval invention

In Bologna and Paris around the 1100s, teachers and students formed guilds — universitates — to protect themselves and set standards. Students at Bologna even hired and fined their own professors.

Oxford and Cambridge grew in England, and the pattern spread: lectures, disputations, examinations, and degrees that licensed a graduate to teach anywhere in Christendom. Students wore gowns, spoke Latin, and lived in colleges.

Universities open to the world

For centuries universities excluded women and the poor. Through the 1800s and 1900s, doors opened — women's colleges, public universities, and scholarships carried higher learning to millions.

Today universities teach everything from medieval poetry to rocket science, and online courses reach students in every land. The guild of scholars founded in Bologna now spans the Earth.

Did You Know?

At medieval Bologna, students ran the university: they hired the professors, set their fees, and fined any teacher who started a lecture late or skipped difficult material.
