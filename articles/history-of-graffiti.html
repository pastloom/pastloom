History of Graffiti

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Graffiti — writing and drawing on walls — is as old as walls themselves. Ancient visitors scratched their names, jokes and adverts into stone, and some of that writing still survives today.

In the 1900s graffiti exploded into a colourful art movement on the trains of New York. Ever since, the world has argued about the same question: is it vandalism, or is it art?

Scratches of the ancient world

The people of Pompeii wrote on everything: election slogans, prices, poems, insults and 'I was here' messages. When volcanic ash buried the city in 79 CE, it saved this everyday writing for us.

Ancient Egyptian workers left notes at building sites, Roman soldiers scratched board games into pavements, and a Viking visitor carved runes inside Hagia Sophia in Istanbul. Graffiti is history written by ordinary people.

Names on trains

Modern graffiti began with signatures. In the late 1960s, young writers in Philadelphia and New York covered walls and subway trains with their tags — TAKI 183 became famous after a newspaper story in 1971.

Writers competed to be 'king' of a line, developing wildstyle letters, characters and whole-train masterpieces. Graffiti became one of the four elements of hip-hop culture, alongside rap, DJing and breakdancing.

Art or crime?

Cities spend fortunes cleaning graffiti, and unauthorised painting remains illegal in most places. Yet museums now exhibit street art, and Banksy's works sell for millions.

The argument is ancient too — Pompeii's walls show that people have always felt the urge to leave a mark. Graffiti is the people's printing press: uninvited, unedited and impossible to stamp out.

Did You Know?

One piece of Pompeii graffiti reads: 'I am amazed, O wall, that you have not collapsed under the weight of all the scribblers' — a joke about graffiti, written in graffiti, nearly 2,000 years ago.
