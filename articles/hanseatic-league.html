The Hanseatic League

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Hanseatic League was a network of merchant cities that dominated trade in northern Europe for three centuries. Without a king or a capital, towns like Lübeck, Hamburg and Gdańsk cooperated to protect their ships, win trading rights and grow fabulously rich on Baltic grain, fish, timber and furs.

It was history's most successful trading alliance — a league of cities that could boycott kings and even win wars, before the rising nation-states of the Dutch and English slowly took its seas away.

Merchants band together

In the 1100s–1200s, German merchants sailing the Baltic faced pirates, shipwreck, dishonest partners and princes who seized goods at whim. Their answer was cooperation: merchants abroad formed associations, and the towns behind them — led by Lübeck, refounded in 1159 at the neck of Denmark — began acting together. By the 1300s this web of cities had a name: the Hanse, from an old German word for a band or company.

The League never had a king, an army of its own or a capital. Its members — at its height some 200 towns from the Netherlands to Estonia — met in diets, usually at Lübeck, and decided by consensus. It was a state made of trade.

The Baltic trading world

Hanseatic cogs — sturdy, high-sided ships — carried the bulky goods of the north: grain from Poland and Prussia, timber and pitch from the forests, herring from Scania, furs and wax from Russia through Novgorod, amber from the Baltic shore. In return came Flemish and English cloth, salt from Lüneburg, wine and metal goods.

Four great Kontore — fortified trading posts — anchored the system: Novgorod in Russia, Bergen in Norway, London's Steelyard in England, and Bruges in Flanders. There Hanseatic merchants lived by their own rules, weighed goods, settled disputes and guarded their privileges fiercely.

Power and war

Trade gave the League real power. When Denmark threatened its trade, the Hansa went to war in 1368–70 and won: the Treaty of Stralsund gave it control of Danish straits tolls and even a say in Denmark's royal succession — a merchants' league dictating to a king. It boycotted towns and countries that broke agreements, blockaded Norway, and squeezed concessions from England.

Its cities grew rich and beautiful: the brick Gothic warehouses, churches and town halls of Lübeck, Hamburg, Bremen, Gdańsk, Riga and Tallinn — seven of them now UNESCO-listed — were built with Hanseatic profits.

Decline

The League faded slowly. Dutch and English merchants, backed by growing nation-states, outcompeted it; the herring shifted grounds; Novgorod's Kontor was closed by Moscow in 1494; and the Reformation and the Thirty Years' War shattered its world. Member cities drifted away, and the last Hanseatic Diet met in 1669 with only a handful of towns attending.

Yet Hamburg, Bremen and Lübeck still style themselves 'Free and Hanseatic Cities' on their official names and licence plates. The League left a model Europe never forgot: cities prospering together through law, trust and trade rather than conquest.

Did You Know?

The English word 'sterling' may come from 'Easterling' — the name Londoners gave to Hanseatic merchants, whose coins were trusted for their pure silver.
