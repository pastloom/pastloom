History of Street Food

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Street food — hot snacks cooked and sold in the open air — is as old as cities themselves. The moment crowds gathered in markets and harbours, someone set up a pot or a grill to feed them.

From Roman snack bars to Aztec markets, from Asian night markets to today's food trucks, street food has always fed workers, travellers and the poor — quickly, cheaply and deliciously.

Ancient streets, hungry crowds

In Pompeii, archaeologists have uncovered snack counters with jars set into stone bars, still showing painted menus. Most poor Romans had no kitchen, so street food was daily life.

In the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan, markets fed tens of thousands with tamales, tortillas and roasted foods. In China and South-East Asia, vendors carried whole kitchens on poles across their shoulders.

Markets, fairs and night stalls

Medieval fairs and markets across Europe sold pies, sausages and roasted nuts to crowds. In the Ottoman world and India, kebab sellers, tea stalls and sweet-makers lined the busy streets.

As industrial cities grew, street food fed factory workers on short breaks: oysters in London, hot dogs in New York, chaat in Delhi. Each city developed flavours that became its signature.

Street food today

Governments once tried to clear vendors away; now many cities celebrate them. Singapore gathered its hawkers into clean centres, and UNESCO listed its hawker culture as world heritage in 2020.

Food trucks and night markets draw food lovers in every continent. Street food remains what it was in Pompeii: the fastest, friendliest way to taste a city.

Did You Know?

Archaeologists found a street-food counter in Pompeii in 2020 with paintings of the dishes on sale — including a duck and a rooster — still bright after nearly 2,000 years buried in ash.
