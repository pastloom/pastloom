History of Office Work

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

An office is a place where work is done with words and numbers instead of hands and tools. Kings once employed a handful of scribes; today, hundreds of millions of people spend their days at desks.

Quills became pens, pens became typewriters, typewriters became computers. The story of office work is the story of paperwork — and of the people who tame it.

Quills, ledgers and clerks

Every empire ran on clerks. Egyptian scribes recorded taxes in hieroglyphs; Roman clerks copied orders for armies; medieval monks and merchants' clerks filled ledgers with beautiful, careful handwriting.

The clerk's tools barely changed for centuries: quill, ink, sand to dry the page, and wax for seals. A good hand and a head for numbers made a poor boy employable for life.

Typewriters and towers

The 1800s buried the world in paper: railways, banks, insurers and governments all needed letters, forms and files. The typewriter, sold from the 1870s, sped the work — and brought large numbers of women into offices for the first time.

Steel-framed skyscrapers with lifts stacked these offices into the sky. The modern office was born: rows of desks, filing cabinets, telephones — and the nine-to-five day.

The office on a screen

Computers arrived in offices in the 1980s and quietly ate the typewriter, the filing cabinet and the carbon copy. Email replaced the letter; the spreadsheet replaced the ledger.

Then laptops and the internet cut the office loose from its building. In 2020, millions worked from home almost overnight — the biggest change in office life since the typewriter, and the office has never fully gone back.

Did You Know?

The QWERTY order of letters on your keyboard was designed in the 1870s for typewriters. One reason for its strange layout was to keep the machine's swinging metal arms from jamming into each other.
