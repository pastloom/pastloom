Jesus in History

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Jesus of Nazareth is one of the most influential people who ever lived. Historians — whatever their own beliefs — broadly agree on the outline: a Jewish preacher and teacher from Galilee, baptised by John the Baptist, who gathered followers in Roman-ruled Judea and was crucified in Jerusalem on the orders of the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, about 30 CE.

This article looks at Jesus as a figure of history: what can be known from sources, and how the small movement that followed him grew, within a few centuries, into Christianity — the largest religion in the world. Questions of faith are for believers; the historical impact belongs to everyone.

A Jewish preacher in Roman Judea

Jesus was born into a Jewish world under Roman rule. Galilee, where he grew up, was a land of farmers, fishers and artisans, ruled by Rome's client king Herod Antipas; Judea to the south was governed directly by a Roman prefect, Pontius Pilate. It was an age of religious ferment — Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes and zealots all debated how Jews should live under foreign rule.

About 27–29 CE, Jesus was baptised by John the Baptist, an apocalyptic preacher calling Israel to repentance, and began his own mission: proclaiming that the Kingdom of God was at hand.

The teaching and the crowds

Jesus taught in parables — short stories, like the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son, that turned everyday life into moral drama — and in sayings of startling sharpness: love your enemies; the last shall be first; judge not, lest you be judged. He was famed as a healer and exorcist, ate with outcasts and tax collectors, and chose twelve apostles from ordinary working men.

His message — radical mercy, the worth of the lowly, a God addressed as Father — drew crowds and made authorities nervous. In Jerusalem at Passover, the city's most volatile festival, he overturned the money-changers' tables in the Temple. Within days he was arrested, tried, and crucified — Rome's punishment for sedition, under a sign mocking him as 'King of the Jews'.

What historians can say

Almost everything we know comes from the Gospels — Matthew, Mark, Luke and John — written some 40–70 years after his death by believers. Outside them, the Jewish historian Josephus (writing about 93 CE) mentions Jesus, and the Roman historian Tacitus (about 116 CE) confirms his execution under Pilate. No contemporary Roman record survives, which is normal for a provincial preacher.

Using the tools they apply to any ancient figure, most historians accept as firm: his baptism by John, his activity as a teacher and reputed healer in Galilee, and his crucifixion in Jerusalem. His followers' conviction that they had seen him risen — the belief that launched Christianity — is a matter where history hands over to faith.

The movement that changed the world

Within a generation, the apostle Paul and others had carried the message across the Roman world — to Greece, Rome itself, and beyond — opening it to non-Jews and founding communities in the empire's great cities. Persecuted at intervals for three centuries, Christianity grew steadily, until the emperor Constantine favoured it after 312 CE; by 380 it was the Roman Empire's official religion.

From there it spread to become, today, the faith of about 2.3 billion people, and the calendar of most of the world still counts its years from Jesus's traditional birth year. Whether approached as a believer, a historian or a reader of stories, few lives have bent the course of history further.

Did You Know?

The years of the world's most common calendar are counted from Jesus's birth — but the monk who calculated it in 525 CE was a few years off: Herod the Great, alive at Jesus's birth in the Gospel account, died in 4 BCE, so Jesus was most likely born between 6 and 4 BCE.
