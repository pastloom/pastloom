The Bengal Renaissance

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In the 1800s, the region of Bengal — with its capital at Calcutta, then the capital of British India — woke up in a blaze of new ideas. Writers, teachers and reformers questioned old customs, founded schools and colleges, and created modern Bengali literature.

This awakening is called the Bengal Renaissance. It changed Indian education, women's lives and Indian thought — and helped prepare the way for the freedom movement that followed.

Ram Mohan Roy and the first reforms

Raja Ram Mohan Roy knew Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic and English, and argued that Indians should study modern science alongside their own traditions. In 1828 he founded the Brahmo Samaj, a movement for a simpler, more rational faith.

Roy campaigned against sati — the burning of widows on their husbands' funeral pyres — and in 1829 the practice was abolished. He also championed the English-language education that would fill Bengal's new colleges.

Schools, widows and new books

Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, a scholar of legendary kindness, opened schools for girls and fought until Hindu widows were allowed by law to remarry, in 1856. He also created the Bengali alphabet book from which children still learn.

Bankim Chandra Chatterjee wrote the first great Bengali novels, and Michael Madhusudan Dutt gave Bengali its first epics. Newspapers, theatres and printing presses made Calcutta a city arguing with itself — the surest sign of a renaissance.

Tagore and the flowering

The movement's crown was Rabindranath Tagore: poet, novelist, painter and composer of the national anthems of both India and Bangladesh. In 1913 he became the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Swami Vivekananda carried Indian philosophy to the world with his famous speech at Chicago in 1893, and the scientist Jagadish Chandra Bose showed plants respond to stimulus. The Bengal Renaissance had made one region the thinking heart of a nation.

Did You Know?

Rabindranath Tagore is the only person in history to have written the national anthems of two countries — India's Jana Gana Mana and Bangladesh's Amar Shonar Bangla.
