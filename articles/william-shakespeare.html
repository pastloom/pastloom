William Shakespeare

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

William Shakespeare (1564–1616) is the most famous writer in the English language — a glove-maker's son from Stratford who became the playwright of the Globe Theatre in London and left about 39 plays and 154 sonnets that the world has never stopped performing.

Hamlet, Macbeth, Romeo and Juliet, King Lear — his characters and phrases are woven into everyday speech in a hundred languages. No writer, before or since, has shaped a language and its imagination so completely.

The glover's son from Stratford

William Shakespeare was born in 1564 in Stratford-upon-Avon, a market town of some 1,500 people, son of a glove-maker who rose to be the town's mayor-like bailiff. He likely attended the local grammar school — Latin, rhetoric, classics — and then, at eighteen, married Anne Hathaway; they had three children, including twins Hamnet and Judith.

Then he vanishes from record for several years — the 'lost years' — before surfacing in London by 1592 as an actor and rising playwright, already successful enough to be attacked by a jealous rival as an 'upstart crow'. The provincial glover's son had conquered the capital's stage.

The Globe and the plays

Shakespeare wrote for a living theatre and a mixed crowd — groundlings standing for a penny, merchants and nobles in the galleries. In 1599 his company built the Globe, a round open-air theatre on the Thames' south bank, and Shakespeare, as part-owner, grew prosperous on its success. He wrote about 39 plays: comedies like A Midsummer Night's Dream and Twelfth Night, histories of England's kings, and the great tragedies.

In a few astonishing years (about 1600–1608) he produced Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth — plays that stare into ambition, jealousy, madness and power and have never left the world's stages since. His language remade English itself: hundreds of words and phrases we still use daily were coined or popularised by him.

The man we barely know

For the most famous writer in English, Shakespeare left remarkably little of himself: no letters, no manuscripts of the plays, a businesslike will (famous for leaving his wife his 'second-best bed'), and six shaky signatures. We do not even know for certain what he looked like. This thin record has fed endless theories that someone else wrote the plays — but his fellow actors, who published his works in the First Folio of 1623, seven years after his death, credited him plainly, and scholars overwhelmingly agree.

The Folio mattered enormously: without it, eighteen plays — including Macbeth and The Tempest — would have been lost entirely, surviving only in performance.

The immortal afterlife

Shakespeare died in Stratford on 23 April 1616 — by tradition, his 52nd birthday. His reputation, high in his lifetime, grew into something without parallel: translated into more than 100 languages, performed everywhere from Tokyo to Tehran, quoted by people who have never seen a play.

Actors call him simply 'the Bard'. Four centuries on, his characters — Hamlet doubting, Lady Macbeth scheming, Falstaff laughing — remain the deepest mirrors English literature holds up to human nature. A glover's son from a small town became the world's playwright.

Did You Know?

Shakespeare is credited with coining or popularising over 1,700 English words and phrases — including 'eyeball', 'bedroom', 'lonely', 'break the ice' and 'wild goose chase'.
