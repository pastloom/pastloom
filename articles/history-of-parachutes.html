History of Parachutes

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A parachute is a giant cloth umbrella that slows a fall, letting a person float safely down from the sky. The idea is older than the aeroplane — dreamers drew parachutes centuries before anyone could fly.

When balloons and then aircraft arrived, the parachute became the lifesaver of the air — and later, a sport.

Dreams of falling slowly

Chinese stories tell of an emperor escaping a burning barn with hat-like umbrellas, and Leonardo da Vinci designed a stiff pyramid canopy in about 1485, writing that a man with such a tent of cloth could jump from any height without harm.

In 1783, as balloons rose over France, Louis-Sébastien Lenormand jumped from a tower with a rigid parachute and coined the word itself: parachute — 'guards against a fall'.

Balloon jumps and the first parachutists

In 1797 André-Jacques Garnerin cut himself loose from a balloon high over Paris and swung down beneath a frameless cloth canopy — the first true parachute descent. The swinging nearly made him sick, but he landed alive, and parachuting became a fairground sensation.

When aeroplanes arrived, pilots at first had no escape at all. Compact parachutes packed on the back, proven in the late 1910s, at last gave airmen a second chance — though some commanders resisted, fearing pilots would abandon their planes too quickly.

Paratroopers, sport and space

The Second World War made the parachute a weapon of war: thousands of paratroopers floated into battles from Normandy to Arnhem, and air forces dropped supplies by parachute to soldiers and civilians alike.

In peace, parachuting became a sport of freefall and formation flying. And when humans returned from space, it was parachutes that lowered Apollo and later capsules gently into the sea — Leonardo's cloth tent, catching the sky itself.

Did You Know?

In 2000, a parachute built exactly to Leonardo da Vinci's 1485 design was tested in a real jump — and it worked, floating its jumper safely to Earth more than 500 years after it was drawn.
