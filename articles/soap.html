Soap

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Soap is so ordinary that we forget it had to be invented. Nearly five thousand years ago, people in Babylon discovered that boiling fats with ashes produced a paste that could lift away dirt and grease — and one of humanity's most useful inventions was born.

From Roman bathhouses to the famous soap cities of Aleppo and Marseille, soap slowly moved from luxury to necessity. When science revealed germs in the 1800s, humble soap turned out to be something greater still: one of the greatest life-savers in history.

The Babylonian recipe

Soap is one of the oldest inventions in daily life. A clay tablet from Babylon, written about 2800 BCE, gives a recipe: boil fats with ashes, and you get a cleansing paste. The Egyptians used similar mixtures for washing, and for preparing wool for weaving.

Soap works because of its double nature: one end of a soap molecule loves water, the other loves grease. It surrounds dirt and oil in tiny bubbles called micelles, and the water washes them away. The ancients knew none of this science — but they knew the result.

Rome, legend and the soap-makers

A Roman legend says soap was discovered on Mount Sapo, where rain washed animal fat and ashes from sacrifices into the river below, and women found the water there cleaned clothes wonderfully. The story gave us the word 'soap' — though historians suspect it is only a story.

What is certain is that peoples such as the Gauls made soap from goat fat and beech ashes, and used it on their hair. In the Roman world, bathhouses were everywhere, though many Romans cleaned themselves with oil and a scraper rather than soap.

Aleppo, Castile and the soap cities

The craft flowered in the Islamic world. By the 700s–1000s, soap-makers in cities like Aleppo in Syria were boiling olive oil with laurel oil into hard, green bars — Aleppo soap is still made by hand in almost the same way today.

From there, hard soap-making spread to Castile in Spain and Marseille in France, whose olive-oil soaps became famous across Europe. In Marseille the recipe was protected by law from 1688: only olive oil, boiled in great cauldrons. Soap, once a luxury, slowly became an everyday good.

Soap learns to save lives

In the 1700s–1800s, cheap factory-made soda (alkali) turned soap into an industry, and bars became affordable for ordinary families. Then doctors discovered germs — and realised that this ancient mixture was a weapon. When hospitals and homes adopted handwashing with soap, deaths from infection fell dramatically.

In the 1900s, synthetic detergents joined soap for laundry and dishes, and liquid soaps filled bathrooms. Yet the original invention still stands guard in every home: a Babylonian recipe, nearly five thousand years old, remains one of the cheapest and strongest protections of human health.

Did You Know?

During the 2020 pandemic, the whole world was reminded of soap's ancient power — twenty seconds of handwashing with soap remains one of the simplest ways to stop disease.
