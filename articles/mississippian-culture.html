The Mississippian Culture

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Mississippian Culture was a network of farming peoples who built mound cities across the river valleys of south-eastern and mid-western North America from about 800 to 1600 CE.

Their greatest city, Cahokia, was larger than London at the time. When European explorers arrived, Mississippian chiefdoms still ruled much of the American South-East.

Cities of earth

Mississippian towns were built around great plazas with flat-topped mounds. Temples and chiefs' houses stood on the mounds, and wooden palisade walls protected the largest cities.

Farming maize fed thousands of people. Chiefs organised trade in copper from the Great Lakes, shells from the Gulf Coast and fine stone from distant quarries.

Beliefs carved in shell and copper

Mississippian artists carved shell gorgets and copper plates with winged figures, dancers and symbols of the sun and the world above and below.

Games mattered too: chunkey, played by rolling a stone disc and throwing spears at where it would stop, drew huge crowds and heavy betting across the region.

Decline and descendants

By 1500 many great centres, including Cahokia, had faded — perhaps from climate change, war and the strain of feeding big cities. Smaller chiefdoms carried the culture on.

Spanish explorer Hernando de Soto met powerful Mississippian chiefdoms in the 1540s. The Natchez, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek and other nations of today descend from these peoples.

Did You Know?

Mississippian people played chunkey with polished stone discs so treasured that they were passed down for generations and buried with champions.
