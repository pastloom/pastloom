The Roman Triumph

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The triumph was Rome's greatest honour: a victory parade through the city, granted to a general who had won a great war. For one day, the general rode in a golden chariot, dressed like a god, while the whole city cheered.

Behind him marched his army, his prisoners and wagons of treasure. The triumph showed Rome's people exactly what their empire was winning — and made generals into legends.

The day of glory

The procession could take a whole day to pass. First came officials and musicians, then wagons piled with gold, silver, paintings and statues looted from the defeated land, and placards naming the cities and peoples conquered.

Then came the prisoners — kings and queens in chains, sometimes with their children — followed by white oxen for sacrifice. Behind them rode the general in his chariot, and finally his soldiers, singing songs, their spears wreathed in laurel.

Dressed like a god — with a warning

The triumphing general wore the purple-and-gold dress of Jupiter, king of the gods, and his face was painted red like the god's statue. A slave stood behind him in the chariot holding a golden crown over his head — and, tradition says, whispering: 'Remember, you are only a man.'

The parade ended at Jupiter's great temple on the Capitoline Hill, where the general laid down his laurel wreath. The leading prisoners were usually executed or imprisoned.

Fame that built monuments

Triumphs were politics as much as celebration: they advertised a general's greatness to the voters — and later, emperors kept triumphs for their own family alone. Julius Caesar celebrated four triumphs in 46 BCE, with feasts for the whole city.

Rome built permanent stone arches to remember great triumphs — the Arch of Titus, showing the treasures of Jerusalem, still stands by the Forum. The word 'triumph' itself survives in our language for any great victory.

Did You Know?

Cleopatra's sister Arsinoe was marched in Caesar's triumph in chains of gold — the crowd felt so sorry for her that Caesar spared her life, a rare mercy for a triumph's star prisoner.
