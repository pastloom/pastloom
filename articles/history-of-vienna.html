History of Vienna

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Vienna, the capital of Austria, stands on the River Danube in the heart of Europe. For about 600 years it was the capital of the Habsburg emperors, who ruled lands stretching across much of the continent.

Vienna is famous above all for music: Mozart, Beethoven, Haydn, Schubert and Strauss all lived and worked there, and its coffee houses and palaces still glow with imperial splendour.

From Roman fort to imperial capital

The Romans built a military camp called Vindobona on the Danube frontier, and a town grew around it. In the Middle Ages the Babenberg and then the Habsburg families made Vienna their home.

From the 1400s the Habsburgs were emperors of the Holy Roman Empire, and Vienna became a great capital. Twice the Ottoman Turks besieged it — in 1529 and in 1683, when a relief army saved the city — but Vienna survived and grew grander, crowned by St Stephen's Cathedral and the Schönbrunn Palace.

The city of music

In the 1700s and 1800s Vienna was the music capital of the world. Haydn taught there, Mozart performed there, Beethoven composed there though deaf, and Schubert was born there. Later, the Strauss family filled its ballrooms with waltzes, and the whole city danced.

Vienna's coffee houses became its living rooms, where writers, thinkers and revolutionaries argued over newspapers. In 1900 Vienna was a giant of art and ideas — home to the painter Klimt and to Sigmund Freud, the founder of psychoanalysis.

Empire's end and modern Vienna

The First World War destroyed the Habsburg Empire in 1918, and huge Vienna suddenly became the capital of small Austria. In 1938 Nazi Germany annexed Austria, and the city suffered in the Second World War; afterwards it was divided among the Allied powers for ten years, like Berlin.

Since 1955 Vienna has been the capital of a neutral Austria and a home of the United Nations. Surveys often rank it among the most liveable cities on Earth — an imperial capital that learned to be comfortable.

Did You Know?

The croissant is often linked to Vienna: bakers there are said to have shaped pastries like the crescent moon to celebrate the city's rescue from the Ottoman siege of 1683.
