The Holocaust: History and Memory

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Holocaust was the murder of six million Jewish people by Nazi Germany during the Second World War, along with millions of other victims. Our main article, The Holocaust, tells the history of the crime itself. This article is about what came after: how the world has remembered it.

Survivors, historians, teachers and artists have spent eighty years preserving the evidence — names, photographs, diaries and recorded testimony — so that the victims are not forgotten and the lessons are not lost. Remembering is itself a part of history.

The survivors speak

In the first years after liberation, many survivors found that the world did not want to hear their stories, and they rebuilt their lives in silence. Slowly, that changed: survivors wrote memoirs, and the trial of Adolf Eichmann in Jerusalem in 1961 brought witnesses' voices to a worldwide audience.

Recording projects followed — first on paper and tape, then on film. The Shoah Foundation, founded by the film director Steven Spielberg in 1994, recorded more than 50,000 survivor testimonies, so that future generations can hear the witnesses in their own words.

Memorials and museums

The sites of the camps themselves were preserved as memorials, where visitors walk the ground and read the names. In Jerusalem, Yad Vashem gathers the names of the victims — millions have been recovered, one by one, on 'Pages of Testimony' filled in by families and friends.

Memory also lives in ordinary streets. Across Europe, small brass 'stumbling stones' set into the pavement mark the last homes of individual victims, each engraved with a name — a memorial built one person at a time.

Why memory matters

Most countries now teach the Holocaust in schools, and 27 January is kept as International Holocaust Remembrance Day. The purpose is double: to honour the victims, and to warn how prejudice, propaganda and silence can lead a society towards crime.

As the last survivors grow old and pass away, their recorded voices, the preserved documents and the memorials carry the memory forward. Historians answer those who deny the Holocaust with evidence — the documents, photographs and testimony that remembrance has protected.

Did You Know?

The small brass 'stumbling stones' set into pavements across Europe — each engraved with one victim's name outside their last home — now number more than 100,000, making them the largest scattered memorial in the world.
