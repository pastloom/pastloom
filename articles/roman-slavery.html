Roman Slavery

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Slavery stood at the base of Roman life. At the height of the empire, perhaps one person in five in Italy was enslaved — working in homes, farms, mines, workshops and even offices and schools.

Enslaved people had no legal rights and could be bought and sold. Yet Rome's story cannot be told without them: they built, cooked, taught and farmed, and some rose to freedom and even wealth.

Where enslaved people came from

Rome's wars poured captives onto the market: after some victories, tens of thousands of prisoners were sold at once. Pirates kidnapped travellers and sold them in ports like Delos, where writers claimed 10,000 people could be sold in a single day.

Children born to enslaved women were enslaved from birth. Traders also bought people from beyond the frontiers, so the enslaved came from almost every land around the Roman world.

Lives of work

Most enslaved people worked on farms — the empire's hardest grind along with the mines, where life was short and brutal. In cities, household slaves cooked, cleaned, carried litters and raised children.

Skilled slaves could be doctors, teachers, bankers or managers — Greek tutors taught the children of the rich, and some enslaved secretaries ran great estates. A few saved small tips and gifts, called peculium, hoping one day to buy their freedom.

Freedom and revolt

Rome freed slaves more often than many slave societies. Freed men and women became citizens — though never quite equal — and some freedmen grew rich; the emperors' own offices were run by powerful imperial freedmen.

But the system rested on force, and it exploded in revolts. The most famous, led by the gladiator Spartacus in 73 BCE, defeated Roman armies for two years before it was crushed with terrible cruelty.

Did You Know?

Some Roman tombstones were carved for enslaved people by their fellow slaves — short, loving messages that are almost the only words the enslaved left about themselves.
