History of Astrology

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Astrology is the old practice of linking the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets to events in human lives. It began in Babylon more than 2,500 years ago, where sky-watchers kept careful records of the heavens.

For most of history, astrology and astronomy were studied together by the same scholars. Only in the last few centuries did they separate — astronomy becoming a science of measurement, astrology continuing as a tradition of horoscopes.

Babylon — watching the sky for signs

Babylonian priests watched the sky night after night, recording eclipses, comets and the movements of the planets on clay tablets. They came to read these as signs — omens — for kings and kingdoms.

By about the 400s BCE, Babylonian astrologers had divided the sky into twelve zodiac signs and were casting personal horoscopes — charts of the heavens at the moment of a person's birth. It was painstaking, mathematical work, and it laid foundations that astronomy also used.

Greece, Rome, India and the Islamic world

Greek scholars took Babylonian astrology and joined it with Greek ideas about the universe. In Alexandria, Ptolemy wrote the Tetrabiblos (about 150 CE), the most influential astrology book ever written, used for over a thousand years.

Astrology travelled east as well as west. In India it joined with local astronomy as Jyotisha; in China, court astrologers read the sky for the emperor and helped fix the calendar. Scholars in the Islamic world translated Ptolemy, improved star tables, and carried astrology into medieval Europe, where kings kept court astrologers.

From court astrologers to newspapers

In the 1600s, astronomers such as Kepler still cast horoscopes for patrons — even as their discoveries were changing astronomy forever. As telescopes and physics advanced, universities dropped astrology, and by the 1700s educated opinion treated it as an old belief, not a science.

Yet astrology never vanished. Almanacs kept it alive for ordinary readers, and in the 1900s newspaper 'star sign' columns made it popular worldwide. Historians value its long story because, for two thousand years, the wish to read the sky drove people to watch, record and calculate the heavens with great care.

Did You Know?

Johannes Kepler, who discovered the laws of planetary motion, earned part of his living casting horoscopes — including one for a famous general of the Thirty Years' War.
