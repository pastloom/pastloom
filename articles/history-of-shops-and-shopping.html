History of Shops and Shopping

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

A shop is a fixed place that sells one kind of thing — bread, shoes, books or cloth. For most of history, shopping meant markets and fairs; the shopkeeper with a counter, a sign and a glass window is a newer invention.

From Roman shopfronts and medieval craftsmen's workshops to glittering department stores and shopping on a phone, this is the story of how buying things changed.

From stall to shop

Roman towns had real shops: a wide counter opening onto the street, with jars of wine, oil and grain behind it. In the Middle Ages, craftsmen sold from their workshops — you watched the shoemaker make your shoes.

Prices were settled by bargaining, and every customer paid a different price. Then, in the 1700s, Quaker traders in Britain began marking one honest price for everyone. Fixed prices, strange at first, made shopping faster and fairer.

The department store arrives

In 1852 Paris gained Le Bon Marché, often called the first department store: many departments under one roof, fixed prices, freedom to walk in just to look, and goods you could return. Shopping became entertainment.

Plate-glass windows turned high streets into galleries, and stores like Selfridges in London added restaurants, reading rooms and grand staircases. For women especially, the department store was a respectable new public world.

Self-service and the screen

In 1916 a Memphis grocer, Clarence Saunders, opened Piggly Wiggly, where shoppers took a basket and chose goods themselves — the birth of the self-service shop and, later, the supermarket.

After the Second World War came shopping malls, then, in the 1990s, shops on the internet. Today a shop can be a phone screen — but it still uses the old inventions: display, fixed prices and trust.

Did You Know?

Le Bon Marché in Paris shocked shoppers in the 1850s by letting people enter freely just to look, with no duty to buy — an idea so new that some visitors were afraid to go in.
