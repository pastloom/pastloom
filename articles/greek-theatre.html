Greek Theatre

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Theatre as we know it was born in ancient Greece. In open-air theatres carved into hillsides, Greek actors wearing masks performed tragedies and comedies before thousands of people.

The plays of Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes are still performed all over the world today — and words like 'theatre', 'drama', 'comedy' and 'tragedy' all come from Greek.

Festivals of Dionysus

In Athens, plays were performed at religious festivals for Dionysus, above all the great spring festival called the City Dionysia. Playwrights competed for prizes, and the whole city came to watch for days.

A tragedy told a serious story from myth — kings, heroes and terrible choices. A comedy made fun of politicians, generals and famous thinkers, often rudely, and the audience loved it.

Masks, chorus and stone seats

Greek theatres were built into hillsides in a great half-circle of stone seats, with a round dancing floor and a small building behind it for scenery. The theatre at Epidaurus, built in the 300s BCE, is so well designed that a whisper on stage can be heard in the back row.

Actors wore large masks with strong expressions, so the whole audience could see who each character was. A group called the chorus sang and danced between scenes, commenting on the story.

Stories that never died

Sophocles' Oedipus Rex and Antigone, and Euripides' Medea, ask questions about fate, family and justice that still trouble us. Aristophanes' comedies mocked the Peloponnesian War while it was still being fought.

When Rome conquered Greece, it took Greek theatre home with it. Roman plays, and later the theatres of Europe, grew from Greek roots — every modern stage still stands in the Greeks' shadow.

Did You Know?

At Epidaurus, the best-preserved ancient Greek theatre, a coin dropped in the centre of the stage can be heard clearly from the very top row of seats, 60 metres away.
