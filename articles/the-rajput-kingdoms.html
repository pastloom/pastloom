The Rajput Kingdoms

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Rajputs were warrior clans of northern and western India, famous for courage, honour and great hill forts. From about the 700s CE, Rajput kingdoms such as Mewar, Marwar and Amber ruled much of today's Rajasthan and beyond.

For a thousand years the Rajputs fought invaders, served — and resisted — the Mughals, and filled their desert land with palaces and fortresses. Their stories of bravery are still told across India.

Clans of the desert

The Rajputs were not one kingdom but many clans — Sisodias, Rathores, Chauhans, Kachhwahas and others — each ruling its own kingdom from a fort on a hill or rock.

Their code prized bravery above life itself: a Rajput ruler was expected to fight to the death rather than surrender his fort. Their desert cities — Udaipur, Jodhpur, Jaipur, Jaisalmer — grew rich on trade routes crossing the Thar desert.

Facing sultanates and Mughals

In 1192 the Rajput king Prithviraj Chauhan was defeated by Muhammad Ghori at the Second Battle of Tarain — a turning point that opened northern India to the Delhi Sultanate. Yet the Rajput kingdoms were never fully conquered, and their forts held out for centuries.

When the Mughal emperor Akbar came to power, he chose alliance over endless war: Rajput princesses married into the Mughal family and Rajput generals led Mughal armies. But Mewar's Rana Pratap refused to submit, and his stand at Haldighati in 1576 became legend.

The forts remain

In later centuries the Rajput states dealt with the Marathas and then the British, keeping their own rulers as princely states until India became independent in 1947.

Their hill forts — six of them now World Heritage Sites — still crown the ridges of Rajasthan, and the tales of Rajput queens, poets and warriors remain at the heart of the region's identity.

Did You Know?

The walls of Kumbhalgarh Fort in Mewar run for about 36 kilometres — often called the second-longest continuous wall in the world, after the Great Wall of China.
