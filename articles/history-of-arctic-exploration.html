History of Arctic Exploration

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Arctic — the ocean of ice around the North Pole — was one of the last great challenges on Earth. Explorers went north hunting for sea routes to Asia, for the Pole itself, and sometimes simply because the white unknown was there.

Their story is one of courage and disaster: ships crushed by ice, crews saved by Inuit knowledge, and a long race to stand at the top of the world.

Sailors at the edge of the map

The first Arctic explorer whose name we know is Pytheas of Massalia, who sailed north around 325 BCE and told of a land called Thule where the sea turned thick and strange — probably the edge of the ice. Greeks and Romans thought he had invented it.

A thousand years later, Norse sailors pushed further: Iceland, then Greenland, then the coast of North America itself. Dutch and English whalers followed the whales north, learning the ice seasons the hard way.

Two passages and a pole

While western sailors hunted the Northwest Passage, others sought a Northeast Passage along Siberia's coast. Barentsz's crew survived the winter of 1596–97 in a wooden house on Novaya Zemlya — the first recorded Arctic wintering by Europeans.

Russia's Great Northern Expedition (1733–1743), led in part by Vitus Bering, mapped Siberia's whole Arctic coast. The Northeast Passage itself was finally sailed by Nordenskiöld's Vega in 1878–79 — while the Northwest Passage had already swallowed Franklin's expedition a generation earlier.

The top of the world

By 1900 only one prize remained: the Pole itself. Frederick Cook and Robert Peary both claimed to have reached it, in 1908 and 1909, and argued for decades; most historians now doubt both claims. The first undisputed visit came from the air — the airship Norge flew over the Pole in 1926, carrying Roald Amundsen.

Confirmed journeys on the surface came even later: by snowmobile in 1968, and in 1969 Wally Herbert's team crossed the whole Arctic Ocean by dog sled. Today the ice those men crossed is thinning, and research stations and satellites have replaced the sledges.

Did You Know?

Barentsz's crew left their winter house on Novaya Zemlya in 1597 — and when it was found nearly 300 years later, their clock, books, tools and even their shoes were still inside, preserved by the cold like a time capsule.
