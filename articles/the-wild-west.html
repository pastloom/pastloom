The Wild West

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Wild West is the name for the American frontier of the 1800s — the decades when settlers, cowboys, miners, soldiers and outlaws poured into the lands west of the Mississippi River. It is the world of cattle drives, gold rushes, wagon trains, sheriffs and gunfights that films made famous.

The real West was wilder and more complicated than the movies: a land of opportunity for some and catastrophe for the Native American nations who had lived there for thousands of years. The frontier era lasted barely thirty years — about 1865 to 1895 — but it shaped America's image of itself forever.

A continent opens

In 1803 the United States bought Louisiana from France, doubling its size overnight; in 1804–06 Lewis and Clark crossed to the Pacific and back. Settlers followed on trails like the Oregon Trail — months by covered wagon, through cholera, rivers and deserts. In 1848 gold was found in California, and by 1849 some 300,000 'forty-niners' had rushed west, founding San Francisco almost overnight.

In 1862 the Homestead Act offered free land to anyone who would farm it, and in 1869 the first transcontinental railroad joined the coasts — a journey of months became a week. The frontier moved faster than the law could follow, which is where the 'wild' came from.

Cowboys, cattle and boomtowns

After the Civil War, Texas was full of wild longhorn cattle and the industrial North was hungry for beef. Cowboys — working horsemen, about a third of them Mexican vaqueros, Black or Native — drove herds a thousand miles north to rail towns like Abilene and Dodge City. The cattle kingdom boomed until barbed wire (patented 1874) and brutal winters in the 1880s closed the open range.

Mining camps and cow towns sprang up and vanished in years. Some produced legends: Deadwood, Tombstone — where the Earp brothers and Doc Holliday fought the gunfight at the O.K. Corral in 1881 — and Dodge City, 'the wickedest little city in America'. Real gunfights were rare; the myth, fed by dime novels and Buffalo Bill's Wild West shows, was enormous.

The tragedy of the Native nations

The West was not empty. Hundreds of Native nations — Lakota, Cheyenne, Apache, Comanche, Nez Perce and many more — had lived there for millennia. Settlers, soldiers and railroads brought war, broken treaties and disease; the near-extermination of the buffalo — from tens of millions to a few hundred by the 1880s — destroyed the Plains peoples' way of life deliberately.

Native resistance produced its own legends: Crazy Horse and Sitting Bull destroyed Custer's command at Little Bighorn in 1876; Geronimo evaded thousands of soldiers for years. But by 1890, after the massacre at Wounded Knee, armed resistance had ended and the surviving nations were confined to reservations — the frontier's darkest chapter.

The frontier closes, the myth begins

In 1890 the US Census declared the frontier line gone — the continent was settled, if thinly. Three years later the historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued the frontier had made America: individualist, inventive, democratic. Whether or not he was right, Americans believed him.

The West then passed into myth: Owen Wister's novels, Hollywood's westerns — for decades the most popular film genre on Earth — and the cowboy as a global symbol of freedom. The real West was muddier, more diverse and more violent against its first peoples than the myth admits. Both the myth and the truth are part of its history.

Did You Know?

The most famous gunfight in Western history, at the O.K. Corral in 1881, lasted about 30 seconds — and it did not actually happen in the O.K. Corral, but in a lot nearby.
