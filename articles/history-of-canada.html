History of Canada

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Canada is the second largest country in the world by land. Indigenous peoples have lived there for thousands of years, hunting, fishing and trading across its forests, plains and Arctic coasts.

French and British settlers arrived from the 1500s, and in 1867 several colonies joined together as the Dominion of Canada. Today Canada is a bilingual country, with English and French as its official languages.

The first Canadians

Long before Europeans came, First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples built rich cultures across the land — from the totem poles of the Pacific coast to the igloos of the Arctic.

About 1,000 years ago, Norse sailors from Greenland built a small settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland — the first Europeans in the Americas, 500 years before Columbus.

French and British Canada

From the 1500s, French traders came for fish and furs and founded Quebec in 1608. Britain and France fought over Canada, and Britain won in 1763.

Most French settlers stayed, keeping their language and Catholic faith — which is why Quebec is French-speaking today. Loyalists fleeing the American Revolution added English-speaking settlers.

Confederation and a modern country

On 1 July 1867, Ontario, Quebec, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia joined as one dominion, and other provinces followed. A railway across the whole continent, finished in 1885, tied the young country together.

Canada fought in both world wars, welcomed immigrants from every continent, and in 1982 gained its own constitution and Charter of Rights. Reconciliation with Indigenous peoples remains an important national task.

Did You Know?

Canada's name likely comes from kanata, a word meaning 'village' in the language of the St Lawrence Iroquois people.
