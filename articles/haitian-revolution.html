Haitian Revolution

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was the only successful revolt by enslaved people in history. In the French sugar colony of Saint-Domingue, the enslaved rose up and fought for their freedom.

They defeated the armies of great empires and founded a new country: Haiti — the first modern republic led by people of African descent.

Sugar, slavery and revolt

Saint-Domingue was the richest colony of its age, producing about half the world's sugar. Its wealth rested on the brutal labour of around half a million enslaved Africans.

In August 1791, inspired in part by the French Revolution's talk of liberty and rights, the enslaved rose in revolt and burned the great plantations.

Toussaint Louverture

From the uprising emerged Toussaint Louverture — himself once enslaved — a brilliant general and governor. Under his leadership, slavery was abolished in the colony.

He defeated Spanish and British forces and made Saint-Domingue almost independent. But Napoleon sent a great army to restore French power. Toussaint was seized and died in a French prison in 1803.

Haiti is born

Under Jean-Jacques Dessalines, the freedom fighters defeated the French at the Battle of Vertières. On 1 January 1804 they declared independence, naming the country Haiti — a Taíno word meaning 'land of high mountains'.

The revolution terrified slave-owning empires everywhere. It also helped convince Napoleon to sell the vast Louisiana territory to the United States, nearly doubling that country in size.

Did You Know?

Haiti was the second country in the Americas to win independence — after the United States — and the first to abolish slavery completely.
