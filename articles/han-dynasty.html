Empires & Kingdoms

Han Dynasty

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Han dynasty ruled China for four hundred years, from 206 BCE to 220 CE — so successfully that the Chinese people still call themselves the Han people, and their writing 'Han characters'. Founded by a commoner, it turned the harsh first empire of Qin into a lasting civilisation.

Under the Han, China opened the Silk Road, chose officials by examination, and invented paper. Its fall in 220 CE began centuries of division, but the Han model of empire became China's permanent template.

Liu Bang founds a dynasty

China's first empire, the Qin, unified the country in 221 BCE — and collapsed within fifteen years under the weight of its own harshness. Out of the civil war rose Liu Bang, a commoner and former minor official, who defeated his rival Xiang Yu and in 202 BCE founded the Han dynasty. He took the throne name Gaozu.

The Han kept the Qin's unified system — one script, one coinage, central government — but softened its cruelties: lower taxes, fewer forced-labour demands, a lighter hand. Confucianism, with its ideals of educated, moral officials, became the state's guiding philosophy. The formula worked: the Han lasted four centuries, and 'Han' remains the name of China's majority people and their characters today.

Emperor Wu and the Silk Road

The dynasty's greatest ruler, Emperor Wu (reigned 141–87 BCE), pushed China's frontiers to their widest: into Korea, Vietnam, and westward against the Xiongnu nomads of the steppe. In 138 BCE he sent an envoy, Zhang Qian, west to find allies; Zhang returned thirteen years later with tales of distant kingdoms — Ferghana, Bactria, Persia — and the routes to reach them.

Those routes became the Silk Road. Chinese silk, lacquer and later paper travelled west; horses, grapes, glass, music and ideas travelled east. Under Wu, the state also founded an imperial university and the examination ideal: officials chosen for learning, not birth.

The break: Wang Mang and the Eastern Han

In 9 CE a reforming minister, Wang Mang, seized the throne and tried radical changes — abolishing slavery, redistributing land, reforming the coinage. Floods, famine and rebellion destroyed him; in 23 CE his capital fell and he was killed. A Han prince restored the dynasty in 25 CE, moving the capital east to Luoyang — the Eastern Han.

The restored dynasty still shone: in 105 CE the court official Cai Lun standardised papermaking, one of history's most consequential inventions; the astronomer Zhang Heng built a seismometer that detected distant earthquakes; and Han armies and traders reached deep into Central Asia.

Fall of the Han

By the 180s the dynasty was rotting from within: child emperors, court factions of eunuchs and officials, corrupt landowners squeezing peasants. In 184 the Yellow Turban rebellion — a vast peasant rising led by a faith healer — shook the empire; warlords raised armies to crush it and never gave their power back.

In 220 CE the last Han emperor abdicated to the warlord Cao Pi. China split into Three Kingdoms and would not fully reunite for nearly four centuries. But the Han had defined what China was: its name, its script, its Confucian state and its place at the centre of East Asia all date from these four hundred years.

Did You Know?

Our word 'paper' traces to papyrus, but paper itself is Chinese: a Han court official, Cai Lun, is honoured as its inventor — and the oldest surviving scraps come from Han-dynasty China.
