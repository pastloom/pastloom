The Rebellion of 1857: The Spark, the Leaders and the Change

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 1857 India exploded in the largest uprising against British rule of the 1800s. Indian soldiers called sepoys rose against the East India Company, and the revolt spread across northern India with farmers, princes and ordinary people joining in.

Many in India remember it as the First War of Independence. It failed — but it ended the East India Company's rule forever and changed how Britain governed India.

The spark: cartridges and anger

Beneath the revolt lay years of anger: heavy taxes, land taken from princes, and fears that the British meant to destroy Indian religions and castes. The new Enfield rifle lit the fuse — its cartridges had to be bitten open, and rumours spread that they were greased with fat from cows, sacred to Hindus, and pigs, forbidden to Muslims.

In March 1857 a sepoy named Mangal Pandey attacked British officers at Barrackpore and was hanged. On 10 May, sepoys at Meerut freed their jailed comrades and marched to Delhi, where they proclaimed the old Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, as their leader.

The leaders of the revolt

In Jhansi, the widowed queen Rani Lakshmibai fought to defend her kingdom and died in battle in 1858 — the British commander called her the most dangerous of the rebel leaders. At Kanpur, Nana Saheb led the rising; his general Tantia Tope kept a moving guerrilla war alive for nearly a year.

In Lucknow, Begum Hazrat Mahal led the resistance and ruled in the name of her young son. Across the north, villages rose, and for months British rule in much of northern India simply vanished.

Defeat — and a changed India

British forces, helped by loyal Indian troops, slowly retook Delhi, Lucknow and the rebel strongholds. The revenge on all sides was terrible, and Delhi's emperor was exiled to Burma, ending the Mughal line.

In 1858 the British government took India directly under the Crown. Queen Victoria promised to respect Indian religions and the rights of princes. The Company was finished, the army was rebuilt — and the memory of 1857 stayed alive until the freedom movement won in 1947.

Did You Know?

Rani Lakshmibai fought in battle dressed as a soldier and died at Gwalior in June 1858, aged about 29 — she is still one of India's most loved heroes.
