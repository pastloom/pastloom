The Roman Senate

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Senate was Rome's council of elders — a group of experienced men from leading families who advised the rulers and, for centuries, guided the whole Roman state.

It began under the kings, grew powerful in the Republic, and survived into the Empire. Its meeting house, the Curia, still stands in the Roman Forum today.

From royal advisers to rulers of Rome

Under the kings, senators were advisers with little real power. When the Republic began in 509 BCE, the Senate slowly became the state's steady centre: consuls came and went each year, but senators served for life and carried the state's memory.

The Senate controlled the treasury, received foreign ambassadors, decided questions of war and peace, and told officials what to do — advice they rarely dared refuse.

Who could be a senator?

Senators were drawn from wealthy families and men who had held public office, working their way up a ladder of posts from quaestor to consul. They wore a white toga with a broad purple stripe and special red shoes.

The Senate was never elected by the people, and ordinary Romans — and all women — were excluded. Even so, for about 500 years it steered Rome from a small city to ruler of the Mediterranean world.

The Senate under the emperors

Augustus kept the Senate but took its real power: emperors controlled the army, the money and the law-making. Senators still governed provinces, held ceremonies and debated — but on the emperor's terms.

Remarkably, the Senate outlived the western empire itself, still meeting in Rome into the 600s CE. The word 'senate' lives on today in the governments of many countries, including the United States.

Did You Know?

The Senate's letters SPQR — Senatus Populusque Romanus, 'the Senate and People of Rome' — were stamped on coins, buildings and army banners, and you can still see SPQR on drain covers in Rome today.
