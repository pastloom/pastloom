Elephants in History

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Elephants are the largest land animals alive, and for at least four thousand years humans have ridden them, worked with them, worshipped them and taken them into battle. From the temples of India to Hannibal's famous march over the Alps, elephants have marched through human history like no other animal.

They have been royal symbols, living war machines, patient workers in the forests of Asia — and, in the age of the ivory trade, victims. Their story shows both the best and the worst of how humans treat the animal world.

Partners of kings in Asia

Elephants have lived alongside humans for thousands of years. In the Indus Valley, seals from 4,000 years ago already show elephants with riders and cloths on their backs. In India the elephant became a symbol of wisdom and royal power, linked with the god Ganesha and the rain-god Indra.

Asian kings kept elephants by the thousand — for ceremony, for carrying treasure, and for war. A king's greatness could be measured by his elephants, and rare white elephants were so sacred in Thailand and Burma that they belonged to the king alone and could not be put to work.

War elephants march west

An elephant charge was one of the ancient world's most terrifying sights: tons of animal, trumpeting, bearing archers in a tower, able to crush men and panic horses that had never smelled an elephant before. Indian armies used them for centuries, and the Persians brought fifteen against Alexander the Great at Gaugamela.

Alexander was so impressed that he built his own elephant corps. After his death his successor kingdoms fought great elephant battles, and the Greek king Pyrrhus brought elephants against Rome in 280 BCE — winning battles that cost him so much that we still speak of a 'Pyrrhic victory'.

Hannibal and the Alps

The most famous war elephants of all belonged to Hannibal of Carthage. In 218 BCE, at the start of his war against Rome, he marched 37 elephants from Spain over the Alps into Italy — through snow, rockfalls and hostile tribes — in one of the most daring marches in history.

Most of the elephants died in the cold that followed, and at the great Battle of Cannae two years later Hannibal won without them. But the image of elephants crossing the snowy Alps never faded, and Rome itself later used elephants in war and in its arena games.

From work to protection

In Asia elephants kept working long after their war days ended — hauling teak logs in Burma and Thailand, carrying hunters and officials, and starring in temple festivals. Even in the two World Wars, elephants carried supplies through jungles where trucks could not go.

The darkest chapter was ivory. In the 1800s and 1900s, hunters killed African elephants in vast numbers for their tusks, to make piano keys, billiard balls and ornaments. Today elephants are protected in most countries, the ivory trade is banned worldwide, and these intelligent, family-loving animals have become symbols of conservation itself.

Did You Know?

Hannibal's most famous elephant, sometimes called Surus, was said to have carried him through the marshes of Italy — most of his elephants died in the cold Alpine winter.
