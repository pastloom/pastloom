Life on the Home Front

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In the Second World War, the 'home front' meant the civilians at home — and for them, too, daily life was transformed. Food and clothes were rationed, cities were blacked out against bombers, and millions of children were sent away from home for safety.

This article describes everyday life on the home front, above all in Britain, where the experience — ration books, air raids and 'make do and mend' — is still remembered by the oldest generation today.

Ration books and make do

Every person, from babies to grandparents, carried a ration book of coupons. A typical week's ration held small amounts of butter, sugar, bacon and meat, with eggs so scarce that families kept hens or used dried egg powder.

Clothes were rationed too, under the slogan 'make do and mend': jumpers were unravelled and reknitted, dresses cut down for children. Meanwhile the 'Dig for Victory' campaign turned gardens and parks into allotments that grew a large share of the nation's vegetables.

Children far from home

In the first days of September 1939, about 1.5 million people — mostly schoolchildren with gas masks, name labels and small suitcases — were evacuated from the cities to the countryside, to live with strangers.

Some children were happy and well loved; others were homesick and miserable. When the expected bombing did not come at once, many drifted home — and were evacuated again when the real bombing, the Blitz, began in 1940.

Nights of the Blitz

At night, Britain went dark: blackout curtains, dimmed street lights and wardens crying 'Put that light out!'. Families slept in Anderson shelters in their gardens, or in London's Underground stations, which became dormitories for thousands.

About 43,000 British civilians were killed in the Blitz of 1940–41. Yet shops reopened among the rubble, buses ran, and the famous home-front spirit — a mixture of courage, humour and sheer stubbornness — carried everyday life on.

Did You Know?

During the war, British children received concentrated orange juice and cod-liver oil for vitamins — and because rationing shared food fairly, many poor children were actually healthier than they had been before the war.
