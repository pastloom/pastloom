Vasco da Gama

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Vasco da Gama was a Portuguese sailor who in 1498 completed the first sea voyage from Europe to India — around the bottom of Africa and across the Indian Ocean.

His voyage opened a sea road for spices that changed world trade forever, making Portugal rich and linking Europe, Africa and Asia by ship.

Around Africa

For decades Portuguese sailors had been edging down the African coast, and in 1488 Bartolomeu Dias had rounded the Cape of Good Hope. Da Gama's mission was to go further — all the way to India.

His four ships left Lisbon in July 1497, swung wide into the Atlantic to catch the winds, and rounded the Cape after a stormy passage. Sailing up Africa's east coast, he found ports, pilots and finally a guide who knew the crossing to India.

Landfall at Calicut

On 20 May 1498, da Gama anchored at Calicut, on India's south-west coast — the heart of the spice trade. The local ruler received him, though the Portuguese gifts seemed poor beside the riches of the East, and Muslim merchants who controlled the trade opposed him.

The return voyage was terrible: contrary winds made the crossing take three months, disease spread, and many sailors died. Of about 170 men who set out, only around 55 came home. But the ships carried spices — and proof the sea road worked.

Empire and a hard legacy

Da Gama returned to India with a war fleet in 1502 and used violence to force Portuguese power into the spice trade. Portugal built forts and ports from Africa to Asia and grew rich on pepper and cinnamon.

He died in India in 1524, shortly after arriving as viceroy. His voyage is remembered as a triumph of navigation — and as the beginning of centuries of European empire in Asia, with all the harm that followed.

Did You Know?

Da Gama's first voyage lasted more than two years and covered about 24,000 miles — at the time, the longest ocean voyage ever completed.
