The Boxer Rebellion: The Siege of Beijing

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 1900, a movement of Chinese fighters called the Boxers rose up against foreign influence in China. They besieged the foreign embassies in Beijing for 55 days, and the world's great powers sent an army to crush them.

The Boxer Rebellion was the dramatic climax of a century in which foreign powers had humiliated China. Its failure left the Qing dynasty broken, only a decade before it fell completely.

A century of humiliation

Through the 1800s, Britain, France, Russia, Japan and others forced trading ports, territory and special rights from a weakening Qing China. Railways and missions spread inland, and many Chinese blamed foreigners for drought, flood and poverty.

In Shandong province, a movement of martial-arts societies — nicknamed Boxers in the West — began attacking missionaries and Chinese Christians. The Boxers believed rituals and training made them immune to bullets.

The siege of Beijing

In June 1900 the Boxers entered Beijing, and the Qing court, under the Empress Dowager Cixi, chose to support them and declared war on the foreign powers.

For 55 days, a few hundred foreign soldiers and thousands of Chinese Christians held the legation quarter against Boxer and imperial attacks, until an international relief army fought its way into the city in August.

Defeat and punishment

The Eight-Nation Alliance — Japan, Russia, Britain, France, the United States, Germany, Italy and Austria-Hungary — occupied Beijing, and Cixi fled in disguise.

The Boxer Protocol of 1901 forced China to pay an enormous indemnity and accept foreign troops on its soil. The Qing survived, humiliated, for ten more years — but few Chinese believed in it any longer.

Did You Know?

The Boxers' name came from their martial-arts training: foreigners called them Boxers because their exercises looked like shadow-boxing.
