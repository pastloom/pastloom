The Great Wall of China

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Great Wall of China is the largest building project in human history — not one wall but a network of walls, towers and forts built by Chinese dynasties over more than 2,000 years to guard their northern frontier. All its branches together stretch over 21,000 kilometres.

The stone wall in the famous photographs is mostly the Ming dynasty's work of the 1400s–1600s. The Wall never made China invincible — but as a symbol of endurance, organisation and sheer will, nothing on Earth matches it.

Many walls, many centuries

There is no single Great Wall. Chinese states began building frontier walls in the 7th century BCE — first against each other, then against the horse-riding nomads of the northern steppe, whose raids no army could easily stop. When the First Emperor, Qin Shi Huang, unified China in 221 BCE, he ordered the northern walls joined and extended into one line of defence.

Most early walls were rammed earth, not stone — pounded layer upon layer until hard as concrete. In the dry deserts of the west, stretches of those 2,000-year-old earth walls still stand.

The Han and the Silk Road walls

The Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) pushed the wall far west into the Gobi Desert, guarding the Silk Road trade routes to Central Asia. Watchtowers signalled with smoke by day and fire by night — a message could travel hundreds of kilometres in hours.

For long periods the wall mattered less: strong dynasties like the Tang preferred diplomacy, marriage alliances and armies in the field. Walls rose when China felt weak, and crumbled when it felt strong — the Wall's history is a barometer of Chinese confidence.

The Ming wall: the wall in the photographs

The wall everyone pictures — sweeping stone ramparts over green mountains, with elegant watchtowers — is the Ming dynasty's work (1368–1644). After the Mongols were expelled from China, the Ming, fearing their return, rebuilt the northern defences on a colossal scale: brick-and-stone-faced walls near the capital, some wide enough for five horsemen abreast.

Millions of soldiers, peasants and prisoners laboured over two centuries; countless died, and legends call the Wall the world's longest cemetery. Its famous sections near Beijing — Badaling, Mutianyu — are Ming engineering at its finest.

Did it work — and what it means now

As a military barrier the Wall was mixed: it stopped small raids and controlled trade and migration, but determined armies bribed guards, found gaps, or simply went around. In 1644 the Ming fell when a general opened the gates at Shanhaiguan to the Manchu — who then ruled China as the Qing and had no need of a wall against themselves.

Today the Great Wall is China's national symbol and a UNESCO World Heritage Site, visited by millions. It is often said to be visible from space — it is not, not to the naked eye — but its true scale needs no myth: 21,000 kilometres of wall, raised by one civilisation across two thousand years.

Did You Know?

All the walls of all dynasties, laid end to end, would stretch over 21,000 kilometres — more than halfway around the Earth. The Wall is not one wall, but hundreds built over 2,000 years.
