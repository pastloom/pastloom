Knights and Castles

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Knights and castles belong to the Middle Ages, the long period between about 500 and 1500 CE. Knights were armoured soldiers on horseback who served kings and lords.

Castles were their strong homes — stone fortresses with thick walls and tall towers. Together, knights and castles protected the land, and also ruled it, for hundreds of years.

Becoming a knight

A boy who wanted to become a knight began training at about seven years old as a page, then became a squire, learning to ride, fight and serve at table.

At about twenty-one, if he had proved himself, he was knighted in a special ceremony — traditionally with a tap of a sword on his shoulders. Knights were expected to follow chivalry: to be brave, loyal, honest and kind to the weak.

Castles of wood and stone

The first castles were wooden towers on high mounds, quick to build but easy to burn. From about the 1100s, lords rebuilt them in stone, with walls several metres thick.

A great castle had a deep moat, a drawbridge, arrow slits and a strong central tower called the keep, where people could hold out during a siege. Castles were homes, storehouses, courts and prisons — not only fortresses.

The end of the age of castles

Armies learned to attack castles with siege towers, tunnels and huge stone-throwing machines. Then gunpowder arrived from China, and cannons could smash even the thickest stone walls.

By the 1500s, kings had strong central armies and knights and castles were no longer needed. Many castles fell into ruins — but hundreds still stand today, and their stories of sieges, feasts and tournaments still fill books and films.

Did You Know?

Some castle spiral stairs were built to twist clockwise going up — so a right-handed attacker climbing the stairs could not swing his sword properly, while the defender above him could.
