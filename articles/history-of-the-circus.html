History of the Circus

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The circus is a show of skill and wonder — acrobats flying through the air, clowns making everyone laugh, jugglers, strongmen and trained animals, all in a round ring.

The modern circus was invented in London in 1768 by a horse-riding soldier. But the word circus is far older: it comes from the great race tracks of ancient Rome.

From Roman races to Astley's ring

Ancient Rome's Circus Maximus was a vast stadium where chariots raced before huge crowds. The name 'circus' — circle — stayed in language long after the races ended.

In 1768, Philip Astley, a former cavalry soldier, opened a riding school in London where trick riders performed in a ring between lessons. He added clowns, acrobats and strongmen — and the modern circus was born. His ring size, about 13 metres, suited galloping horses and is still used today.

The travelling big top

In the 1800s, circuses took to the road. Enormous tents — big tops — rose in town after town, and trains carried animals, wagons and performers across America and Europe.

Showmen like P. T. Barnum made the circus a giant spectacle with parades, wild animals and wonders from every land. The trapeze, invented in France in 1859, sent flyers sailing above the gasping crowd.

The circus changes

In the later 1900s, people began to question animals performing in circuses, and many countries banned wild-animal acts. Television also stole some of the circus's audience.

The circus answered by reinventing itself. Companies like Canada's Cirque du Soleil built shows of pure human skill, music and theatre — no animals at all — and filled theatres around the world.

Did You Know?

The circus ring is about 13 metres wide because that was the smallest circle a galloping horse could run in while a rider stood on its back — and circuses still use Philip Astley's size after 250 years.
