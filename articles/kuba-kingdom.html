The Kuba Kingdom

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Kuba Kingdom was a kingdom of the Kuba people in Central Africa, in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It was united around 1625 and became famous across the world for its extraordinary art.

Kuba kings ruled a federation of peoples between the Kasai and Sankuru rivers. Their weavers, carvers and metalworkers produced some of the most admired cloth and sculpture in all of African art.

Shyaam, the great organiser

Kuba tradition credits King Shyaam, who ruled in the early 1600s, with uniting the Kuba peoples and creating their kingdom. Stories say he travelled widely and brought back new crops, crafts and ideas.

He is remembered as a lawgiver and inventor. Royal portrait statues called ndop were carved for Shyaam and later kings — among the earliest portrait sculptures in Africa.

A kingdom of artists

Kuba cloth is world-famous: men wove raffia palm fibre into cloth, and women embroidered it with bold geometric patterns, making 'Kuba cloth' prized by collectors and museums everywhere.

The kingdom also produced fine carved cups, boxes and masks, and its blacksmiths and brass-casters served the court. Art was not decoration here — it showed rank, memory and royal power.

Peace, then colonisation

For much of its history the Kuba kingdom avoided the worst of the slave trade by its inland position, and grew prosperous on farming and regional trade.

In the late 1800s it came under the rule of King Leopold II's Congo Free State and then Belgium. The Kuba king, the Nyim, still reigns as a traditional ruler, and Kuba art continues to inspire artists today.

Did You Know?

Kuba kings kept their history in the carved ndop statues — each king's statue showed his own personal emblem, like a drum or a game board, so people could 'read' who he was.
