Maurya Empire

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Maurya Empire was the first great empire of India. Founded by Chandragupta Maurya about 321 BCE, it united almost the whole subcontinent for the first time — from Afghanistan to Bengal — under one rule.

Its most famous emperor, Ashoka, began as a conqueror and became a messenger of peace, carving messages of kindness and tolerance on pillars across his empire. His lions and his wheel still stand at the heart of modern India's symbols.

Chandragupta builds an empire

After Alexander the Great's armies reached India's edge and withdrew, a young man named Chandragupta Maurya seized the throne of the Nanda kingdom in about 321 BCE. Guided, tradition says, by the brilliant strategist Chanakya — author of the Arthashastra, a famous manual of statecraft — Chandragupta conquered across northern India.

His empire stretched from Afghanistan to Bengal. When Alexander's successor Seleucus tried to reclaim the eastern lands, Chandragupta defeated him and sealed the peace with a treaty — and a gift of 500 war elephants that Seleucus used in his own wars. A Greek ambassador, Megasthenes, described the splendid capital, Pataliputra, in his book Indica.

Ashoka and the war that changed him

Chandragupta's grandson Ashoka became emperor about 268 BCE. Early in his reign he conquered Kalinga on India's east coast — a war so bloody, with perhaps 100,000 killed, that Ashoka was filled with remorse. He turned to Buddhism and transformed his rule.

Across his empire Ashoka carved his messages on rocks and polished stone pillars — the first readable inscriptions of ancient India. In them he speaks directly to his people: be kind to parents, servants, animals and all religions; he planted trees along roads, dug wells, built rest houses and hospitals, and sent missions — tradition says including his own son and daughter — to Sri Lanka and beyond.

Governing a giant

The Mauryan Empire was one of the largest states of the ancient world, ruling perhaps 30–40 million people. From Pataliputra, a vast administration of officials, spies and tax collectors governed provinces ruled by royal princes, while a huge army — Greek writers claimed hundreds of thousands of soldiers, elephants and chariots — guarded it.

The state managed forests, mines, irrigation and trade, and standardised weights and coinage. Roads linked the empire, including the great northern highway that later ages rebuilt again and again.

Decline — and an undying symbol

After Ashoka's death in 232 BCE, the empire slowly shrank under weaker kings, and in 185 BCE the last Maurya was assassinated by his own general, ending the dynasty. The empire broke into regional kingdoms.

But Ashoka himself was never entirely forgotten — and in the 1800s, when scholars deciphered his inscriptions, India rediscovered its emperor of peace. When India became independent in 1947, it chose Ashoka's lion capital as its national emblem and his wheel, the Ashoka Chakra, for its flag.

Did You Know?

India's national emblem — the lions of Ashoka — and the wheel on India's flag both come from pillars carved for the emperor Ashoka more than 2,200 years ago.
