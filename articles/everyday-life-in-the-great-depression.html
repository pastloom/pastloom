Everyday Life in the Great Depression

Our main article on the Great Depression explains the crash of 1929 and how the crisis spread around the world. This companion stays closer to home — with the ordinary families who lived through the hungry 1930s.

It is a story of breadlines and shantytowns, of dresses sewn from flour sacks and dinners stretched from almost nothing — and also of radio laughter, cinema dreams and neighbours helping neighbours survive.

Losing work, losing home

When factories closed and banks failed, wages stopped — but rent did not. Families doubled up with relatives, sold furniture piece by piece, and burned whatever they could to keep warm.

Those who lost everything built shacks from scrap wood, tin and cardboard on empty land at the edges of cities. These Hoovervilles had their own mayors and rules. In the cities, charities and churches ran breadlines and soup kitchens, and men sold apples on street corners for a few cents each.

Making do with almost nothing

Depression families became masters of thrift. Mothers turned printed flour sacks into dresses and shirts — flour companies kindly began printing flowers on the sacks. Shoes were patched with cardboard, meals stretched with beans and potatoes, and nothing, not even a bent nail, was thrown away.

Barter replaced money in many places: a dentist might fix a tooth for a basket of eggs. Gardens sprang up in every spare patch of ground, and older children left school to earn whatever they could.

Small joys and holding on

For a few cents, the cinema offered two hours of warmth, music and glamour, and millions went weekly. At home, the radio was free once you had one: families gathered for comedies, boxing matches and President Roosevelt's calm fireside chats, which made hard times feel shared.

Board games boomed too — Monopoly, released in the middle of the Depression, let penniless players pretend to be rich landlords for an evening. It was escape, but it was also togetherness, and for many families that was what carried them through.

Did You Know?

Flour and feed sacks became so popular for home sewing that the companies competed with prettier prints — some even printed doll patterns and washing instructions that faded out, so the cloth could become a dress.
