The Nabataeans

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Nabataeans were an Arab people who grew rich controlling the desert trade routes of what is now Jordan. Their capital was Petra — the rose-red city carved into rock cliffs.

They were master water engineers: in a land of almost no rain, they collected every drop, grew gardens in the desert, and kept their caravan cities secret and safe.

Masters of the incense road

Frankincense and myrrh — perfumes worth more than gold in temples and funerals — came from southern Arabia. Nabataean caravans carried them north through the desert to ports and cities of the Mediterranean.

Nabataean guides knew the hidden water holes. Rivals could not follow, and the tolls and trade made the Nabataeans quietly, enormously rich.

Water from stone

Rain in their desert falls in rare, violent floods. The Nabataeans dammed the floods, carved channels and cisterns into the rock, and sealed them so enemies could not find the water.

This hidden water system let Petra support tens of thousands of people, with pools, baths and gardens in a canyon where today visitors carry water bottles.

Petra, and joining the Roman world

Visitors entered Petra through the Siq, a narrow slot in the cliffs — then the Treasury facade burst into view, carved from pink rock nearly 40 metres high.

Beyond lay a royal city of tombs, temples, a theatre and colonnaded streets. In the 1980s Petra became a World Heritage Site, and its Treasury is now one of the most recognised buildings on Earth.

In 106 CE the emperor Trajan annexed the Nabataean kingdom as Rome's province of Arabia. There was little fighting — the Nabataeans became Roman citizens and soldiers.

As sea routes took over the incense trade, Petra slowly declined, and earthquakes hastened its end. The city was forgotten by the outside world until travellers publicised it again in the 1800s.

Did You Know?

The Nabataeans' Aramaic writing gradually changed over the centuries into the script that became Arabic writing — so their merchants helped shape how Arabic is written today.
