Oak Island and Its Mystery

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

On a small island off the coast of Nova Scotia, Canada, there is a pit that people have been digging for more than 225 years. It began in 1795, when a teenager noticed a strange hollow in the ground beneath an old oak tree — and stories of buried pirate treasure did the rest.

Six men have died hunting for whatever lies at the bottom of the 'Money Pit'. Nothing proven has ever been brought up — yet the digging goes on.

The Money Pit

In 1795, sixteen-year-old Daniel McGinnis found a curious sunken patch of ground on Oak Island, beneath an oak tree with a ship's tackle block hanging over it. With two friends, he dug — and struck wooden platforms of oak logs at regular depths, like floors in a shaft.

In 1803 a company took over and dug deeper, finding layers of charcoal, putty and coconut fibre — a strange material in Canada. At about 30 metres their tools struck something hard. The next morning the pit had filled with seawater to exactly the level of the sea. No pumping could empty it.

Two centuries of diggers

Company after company tried. Drilling in 1849 was said to pass through two chests of loose metal and bring up three links of a gold chain — the pit's most famous 'evidence', never seen again. Engineers decided the pit was protected by man-made flood tunnels from the shore, designed to drown any digger.

The hunt has been dangerous as well as expensive: a boiler explosion in 1861, a fall in 1897, and in 1965 four men overcome by fumes in a shaft. Six deaths gave rise to a grim saying that seven must die before the island gives up its treasure.

What might be down there?

Theories have multiplied for two centuries: Captain Kidd's pirate gold, a French naval pay ship hidden during wartime, Marie Antoinette's jewels smuggled from revolutionary France, even Shakespeare's missing manuscripts. Sceptics answer that the 'pit' may be a natural sinkhole, and the platforms and stone tales added later.

Modern searchers, led by the Lagina brothers and filmed since 2014, have found old coins, a lead cross and timbers in the island's swamp — intriguing, but still no treasure. Oak Island remains what it has been since 1795: a hole in the ground, full of water and hope.

Did You Know?

US President Franklin D. Roosevelt invested in an Oak Island treasure company as a young man — and followed the hunt with interest for the rest of his life, even from the White House.
