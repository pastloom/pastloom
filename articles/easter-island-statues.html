Easter Island and Its Statues

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Easter Island — called Rapa Nui by its people — is one of the most remote inhabited islands on Earth, far out in the Pacific Ocean. It is famous for nearly 900 giant stone statues called moai.

The statues, with their huge heads and long faces, were carved by Polynesian islanders between about 1250 and 1500. How they moved such heavy stones across the island has puzzled people for centuries.

The island of the moai

Polynesian sailors reached Rapa Nui by about 1200, crossing thousands of kilometres of open ocean in canoes. Their descendants carved the moai to honour important ancestors.

The statues are not just heads. Many have full bodies, which over the centuries were buried by soil, so only the heads showed above ground. Most moai once stood on stone platforms called ahu, facing inland to watch over villages, and many had eyes of white coral and red stone hats, called pukao.

How were they moved?

The heaviest moai weighs more than 80 tonnes, yet the islanders had no wheels, animals or metal. One strong idea is that teams rocked the statues from side to side with ropes, walking them upright across the island — island stories themselves say the statues walked.

Many statues still lie unfinished in the quarry, as if work suddenly stopped. Why carving ended, and why statues were later toppled, is still debated.

A hard lesson from the island

When Europeans arrived in 1722, the island's great palm forests were gone. Trees had been cut for canoes, farms and moving statues, and without trees the soil washed away and life grew harder.

Today the moai are protected, several have been raised again onto their platforms, and the Rapa Nui people care for the statues of their ancestors as a living part of their culture.

Did You Know?

The famous Easter Island heads are not heads alone — digs have uncovered full bodies buried underground, some covered in carved patterns.
