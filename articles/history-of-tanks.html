History of Tanks

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The tank is an armoured fighting vehicle that carries its guns across rough ground on metal tracks. It was invented to break the deadly deadlock of the First World War.

In little more than a hundred years, tanks grew from slow, clanking boxes into fast, computer-guided machines — and they changed how wars are fought on land.

Born in the mud of the trenches

By 1915, the First World War's trenches, barbed wire and machine guns had stopped armies in their tracks. Britain built a secret weapon: an armoured vehicle on caterpillar tracks that could crush wire and cross trenches.

The first tanks were slow, hot and unreliable — many broke down before reaching the enemy. But at Cambrai in 1917, a mass tank attack punched through German lines and showed what the new weapon could do.

Tanks take over the battlefield

Between the wars, thinkers like Britain's J.F.C. Fuller and Germany's Heinz Guderian argued that tanks should strike fast and deep, with planes and infantry supporting them.

In the Second World War this became blitzkrieg — lightning war. Great tank battles, like Kursk in 1943, the largest tank battle in history, decided the fate of nations.

The modern tank

Cold War tanks gained powerful guns, thick sloped armour and engines that could cross deserts and snow. Crews shrank to three or four with automatic loading in some designs.

Today's main battle tanks aim with lasers and computers and can fight at night. Missiles and drones now challenge them — but a century after the Somme, the tank remains the heavy fist of land armies.

Did You Know?

The first tanks came in 'male' and 'female' versions: males carried small cannons, while females carried only machine guns.
