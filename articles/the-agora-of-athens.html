The Agora of Athens

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Agora was the open heart of ancient Athens — a large square below the Acropolis where citizens shopped, talked, voted, watched processions and heard the latest news.

If you want to understand how Athenian democracy worked day by day, the Agora is the place to look. Socrates spent his life there, asking questions in its shaded walkways.

Marketplace and meeting place

From dawn, the Agora filled with stalls selling fish, olives, oil, wine, pottery, shoes and slaves, while farmers brought produce from the Attic countryside. Money-changers sat at tables, and porters and craftsmen looked for work.

But the Agora was never only a market. It was where citizens met their friends, heard speeches, gossiped about generals and politicians, and formed the opinions they would vote on in the Assembly.

The buildings of democracy

Around the square stood the buildings that ran the world's first democracy: the council house where the Council of 500 prepared business, the round Tholos where officials on duty ate and slept, and law courts where large juries of citizens judged cases.

Covered walkways called stoas gave shelter from sun and rain. In the Stoa Poikile — the Painted Stoa — the philosopher Zeno later taught, which is why his followers are called Stoics.

Ostracism and Socrates

Once a year, citizens could vote in the Agora to exile one man for ten years, scratching his name on a piece of broken pottery called an ostrakon. Thousands of these pottery votes have been found in the Agora's wells and drains.

It was also here that Socrates walked and talked for decades, questioning generals, poets and politicians — and here, in a sense, that his story ended, for the charges against him grew out of a lifetime of public argument in the square.

Did You Know?

Archaeologists have found heaps of ostraka in the Agora with the name of Themistocles, the hero of Salamis, scratched on them — many written by the same few hands, suggesting his enemies prepared piles of votes in advance to hand out.
