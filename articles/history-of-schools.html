History of Schools

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Schools are where children learn together with a teacher. They began thousands of years ago, when the first cities needed people who could read, write and count.

For most of history, only a few children — usually rich boys — went to school. Free schooling for every child is a surprisingly new idea, only about 150 years old.

The first classrooms

In ancient Mesopotamia, boys training to be scribes sat in schools called tablet houses, writing wedge-shaped signs on wet clay. Mistakes could earn a beating — school complaints are as old as school itself.

Egypt, India, China and Greece all built their own traditions. In Athens, Plato founded his Academy in about 387 BCE, and in India the great centre of Nalanda drew students from across Asia.

Schools of faith and the first universities

For centuries, most schools were run by temples, mosques and churches. Madrasas taught the Quran, law and science across the Islamic world, while monastery and cathedral schools kept learning alive in Europe.

In 1088, the University of Bologna opened in Italy — often called the oldest university still teaching today. Oxford, Cambridge, Paris and others soon followed, and students travelled across continents to study, much as they do now.

School for every child

Until the 1800s, most children worked in fields and workshops and never learned to read. Reformers argued that every child deserved an education.

Country by country, free and compulsory schooling became law, and girls at last entered classrooms in large numbers. Today, the school day — lessons, bells, homework and exams — is a shared experience for children almost everywhere on Earth.

Did You Know?

The word school comes from a Greek word, schole, which meant free time or leisure — time free for learning and discussion.
