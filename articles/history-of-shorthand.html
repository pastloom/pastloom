History of Shorthand

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Shorthand is writing at the speed of speech. Instead of full letters, it uses short strokes and symbols for sounds, so a trained writer can keep up with a fast talker.

Roman senators, Victorian journalists and generations of secretaries all depended on it. Shorthand shaped parliaments, newspapers and courts long before recording machines existed.

Rome invents fast writing

Marcus Tullius Tiro, secretary to the great speaker Cicero, built a system of shortened signs around 63 BCE so he could capture speeches word for word. Some Tironian signs survived in handwriting for over a thousand years.

Through the Middle Ages, monks and clerks used various note systems to record sermons and debates, but shorthand's great age was still to come.

The shorthand century

In the 1800s, parliaments, law courts and newspapers all needed exact, fast records. Isaac Pitman's phonetic shorthand of 1837 spread through the English-speaking world, and John Robert Gregg's smooth, cursive system of 1888 conquered American offices.

Shorthand became a key skill — especially for women entering office work, whose notebooks filled the filing cabinets of the business world. Speed contests saw champions exceed 280 words a minute.

Machines take the note

Dictation machines, tape recorders and computers shrank shorthand's empire in the late 1900s, and most schools stopped teaching it.

But where an official record must be perfect — above all in courtrooms — fast writers still rule, now often on stenotype machines that type whole syllables at a stroke.

Did You Know?

Charles Dickens learned shorthand as a young reporter and called the squiggles 'a savage stenographic mystery' — the skill launched his career, taking him into Parliament's press gallery before he became a novelist.
