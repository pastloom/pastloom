Hiroshima and Nagasaki

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In August 1945, near the end of the Second World War, the United States dropped two atomic bombs on Japan — the only nuclear weapons ever used in war. The first destroyed the city of Hiroshima on 6 August; the second struck Nagasaki on 9 August.

Tens of thousands of people died instantly, and many more died later from burns and radiation. Within days, Japan announced its surrender, and the Second World War ended. The bombings still raise deep moral questions that people debate today.

The road to August 1945

By the summer of 1945, Germany had surrendered but Japan fought on, even as its cities were being destroyed by ordinary bombing. American leaders feared that invading Japan would cost enormous numbers of lives on both sides.

In July 1945, scientists of the secret Manhattan Project successfully tested the first atomic bomb in the New Mexico desert. President Harry Truman decided to use the new weapon against Japan, hoping to force a quick surrender.

Two mornings in August

On 6 August, the B-29 Enola Gay dropped Little Boy over Hiroshima. The bomb exploded above the city with a blinding flash, creating a fireball and shock wave that flattened almost everything within two kilometres. Fires consumed much of what remained.

Three days later, a second bomb, Fat Man, was dropped on Nagasaki — the day's secondary target, after clouds covered the first choice, Kokura. Nagasaki's hills limited the damage somewhat, but about a third of the city was destroyed. The survivors, called hibakusha, carried burns, sickness and memories for the rest of their lives.

Surrender and shadow

On 15 August, Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's surrender in a radio broadcast — the first time most Japanese people had ever heard his voice. The formal surrender followed on 2 September, ending the Second World War.

The bombings opened the nuclear age. Ever since, the world has lived with weapons able to destroy whole cities, and Hiroshima and Nagasaki stand as warnings. Each August, paper lanterns float on Hiroshima's river in memory of the dead, and the city's Peace Memorial — the skeleton-domed Atomic Bomb Dome — is preserved as it was left in 1945.

Did You Know?

Tsutomu Yamaguchi survived the Hiroshima bombing, travelled home to Nagasaki — and survived the second bomb too. He lived to the age of 93 and spent his later life campaigning against nuclear weapons.
