The United Nations: How It Began

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The United Nations did not appear in one day. It was built step by step during World War II, by leaders who wanted a stronger peace organisation than the League of Nations, which had failed to stop the war.

The name was first used in 1942, the plan was drawn up while the war still raged, and the organisation officially began on 24 October 1945 — weeks after the war ended.

A name born in wartime

In August 1941, American president Franklin D. Roosevelt and British leader Winston Churchill issued the Atlantic Charter, promising a fairer, safer world after the war. On 1 January 1942, twenty-six nations fighting Germany and its allies signed a declaration calling themselves the United Nations.

In 1944, the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union and China met at Dumbarton Oaks in Washington and drafted the plan for a permanent organisation — stronger than the League, with a Security Council that could act.

Fifty nations in San Francisco

In April 1945, as the war in Europe ended, fifty countries gathered in San Francisco to write the final Charter. Roosevelt had died just before the conference, but the work went on in his memory.

Smaller countries argued hard for a General Assembly where every nation has a voice. The big powers kept a veto in the Security Council. On 26 June 1945, the Charter was signed.

The first days

Countries then approved the Charter one by one. When enough had done so, the United Nations officially began on 24 October 1945 — a date still celebrated as United Nations Day. Poland, which had not been at San Francisco, signed later and still counted as the fifty-first original member.

The first General Assembly met in London in January 1946. The young organisation later chose New York as its permanent home — and grew from 51 members to the 193 of today.

Did You Know?

A blank space was left in the San Francisco signature list for Poland, whose government was not settled in time — Poland signed later and is still counted among the 51 founding members.
