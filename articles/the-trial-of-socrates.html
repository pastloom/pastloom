The Trial of Socrates

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

In 399 BCE, the city of Athens put its most famous thinker, Socrates, on trial for his life. He was accused of not believing in the city's gods and of corrupting the young men who listened to him.

Socrates defended himself with pride, refused to beg for mercy, and — when the jury condemned him — calmly drank the poison hemlock. His death became the most famous trial story of the ancient world.

Why Athens turned on Socrates

Socrates had spent decades in the Agora questioning everyone — politicians, poets, generals — and showing that they did not know what they claimed to know. Many powerful men hated the embarrassment, and comic poets mocked him on stage.

Athens had also just suffered defeat by Sparta and a brief, brutal rule by thirty tyrants, some of whom had once been Socrates' students. In the frightened democracy that followed, old resentments became a formal charge.

The trial and the defence

Our main account is the Apology, written by Socrates' student Plato. In it, Socrates refuses to flatter the jury. He says he is like a gadfly stinging a lazy horse — annoying, but necessary — and that he will not stop asking questions even to save his life.

The jury found him guilty by a fairly close vote. Offered the chance to propose his own punishment, Socrates suggested he deserved free meals for life. The jury, insulted, voted for death by a larger majority than had convicted him.

The death of Socrates

Friends planned an escape, but Socrates refused: he had lived under Athens' laws all his life, he argued, and would not break them now that they condemned him. Plato's Phaedo describes his last hours — calm, joking gently, discussing the soul.

He drank the cup of hemlock and died as the poison slowly numbed his body. Athens soon regretted the trial, and Socrates became a symbol, honoured ever since, of free thought standing firm against the crowd.

Did You Know?

Plato, who tells us most of what we know about the trial, was about 28 when Socrates died — the execution of his teacher is one reason he turned to philosophy and founded his Academy.
