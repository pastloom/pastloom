The Cuban Missile Crisis

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Cuban Missile Crisis was a terrifying stand-off in October 1962, when the United States discovered that the Soviet Union was placing nuclear missiles in Cuba, just off the American coast.

For thirteen days, the world stood closer to nuclear war than ever before or since. In the end, both leaders stepped back — but only just.

Missiles in Cuba

Cuba's new communist government, led by Fidel Castro, had turned to the Soviet Union for protection after an American-backed invasion failed in 1961. Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev secretly sent nuclear missiles to the island.

On 14 October 1962, an American spy plane photographed the missile sites being built. The missiles could strike American cities within minutes. President John F. Kennedy was told the news in secret.

Thirteen days

Kennedy's advisers argued over air strikes and invasion. Instead, Kennedy ordered a naval quarantine — warships would stop Soviet ships carrying more missiles to Cuba — and demanded the missiles be removed.

Soviet ships steamed toward the blockade line, then turned back. Behind the scenes, tense letters and secret talks searched for a way out that let both leaders save face.

The deal that saved the world

On 28 October, Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles. In return, the United States publicly promised not to invade Cuba — and quietly agreed to remove its own missiles from Turkey.

Both sides had looked into the abyss. Afterwards, a direct hotline was set up between Washington and Moscow, and the two powers began their first real talks about limiting nuclear weapons.

Did You Know?

During the crisis, a Soviet submarine officer named Vasily Arkhipov refused to agree to fire a nuclear torpedo when his captain believed war had begun — a single decision that may have prevented nuclear war.
