The Black Death

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

The Black Death was the deadliest pandemic of the medieval world. Between 1347 and 1353, bubonic plague — carried by fleas on rats along the trade routes of Asia and Europe — killed an estimated third to a half of Europe's population, and millions more across Asia and North Africa.

Cities emptied, wages and power shifted, faith was shaken, and Europe was never the same again. The plague kept returning for centuries, and its shadow lies across everything from medicine to the Renaissance.

A plague travels the Silk Road

The Black Death began in Central Asia, where the plague bacterium lived among wild rodents. In the 1330s–40s it spread along trade routes — with Mongol armies, caravans and grain ships — reaching the Black Sea port of Kaffa in 1346. A famous story says the besieging Mongols catapulted plague corpses into the city; fleeing Genoese ships then carried infected rats and fleas to Sicily in October 1347.

From Italy the plague rolled across Europe like a slow fire: France and Spain in 1348, England in 1348–49, Germany, Scandinavia and Russia by 1353. It had also ravaged the Middle East and North Africa — this was a pandemic of the whole connected world.

The dying

Bubonic plague struck with fever, chills and agonising swellings — buboes — in the neck, armpits and groin; a pneumonic form attacked the lungs and spread by coughing. Medicine was helpless: doctors blamed bad air and the stars, prescribed bleeding and prayer, and often fled. Whole villages were abandoned; chroniclers wrote that the living could barely bury the dead.

Estimates vary, but Europe likely lost between a third and a half of its people — perhaps 25 million or more — in five years. Some cities, like Florence, may have lost half or more. It remains proportionally the deadliest pandemic in recorded history.

A world turned upside down

Society convulsed. Labourers who survived suddenly became precious: wages rose, rents fell, and lords across Europe tried — and often failed — to freeze wages by law. The Church, unable to explain or stop the dying, lost authority; some people turned to frenzied piety, like the flagellants whipping themselves through towns, while others plunged into pleasure, as in Boccaccio's Decameron, a book framed by young people fleeing plague-stricken Florence.

Fear also found scapegoats: Jewish communities were falsely accused of poisoning wells and massacred in many cities — a shameful chapter the Pope himself condemned in vain. Trade collapsed, universities closed, and wars paused for want of soldiers.

The long shadow

The plague never fully left: it returned in waves for nearly 400 years, killing a quarter of London in 1665 and devastating Marseille in 1720, before fading from Europe — why, is still debated. The bacterium itself was identified only in 1894, in Hong Kong, and plague still occurs rarely today, curable with antibiotics.

Yet the Black Death also, paradoxically, cleared ground for change: higher wages, cheaper land, weakened feudal bonds, and a questioning spirit that some historians link to the Renaissance. It is history's starkest lesson in how a microscopic organism can bend the fate of civilisations.

Did You Know?

The word 'quarantine' comes from this plague: ships arriving in Venice had to wait quaranta giorni — forty days — before landing, in case they carried the disease.
