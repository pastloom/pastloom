History of Fairy Tales

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Fairy tales — stories of magic, wishes, witches and talking animals — are far older than children's books. Some tales, like Cinderella, have been told for more than a thousand years, in countries from China to Egypt to France.

They were first stories for adults around the fire. Only in the last 300 years did writers collect them, soften them and give them to children.

Tales of the fire and the road

Folk tales travelled with travellers. A story told in Persia could reach Spain, change its clothes and names, and still keep its heart — the kind child, the cruel stepmother, the magical helper.

Egyptian tales of shipwrecked sailors and magical wives are over 3,000 years old. India's Panchatantra taught wisdom through animal fables, and its stories flowed west into Aesop-like collections and the Arabian Nights.

Collectors give tales to children

In 1697 Charles Perrault published elegant French versions of Cinderella, Sleeping Beauty and Little Red Riding Hood for the court of Louis XIV — with morals politely attached.

The Brothers Grimm collected German tales to save their language's heritage, and across seven editions they steadily removed adult details and added Christian morals, turning a scholars' book into a children's classic. Andersen then showed that new fairy tales could be invented by a single author.

Why fairy tales survive

Victorian editors softened the tales further, and in the 1900s Walt Disney's films — beginning with Snow White in 1937 — fixed their images for the whole world: the glass slipper, the pumpkin coach, the seven dwarfs.

Yet the old tales keep being retold because they carry real fears and hopes in magic dress: being lost, being poor, being brave, being kind. Every age recognises itself in them — which is why they never grow old.

Did You Know?

The Brothers Grimm kept making their tales gentler: in the first edition of 1812, Rapunzel's story hinted she was pregnant — later editions quietly removed it.
