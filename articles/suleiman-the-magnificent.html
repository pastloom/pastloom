Suleiman the Magnificent

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Suleiman the Magnificent was the greatest sultan of the Ottoman Empire. He ruled for forty-six years — longer than any other sultan — and under him the empire reached its largest size and greatest wealth.

Europeans called him 'the Magnificent' for his riches and power. His own people called him 'the Lawgiver', because he organised the laws of the empire into a fair and lasting system.

The sultan of a great empire

Suleiman came to the throne in 1520, aged twenty-six, ruling an empire that already stretched across Turkey and the Middle East. He quickly showed he would expand it in every direction.

His armies took Belgrade, the island of Rhodes and most of Hungary, and in 1529 he besieged Vienna itself, deep in Europe, before winter forced him back. Ottoman fleets ruled the Mediterranean and sailed as far as India.

The Lawgiver at home

Suleiman reformed taxes, punished corrupt officials and gathered the empire's laws into one ordered code, applied in courts from the Balkans to Egypt. That is why Turks remember him as Kanuni, the Lawgiver.

In Istanbul his architect Sinan built the magnificent Suleymaniye Mosque, still one of the city's glories. Suleiman himself wrote poetry under the pen name Muhibbi, 'the Lover' — many poems were for his wife Hurrem Sultan, the most powerful woman of the Ottoman age.

The long twilight

Suleiman led his armies in person into old age. In 1566, aged seventy-one, he set out for Hungary one last time and died in his tent during the siege of Szigetvar — the night before his army won the fortress.

His empire remained mighty for another century, but no later sultan matched his combination of conqueror, lawgiver and builder.

Did You Know?

Suleiman wrote love poems to his wife Hurrem under the pen name Muhibbi. In one he calls her 'my Istanbul, my Karaman, the earth of my Anatolia' — his whole empire, in one line of poetry.
