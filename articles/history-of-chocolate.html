History of Chocolate

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Chocolate comes from the beans of the cacao tree, which grows in the hot forests of Central and South America. The Maya and Aztec made a bitter, spicy drink from the beans, and valued them so highly that beans were used as money.

Spanish ships carried cacao to Europe, where sugar turned the bitter drink sweet. In the 1800s, machines turned chocolate into bars — and it became a treat for the whole world.

The drink of the Maya and Aztec

The Maya grew cacao and drank chocolate frothy and bitter, mixed with chilli and maize, frothed by pouring it from a height. For the Aztec, it was a drink of kings and warriors, and cacao beans paid for goods in the markets.

The Aztec emperor Montezuma was said to drink chocolate from golden cups. When Spanish conquerors arrived, they found warehouses full of precious cacao beans.

Chocolate in Europe

In Spain, chocolate stayed a drink of the royal court for decades, sweetened with sugar and cinnamon. It spread to France and Italy, and chocolate houses opened in London in the 1650s.

For two hundred years chocolate remained a drink for the rich, because cacao and sugar were costly and every cup had to be made by hand.

Bars, boxes and the modern age

In 1828, a Dutch inventor's press squeezed the fat from cacao, making cocoa powder. In 1847, the firm of Joseph Fry in Bristol sold the first solid chocolate bar, and in 1875 Daniel Peter added milk to make milk chocolate.

Machines, railways and advertising made chocolate cheap and famous. Today most cacao grows in West Africa, and chocolate is eaten everywhere — but it began as a bitter drink in the forests of the Americas.

Did You Know?

Cacao beans were once so valuable that in Aztec markets one turkey cost about 100 beans — and people even made fake beans from clay to cheat buyers.
