History of Quarantine

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Quarantine means keeping people who may carry a disease apart from everyone else until we know they are safe. The word comes from the Italian for 'forty days' — the time ships once had to wait outside port during plague years.

The first quarantine law was passed in 1377, and the same idea — older than microscopes or vaccines — was still protecting the world during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Forty days at the harbour

When the Black Death swept Europe, port cities lived in terror of plague arriving by ship. In 1377 the city of Ragusa ordered travellers from infected places to wait a month on islands before entering — the first known quarantine law.

Venice and other ports extended the wait to forty days — quaranta giorni — which gave us the word quarantine. Why forty? Possibly from old medical ideas, religious tradition, or simply hard experience of how long plague took to appear.

Plague islands and health passes

Venice built a lazaretto — a quarantine hospital — on an island in 1423, and the system spread across the Mediterranean. Ships flew flags showing they were waiting, goods were aired and fumigated, and travellers carried health passes proving where they had been.

Quarantine was imperfect and often unfair — the poor suffered most in crowded stations — but in a world without any knowledge of germs, it was one of the few defences that sometimes worked.

Quarantine in modern times

As germ theory, vaccines and antibiotics advanced, long quarantines became rare, replaced by targeted isolation of the sick and short waits for the exposed. Old lazarettos closed or became museums.

Then, in 2020, COVID-19 brought the ancient tool back at global scale: border quarantines, hotel isolation and stay-at-home orders. A 600-year-old idea from plague ports was suddenly part of everyone's life again.

Did You Know?

The yellow flag flown by ships today still carries its old meaning — the letter Q in signal flags stands for quarantine, a request for a clean bill of health.
