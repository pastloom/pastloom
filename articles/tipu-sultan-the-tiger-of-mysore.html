Tipu Sultan, the Tiger of Mysore

This article is written in English. Choose a language to open an automatic translation by Google Translate. Automatic translations can sometimes get historical names and dates wrong — for full accuracy, read the original English article.

Tipu Sultan, ruler of Mysore in South India, was called the Tiger of Mysore — and he took the name seriously. Tigers decorated his throne, his weapons, his flag and even his soldiers' uniforms.

He is remembered as a fierce enemy of the British. But he was also an inventor-king: a pioneer of rocket warfare, a builder of roads and trade, and one of the most inventive rulers of his century. He died in 1799, sword in hand, defending his capital.

The tiger king

Tipu took the throne in 1782, in the middle of a war with the British East India Company begun by his father, Haidar Ali. He made the tiger his personal emblem: his throne stood on a tiger, his cannon were cast with tiger heads, and his favourite toy was a life-size mechanical tiger that growled and moved as it attacked a screaming European figure.

To his enemies he was a terrifying foe; to his admirers, a king who refused to bow to a foreign company while other rulers made peace with it.

An inventor on the throne

Tipu's Mysore was a workshop of new ideas. His iron rockets — the best war rockets of the age — were organised into regular army units. He built roads, dams and state trading houses, sent embassies to France and the Ottoman Empire, and tried to bring silk-making and new crafts to his kingdom.

He introduced a new calendar and new coinage, corresponded with Napoleon, and planted a Liberty Tree as a member of a French revolutionary club — a South Indian sultan dreaming of world alliances against Britain.

The last stand at Srirangapatna

In 1799 the British, allied with Hyderabad and the Marathas, stormed Tipu's island capital of Srirangapatna. Tipu refused offers to surrender and was killed fighting at the breach, his body found under a pile of the dead, still holding his sword.

His sons were taken away, his treasures divided, and his mechanical tiger shipped to London. Two centuries later, Tipu remains one of the most debated figures in Indian history — tyrant to some, freedom-fighter to others, and to all, unforgettable.

Did You Know?

Tipu's mechanical tiger still works: turn its handle in the Victoria and Albert Museum and the tiger growls while the soldier waves his arm and cries out.
